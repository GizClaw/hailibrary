# `en-US` native editorial guide

Judge the text as contemporary American English written for the declared proficiency level. Controlled language must still sound like natural narration and dialogue, not a grammar exercise or a literal rendering from another language.

## Sentence and discourse

- Check articles, determiners, countability, tense and aspect, prepositions, phrasal verbs, pronoun reference, modifier placement, and verb-object collocations in context.
- Prefer the information order and sentence focus a native English narrator would use. Reject topic-comment carryover, omitted articles, overused generic nouns, or connective patterns imported from another locale.
- Read for cadence across pages. Repetition should teach or create an effect; it should not merely preserve another locale's sentence shape.
- At higher levels, vary sentence structure and paragraph movement without inflating the prose with abstract nominalizations or summary statements.

## Narration, dialogue, and titles

- Dialogue should be speakable for the character and situation. Use contractions, ellipsis, idiomatic politeness, and emotional emphasis where natural, while keeping them compatible with the level.
- Do not let all speakers share the narrator's vocabulary or explain facts they would already know.
- Check invented and localized names for readability, unwanted associations, and consistency with the work's setting.
- Titles and chapter titles must work as English titles, not as imported modifier chains, unexplained puns, or literal reproductions of another language's imagery.

## Level-specific judgment

- At A-C, use concrete high-frequency English with natural articles, pronouns, and repeated frames. Do not create fragments or stiff subject-verb-object drills merely to stay short.
- At D-G, allow natural contractions, phrasal verbs, varied dialogue turns, and controlled figurative language.
- At H-K, preserve narrative voice and scene texture while expressing complex ideas. Do not replace prose with policy language, thesis statements, or artificial Socratic dialogue.

For uncertain collocations or senses, use current monolingual American English dictionaries, usage references, and reputable corpora. Corpus evidence must match the intended register and meaning.

## Live reference route

Keep only source links and their purposes in the repository. Open current pages during review and record the exact URLs consulted; do not copy dictionary, corpus, or published-prose content into the project.

### Meaning, usage, and learner-facing examples

- [Oxford Advanced American Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/american_english/): check American senses, pronunciation, usage labels, phrases, and learner-appropriate examples.
- [Merriam-Webster Dictionary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/): cross-check contemporary American senses, status labels, usage notes, and conventional examples. Distinguish editor-written examples from automatically collected web examples.
- [Cambridge Dictionary, US](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/): cross-check meanings, grammar patterns, phrases, and corpus-backed examples; confirm whether an entry or example reflects American, British, or general English.

### Corpus context

- [Corpus of Contemporary American English](https://www.english-corpora.org/coca/): compare collocates and complete contexts across spoken, fiction, magazine, news, academic, television, and other registers. Use fiction for narration and spoken or television contexts for dialogue only when the character and situation are comparable.

A dictionary entry proves neither story cadence nor character voice. A corpus hit proves neither editorial quality nor suitability for the declared level. Search the exact wording and natural alternatives, inspect complete context, and avoid conclusions based only on frequency.

### Publishing and clarity conventions

- [The Chicago Manual of Style Online](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/home/): check American book-publishing conventions for punctuation, capitalization, dialogue, quotations, numbers, and editorial consistency. Some detailed content requires access; use the public Q&A or another authoritative source rather than guessing at a paywalled rule.
- [Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://ies.ed.gov/rel-central/2025/01/federal-plain-language-guidelines): check clarity, information order, concrete wording, and reader-centered nonfiction. Plain-language guidance is not a fiction style template and must not flatten character voice or literary rhythm.

### Native children's prose and informational writing

- [Highlights Kids](https://www.highlightskids.com/): reference contemporary American child-facing voice, short-form rhythm, humor, instructions, and age-appropriate direct address for early and developing readers.
- [TIME for Kids](https://www.timeforkids.com/): reference school-facing American news and informational prose. Its editions are organized for K-1, grade 2, grades 3-4, and grades 5-6; choose a comparable edition instead of treating the site as one difficulty level.
- [National Geographic Kids](https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/article/): reference child-facing science, nature, geography, and explanatory nonfiction. Use it for genre and information-flow comparison, not as proof of a story's factual claim.
- [CommonLit Text Library](https://www.commonlit.org/text-library): compare fiction, nonfiction, poetry, plays, and other classroom texts using its grade and genre filters. Check the original author and publication context; translated, historical, or intentionally stylized texts are not neutral evidence for contemporary American usage.

These pages calibrate discourse, audience, dialogue, and genre. Do not copy sentences, summarize proprietary texts into the work, or imitate a named author's recognizable style. Record only the URL, the language feature examined, and the reviewer's independent conclusion.

## English evidence order

1. Identify whether the doubt concerns sense, grammar pattern, collocation, register, dialogue, or discourse rhythm.
2. Check the intended sense and usage labels in an American monolingual dictionary.
3. Compare the wording and alternatives in COCA using the closest available register.
4. Check Chicago or federal plain-language guidance only when the issue is an applicable editorial or nonfiction convention.
5. Compare discourse rhythm, dialogue, and audience fit against a genre- and level-matched child-facing source such as Highlights, TIME for Kids, National Geographic Kids, or CommonLit.
6. Use published native prose only as genre evidence, never as wording to copy or a style to imitate.
7. Require agreement across at least two evidence layers for a confident researched verdict; otherwise return `NATIVE_REVIEW_REQUIRED`.
