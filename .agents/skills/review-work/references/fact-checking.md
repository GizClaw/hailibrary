# Work fact-checking policy

The purpose is to prevent a language-learning text from teaching false knowledge or normalizing implausible or unsafe behavior. Review both stated facts and assumptions embedded in narrative causality or artwork.

## Build the claim inventory

Inspect all locales, questions, vocabulary definitions, research notes, artwork scene descriptions, and visible images. Record the exact file/page and the proposition a learner could reasonably take away.

Look beyond obvious nonfiction sentences. Common hidden claim categories include:

- animal anatomy, diet, habitat, movement, senses, and behavior;
- weather, seasons, geography, astronomy, ecology, and time scales;
- physical causality, forces, materials, machines, tools, transport, and object use;
- health, food, hygiene, medicine, emergencies, and personal safety;
- history, dates, named people, institutions, technologies, and chronology;
- cultural customs, names, clothing, holidays, social roles, and etiquette;
- quantities, distances, sizes, temperatures, speed, duration, and relative scale;
- what characters know, observe, infer, or can realistically accomplish;
- whether a question's accepted answer overstates what the evidence proves;
- whether an illustration contradicts the prose or depicts an unsafe or impossible action as ordinary.

## Select sources

Prefer sources closest to the claim:

- government agencies, standards bodies, public research institutions, and peer-reviewed research for science, health, safety, measurements, and regulation;
- museums, archives, libraries, universities, primary documents, and recognized scholarly institutions for history and culture;
- official geographic, meteorological, astronomical, environmental, and statistical agencies for place and natural-world claims;
- manufacturers' technical documentation or standards organizations for how a device or material works;
- authoritative language sources under `$review-vocabulary` for lexical claims.

Use current sources when facts, classifications, guidance, names, or standards may have changed. Open the actual source. Do not use a search snippet, model answer, unsourced summary, commercial content farm, or user-edited encyclopedia as the sole support for a material claim.

Use a second independent source when the claim is contested, surprising, culturally sensitive, safety-relevant, medical, historical, or central to the plot. Note when reliable sources disagree.

## Judge the work

Separate these outcomes:

- **Factual error:** the work contradicts reliable evidence.
- **Misleading simplification:** wording is technically incomplete in a way likely to teach the wrong model.
- **Unsupported claim:** plausible, but the required evidence is absent or insufficient.
- **Outdated claim:** once accepted but no longer current.
- **Cultural overgeneralization:** presents one regional, historical, or group-specific practice as universal.
- **Common-sense or causal error:** everyday actions, sequence, scale, motivation, or physical consequences do not make sense.
- **Internal inconsistency:** contradicts the work's own established facts or illustrations.
- **Declared fiction:** impossible or speculative, but clearly established as part of the invented world and applied consistently.
- **Acceptable learner simplification:** accurate at the target level and unlikely to create a misconception.

Do not excuse an accidental factual error merely because the genre is fantasy. Conversely, do not demand real-world proof for an explicitly invented magical rule; check its clarity and internal consistency instead.

## Research manifest

`research.yaml` is an auditable publication record, not a substitute for review. Confirm that each story-relevant source supports the exact claim attributed to it, that uncertainty and scope are represented honestly, and that every material real-world claim is covered.

If independent review finds a material claim that required research, `required: false` is a finding. If fixes are authorized, correct the story, question, artwork scene, or research manifest as appropriate, then repeat the full fact-check rather than reviewing only the edited claim.
