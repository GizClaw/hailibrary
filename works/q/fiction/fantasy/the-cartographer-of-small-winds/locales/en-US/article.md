# The Cartographer of Small Winds

Every apprentice in Bellweather longed to map a grand wind. Mara preferred the small ones. They slipped under doors and argued with chimney smoke. Buttonwind, the smallest, was already tugging at the corner of her unfinished map. Master Orren watched from the stair.

“A useful map must outlive a pretty morning,” he reminded her.

Mara promised that hers would.

Before breakfast, Farmer Sela led Mara down the east terraces. Furrow Friend, a low golden breeze, usually followed the planting rows. That morning it kept bumping through a broken wall, bending the silver seed-sail plants and shaking loose their ripe pods.

“Give us a calm path until harvest, and I’ll stop calling mapmakers indoor gardeners,” Sela said.

At midday, Niko waited on the upper meadow with a copper-and-violet kite. Ridge Runner lifted it smoothly, but Coppercross kept nudging its tail from the side. Niko laughed each time he recovered.

“Don’t tame that one. A kite without a surprise is laundry on a string,” he told Mara.

That evening, farmers asked for more cloth screens along the terraces. Kite fliers wanted every open wind left alone. Both groups expected Mara’s first public map to settle the matter.

She drew one neat path for each wind. The lines fit so well that even Master Orren’s raised eyebrow looked untidy.

“One map, one answer,” Mara said.

At dawn, Mara traced a contour line, a line joining places of equal height, around each terrace. Furrow Friend liked to travel along those level curves. Wherever the line bent sharply, the breeze slowed and rubbed against the stone.

At noon, Mara climbed to the meadow. Coppercross cut across Ridge Runner, the wind lifting Niko’s kite. Mara marked it as a crosswind, a wind blowing across the direction Ridge Runner carried the kite. Near the field wall, its sideways push caused trouble. Here Niko used that push to turn his kite in a wide circle.

“Same wind, different job,” he said.

Mara wrote the words, then squeezed them into a tiny box in the margin.

By lamplight, Mara copied her observations onto the town map. The copper route crossed the farmers’ blue screen marks and spoiled the clean design. If she showed it, Sela might reject the whole plan.

Mara erased Coppercross from the field edge. She told herself that a small wind could find another way. Outside, something tapped the window latch twice.

The farmers followed Mara’s map the next morning. They set low blue screens beside the marked terraces, leaving the golden paths open. Furrow Friend settled between the rows. The seed-sail plants stood steady, and their silver pods stopped knocking together.

Sela rubbed a muddy thumb across one corner. “Your paper has finally earned some dirt,” she said with a rare smile.

On the meadow, Sela stood beside Mara with her seed basket still on her arm. Niko’s kite sagged between broken bands of Ridge Runner. The new screens had split Ridge Runner into weak patches. Coppercross had vanished from the field edge, but a copper-colored coil now gathered in the narrow ravine below town.

“Did your map send it there?” Niko asked.

“Maps only show winds,” Mara answered.

She watched the coil tighten and wished her answer sounded truer.

At breakfast the next day, Coppercross sprang out of the ravine. It raced through the market lane, flipped empty baskets, and tied three awning ropes into one astonishing knot. No one was hurt, but the lane closed while shopkeepers untangled it.

Mara traced the gust backward from the market to the ravine, then to the blue screens set along the route she had erased. Farmers had put those screens where her map directed, closing Coppercross’s usual gap. She ran uphill to see what else had changed.

Festival practice began before the market reopened. As Mara reached the meadow, a sudden sideways gust pulled two kites together. Niko called for slack. The children fed out line so the kites dipped apart, then brought them down one at a time. After they landed, the leftover gust dragged one ribbon tail onto a wooden practice frame and tore it.

Niko stared at the torn ribbon. “That push used to spill out beside Sela’s fields,” he told Mara. “It never hit us like this.”

He wrapped the tail around his spool without making a joke.

Back in the map house, Niko laid the torn tail among Mara’s scattered maps. Sela set a tilted model screen upright on the relief table. Master Orren was already there, leaning on his staff.

“We put up the screens where your map told us,” Sela said. “They calmed the rows for one day, but the gust burst through the market and swept across the practice field.”

“I saw the crossing path and erased it,” Mara admitted.

Mara slid her compass toward Master Orren. He pushed it back and waited.

“What did you observe, and what did you only hope?” Master Orren asked.

Mara watched Furrow Friend cling to the low terraces at dawn. After midday, she returned to find Ridge Runner spread across the meadow. At dusk, she followed Coppercross through the screen gaps and down to the market lane.

Her first map had stacked those changing routes until they looked like one. She opened a clean notebook page for each time of day.

Mara asked Sela and Niko to test one screen on a clear patch below the meadow. Sela turned it a little while Niko flew one kite from the empty upper edge. Mara watched the golden, silver, and copper paths shift. They reset the screen and kept watching until the change happened again.

“That costs us an hour,” Sela said.

“Less than another market knot,” Niko replied.

In the map house, Master Orren held a brass frame while Mara fitted three translucent sheets inside it: morning, noon, and evening. Sela and Niko watched her restore every observed path, including Coppercross. Around the hill, Mara kept the height lines that had helped her predict where Furrow Friend would slow.

In the corner, her map legend explained the map’s colors and symbols. It also marked observations that still needed another test.

At the second meeting, Mara spread the three sheets over the relief table. She gave Sela and Niko each a blank copper tag.

“If you move a screen or shift the practice area, set a tag here,” Mara said. “I’ll map what the wind does next.”

Sela opened a gap between two model screens. Niko shifted the practice marker above the copper route. When Mara lowered the afternoon sheet, both choices fit without covering Coppercross’s path.

On festival day, Furrow Friend moved quietly between the seed-sail plants, and their silver pods stayed in place. After midday, Ridge Runner widened across the meadow and held the kites high. Coppercross curled through the planned gap, where Niko used its sideways push for one careful turn.

Sela’s screens stood in staggered rows instead of a solid wall. Farmers checked them at noon, and kite fliers waited for Ridge Runner to spread before launching.

At sunset, Mara hung the layered map in the map house. Niko held his repaired kite tail beside it, and Sela left one silver seed pod beside the compass.

“Will this map outlive a pretty morning?” Master Orren asked.

“Only if we keep noticing when it is wrong,” Mara said.

Then she added a fresh copper tag for tomorrow’s observations while Buttonwind tried to steal it.
