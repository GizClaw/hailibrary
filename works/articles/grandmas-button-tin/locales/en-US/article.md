# The Button Tin

## Weather Under the Bed

The first storm Nora heard at Grandma June's apartment was under the guest-room bed.

She had flattened one cheek against the floor and reached into the cool dark for her charging cable. Her fingertips struck something round. When she pulled, the thing answered with a bright metal rattle, as if a pocketful of hard candy had rolled down a fire escape.

Nora jerked back. Grandma June appeared in the doorway with damp sheets piled in her arms and three raindrops shining on her glasses.

“What are you hunting?” she asked.

“My cable. It fell back there last night.” Nora pointed beneath the bed. “I didn't put that thing there.”

“That thing was here before you were.”

Together they tugged out a round tin. It had once been turquoise, but years had faded it to the color of a cloudy pond. Cream flowers circled the lid, and a thumbprint of rust marked one hinge. Grandma wiped away the dust with her apron. She did not open it.

At ten, Nora had recently become old enough for adults to tell her she was old enough. Her new cousin would come home from the hospital in two days, and everyone said Nora would be “the big girl” now. The phrase pleased her for half a second, then pinched. It seemed to mean that she should know how to hold a baby and make one stop crying without anybody showing her.

The silent tin was more interesting.

“What's inside?”

“Things I didn't finish using.”

“Then why are they under the bed?”

Grandma raised one eyebrow. “Why is your unfinished charging cable under there?”

Nora laughed. Grandma pressed her thumb to the lid. It sprang open with a soft pop.

Buttons crowded the tin: shell white, honey yellow, ink blue. Some gleamed. Others were scratched cloudy. There were buttons with two holes and buttons with four, a narrow wooden toggle, and one large green button that looked up with four quiet eyes.

Nora reached for it. Grandma's hand covered the tin first.

“Dusty fingers.”

“Buttons don't mind dust.”

“One of them might.” Grandma dug near the bottom and lifted a pearl button no bigger than a lentil. Light rested softly on its curved face. “This came from your mother's white concert blouse. At her first solo, she worried the top button loose and held it in her fist through the whole song. I found it in her wet palm afterward.”

“Mom got scared?” Nora sat up.

“She claimed she wasn't.”

“Then why did she pull off her button?”

“You can ask her.” Grandma's mouth bent at one corner.

Nora looked into the tin again. The buttons no longer seemed like spare parts. Each one appeared to have been stopped just before it rolled out of a story.

## Three That Stayed

Rain ticked against the balcony rail. Grandma draped the damp sheets over chair backs, and Nora forgot her charging cable. She sat cross-legged on the rug and chose a flat red button.

“This one?”

“Your uncle's raincoat. He was seven, and the hood kept falling over his eyes. He came home with one button missing and two minnows in his boots.”

“How did fish get into his boots?”

“He stepped in the creek. He remembered to rescue the fish and forgot to rescue the button.”

Nora laughed so hard that the red button nearly dropped through the heating grate. Grandma caught it with a hand too quick to belong to anybody's idea of an old lady.

A blue glass button had belonged to Grandpa's favorite sweater. On the day of a family photograph, he wore the sweater backward because the patched elbow looked better on the side away from the camera. In the finished picture, his collar leaned sideways, but the blue button shone like a bead of water.

The wooden toggle belonged to Grandma herself. Long ago, she had taken a bus to another city to learn dressmaking. Cold air leaked through the window, so she fastened her brown coat to the throat. When the bus arrived, the toggle hung by one thread. She guarded it in her fist all the way to the tailor shop and sewed it back before she took off her coat.

“Why did you cut it off later?” Nora asked.

“The coat wore out.”

“But you kept the button.”

“The wood didn't wear out.” Grandma said it briskly, as if that settled the matter.

Nora lined the three buttons across her knee: red, blue, brown. She liked the stories, but something in her tightened. Every button knew what the adults had been like before they were adults. Nora felt like a guest who had arrived after all the good chairs were taken.

“Is there one from me?”

Grandma searched and found a pale yellow button shaped like a bear. Nora recognized it from the overalls she had worn at three. She could not remember the overalls. She only remembered the photograph of her round toddler face, which Mom displayed to relatives whenever she wanted Nora to groan.

“I don't want that one,” Nora said.

Grandma's hand stopped between them. “Why not?”

“Because I don't remember it. That's your story about me.”

Only the rain spoke for a moment. Grandma did not say how adorable Nora had been. She did not tell her to be grateful. She returned the bear button to the tin and left the lid resting on a narrow crack.

“Then we'll wait for one you remember,” she said.

Nora looked at the bear button through the narrow crack. Then she pressed the lid shut herself. She still wanted a button whose story she could remember, but she did not want to borrow someone else's. For now, that choice was hers.

## The Coat They Could Touch

That evening, Mom called from the hospital. On the screen, Aunt May lay against a white pillow with a wrinkled red face tucked beside her. The baby's eyes were closed. Her mouth worked in sleep as if she were tasting a secret.

“Your cousin is meeting you,” Mom said, bringing the phone closer.

Nora leaned away without meaning to. “She can't see me, can she?”

“She'll be at Grandma's in two days. Didn't you say you had a present?”

Nora had said that. She had saved enough allowance for a jacket with rabbit ears on the hood. The picture waited in her phone. But the store had sold out, and every other jacket felt like something chosen at the last minute. Nora did not want an adult to buy one and say that babies could wear anything.

After the call, she found Grandma spreading cream-colored cloth over the dining table. A paper pattern, ruler, chalk, and spools of thread waited beside it.

“What's that for?”

“I planned to make a small blanket.” Grandma rubbed the cloth between two fingers. “There's enough for a coat.”

“It won't have rabbit ears.”

“You can keep waiting for the store.”

Grandma's voice was flat, but Nora heard the dare inside it. She enlarged the saved picture. The pink zipper and smiling rabbit face looked perfect while the screen glowed. When it went dark, only the cream cloth remained under her hand.

“Can we finish in time?”

“I can't finish tonight alone.” Grandma held out the ruler. “Together, we still might not.”

Nora took it.

She held the paper pattern while Grandma drew around it. Nora's first cut wandered inside the chalk line. Grandma did not take the scissors away. She redrew that short edge a little narrower, and Nora continued. Snip by snip, the storm moved farther off. They arranged two front pieces, a back, and two sleeves on the table. The parts looked like a small animal that had not learned to stand.

Grandma lifted her sewing machine from the cabinet. The needle flashed when she pressed the pedal. Nora stayed clear and watched the cloth edge. Whenever it began to drift, she tapped the table once. At the cuff, Grandma let her try.

The machine leaped. A line of stitches ran crooked across the cloth.

Nora pulled her foot away. “Take it out.”

“The cuff folds over it,” Grandma said. “No one will see.”

“I will.”

Grandma passed her the seam ripper.

Removing the little stitches took far longer than making them. Halfway through, Nora's palm grew damp and she wanted to leave the rest. Then she thought of Mom's pearl button, held through an entire song in a sweaty fist. She changed her grip and picked out the next stitch.

At ten that night, the coat had a shape. It had a cream body, pale blue edging, and two soft sleeves. Grandma laid two broad pieces of soft hook-and-loop tape beside it. They would be stitched flat inside the overlapping fronts, with no loose small pieces for the baby to pull free.

“These will close it,” Grandma said. “We can finish them tomorrow.”

Then she pushed the button tin across the table. “You may still choose one for the keepsake scrap.”

Nora reached at once for the large green button. Against the cream cloth, it looked like a leaf after rain. She lifted it, then noticed that Grandma had not moved.

“Whose was this?”

Grandma rolled a thread end between her fingers. “Your mother had a green sweater when she was five. A neighbor gave it to her. She wore it until the sleeves stopped halfway down her arms. When I unraveled the sweater, I used the other buttons. This one stayed.”

Nora closed her fingers around it. She loved the button, but it still held someone else's story. She set it on a scrap of cream cloth and did not ask for the needle yet.

## The Unfinished Fastening

Aunt May and the baby came home a day early. The door opened, and the apartment filled with noise: shopping bags crackling, a thermos knocking against the wall, Mom warning everyone about the shoes, Uncle Rob promising he would stay only five minutes while carrying in three bags.

The coat still needed its soft fastening.

Nora hid it behind her back. She had planned to clip every thread and present it properly. But Grandma was helping Aunt May sit down, and Mom was changing the baby. No one noticed Nora in the middle of the room.

Then the baby cried. It was not the thin sound from the phone. Her whole face turned red. Her fists rose beside her ears as if she objected to every lamp and hand. Nora stood holding the coat, unsure whether to step closer or disappear.

“Could someone close the curtain halfway?” Aunt May asked. Her voice was almost lost beneath the crying.

Nora moved first. The room softened, and soon the cries grew shorter. Aunt May saw the coat in Nora's arms.

“Did you make that?”

“Grandma and I did. It isn't finished.”

Aunt May touched the pale blue edge. Her tired eyes curved. “Neither is she. Let's try it.”

Grandma laid the coat lightly over the baby. It fit, but the two front pieces slipped apart like doors that would not stay closed.

“It needs its fastening,” Mom said.

The broad fabric strips waited on the table beside the green button and cream scrap.

The baby caught Nora's smallest finger. The hand was astonishingly small, but its grip was serious. Nora did not move. She saw the empty place on the coat and understood that she did not need the tin to give her a piece of the past. She was here at the table, beside the curtain, inside this small hand's grasp, making today.

She did not say any of that.

“Use the soft strips for her coat,” she told Grandma. “I want the green button for the scrap.”

Grandma asked only, “Sure?”

“It will stay in the tin, away from the baby.”

Nora sat by the window and pushed the thread toward the needle's eye. Grandma did not take it from her. She only knotted the end after Nora got it through. The first stitch came up crooked. Nora drew it back and tried again.

Mom lifted her phone for a picture. Nora shook her head. “When I'm done.”

Nora stitched one broad strip flat around all four edges while Grandma secured its partner to the other front. When they pressed the two soft pieces together, the coat closed without a loose button. The baby's chin moved above the pale blue edge.

“Is the story hers now?” Nora asked.

Grandma set the tin beside her. “The coat is hers. The story started with both of you.”

## The Button from Today

After the baby fell asleep, the apartment quieted. Aunt May drank soup on the sofa. Mom and Uncle Rob argued in whispers about who would wash the dishes. Nora returned the needles to their wooden case and counted them twice, the way Grandma had taught her.

Nora brought back the green button and its scrap of cream cloth. Three times she practiced with spare cloth. On the fourth, she sewed the button firmly to the keepsake scrap. The knots on the back looked like a nest built in a hurry. She left them.

Nora placed the scrap in the tin, where the old buttons crowded around it.

“Whose button is that?” Grandma asked.

Nora borrowed Grandma's brisk voice. “One I remember.”

Grandma laughed and shut the lid. Old buttons rattled around the new scrap.

From the bedroom came a small grunt. Nora went to look. The baby slept in Aunt May's arms. The cream coat lay folded on a chair, its soft fastening hidden safely inside. Behind Nora, the closed tin gave one quiet rattle as Grandma moved it from the table.
