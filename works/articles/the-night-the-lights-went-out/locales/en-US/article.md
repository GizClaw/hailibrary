# When the Building Went Quiet

## The Last Little Window

The heat had stayed after sunset, pressed into the brick walls and the balcony tiles. June came out of the shower with the ends of her black hair dripping down the back of her pajama shirt. The air conditioner hummed above the television. On the rug, she was building an apartment tower from cereal boxes, tape, and scraps of colored paper.

One paper window remained.

June held it against the top floor. The fan swung toward her and blew it from her fingers. It landed under the coffee table.

“You have terrible timing,” she told the fan.

Her mother was cutting a melon in the kitchen. Her father sat at the table, answering a message and promising he would put his phone away in one minute. He had already made that promise twice.

June crawled under the table for the window. She found it beside one of her yellow sandals. She pressed the paper flat, returned to her tower, and held her breath while she dabbed glue on the back.

The room went dark.

The fan slowed with a long falling sigh. The air conditioner stopped. In the kitchen, the knife clicked against a plate and stayed there. June’s paper window vanished between her fingers.

At first she thought she had closed her eyes. She opened them as wide as she could. Nothing changed.

Then the refrigerator stopped humming too.

The silence was not truly silent. Cicadas buzzed in the trees. A car horn sounded on the street. Water moved through a pipe behind the wall. Yet the apartment felt as if it had stepped backward and left June alone in the middle of the rug.

She reached for her tower and knocked it over. Boxes tumbled. Something small rolled beneath the sofa.

“Mom?”

“I’m in the kitchen. Stay where you are,” her mother called. “Your city is all over the floor.”

“I can get a light,” her father said.

His chair scraped. A white glow appeared under his chin. His eyes floated above it, and his nostrils made two enormous shadows.

June gave a startled hiccup.

Her mother said, “Perhaps point the phone away from your face.”

“I was checking whether it worked.”

“Your face works.”

The light moved down. June almost laughed, but she still wanted her mother close enough to touch. She heard careful steps. Then her mother’s hand found her shoulder.

“There you are,” Mom said.

“I was here the whole time.”

“So was I.”

Dad opened the drawer where the flashlight was supposed to live. He found string, tape, old batteries, a toy wheel, and one wool mitten left from winter. No flashlight.

There was a knock across the hall. Mrs. Wu called through the door, “Did your lights go out too?”

Mom opened it. The emergency light in the hallway made the walls green. Mrs. Wu stood there in a flowered nightdress, holding her folding fan like a paddle.

“The whole floor is dark,” Dad said.

From the balcony above came Milo’s voice. “The whole building! I looked down. Every floor is out!”

He sounded delighted to have breaking news. June did not feel delighted. The words every floor stacked the darkness higher than her fallen tower.

## One Small Flame

Mom found a short candle left from somebody’s birthday, but there was no candleholder. Dad suggested melting wax onto a saucer. Mom shook her head.

“A glass jar,” she said. “And far from the curtain.”

She washed an empty jam jar, dried it, and set it on the round stool in the middle of the balcony. June stayed in the doorway while Dad struck a match. The scratch was sharp. An orange flame jumped up so quickly that June stepped behind Mom.

The little candle stood inside the jar. Its light reached the rim of the stool, Mom’s hands, and the toes of June’s sandals. It could not fill the living room. Still, the dark was no longer one huge thing. It had corners now. It had edges.

Dad found the flashlight at last, sleeping in the toolbox on top of the shoe cabinet. He pressed the switch. Nothing happened. He rolled the batteries against his palm as if that might wake them.

“Dead,” he said.

“Then let it have the night off,” Mom said. She carried three slices of melon to the balcony.

June did not follow. Beyond the open door, the courtyard was a deep empty bowl. The streetlights were out. The bright sign above the corner shop was gone. Even the familiar tree looked like a stranger leaning over the wall.

Mom did not pull her outside. She placed a melon slice on a stool near the doorway and sat down beside the candle. Dad borrowed Mrs. Wu’s folding fan and waved it three times before she laughed from the next balcony.

“Handle goes in your hand, Henry.”

Dad looked at the fan. He had been holding it by the wide end.

“I was testing another method.”

“Does the other method make wind?”

“It makes my arm tired.”

Doors slid open above and below them. A woman asked whether anyone was in the elevator. Someone in the courtyard answered that the building manager had checked. A man worried about ice cream melting in his freezer.

“Eat it now,” another neighbor called.

Children cheered on three different floors.

Usually those voices were covered by televisions, kitchen fans, traffic, and music. Tonight they floated through the open center of the building. June heard a baby fussing on six, clothes hangers clicking on nine, and Milo on twelve trying to count every phone light.

“Two, four, seven, eleven—”

“You missed five,” June said before she meant to.

There was a pause above.

“June? Are you outside?” Milo leaned over his railing, only a shape against the sky.

June looked down. She had left the doorway. She was standing beside the little stool with the melon in her hand. Juice ran cool along her wrist.

“Only a little outside,” she said.

“It still counts.”

Mr. Bell, the building manager, came out below with a flashlight. He asked everyone to wait and reminded them not to use the elevator until the power was checked. Neighbors repeated the message from floor to floor. By the time it reached Milo, it had become, “Everybody sit down and wait!”

“He said not to use the elevator,” June called.

“I know. I was talking to my ice cream.”

More laughter moved through the building. June took another bite of melon. This time she did not hurry toward the door.

## What the Dark Kept

The candle threw three shadows onto the balcony wall. Dad raised Mrs. Wu’s fan, and a flat fish swam above June’s head. June made a dog with her hands. Its mouth opened and closed. Mom tried to make a rabbit, but her fingers became two ears with nothing beneath them.

“Where is its head?” June asked.

“Hidden in the dark.”

“How does it know where to hop?”

Mom studied the unfinished rabbit. “Maybe it listens.”

The building was like that rabbit, June thought. Much of it had disappeared, but it was not empty. A spoon fell in one apartment. A cat complained in another. A door opened, and someone called a name down the stairs. Mrs. Wu’s fan snapped shut, then opened again with a soft wooden clap.

Someone on seven began humming a song and forgot it after two lines. A man on eight picked up the wrong tune. Another voice found the first tune and sent it upward again. Nobody sang the whole song, but it traveled around the courtyard before fading. In the quiet afterward, June heard a city bus announce a stop several streets away. The faint voice seemed to come through the other side of a pillow.

At bedtime, she often complained about chairs scraping upstairs or the gate closing below. Now each sound stood by itself. She could tell what was near and what was far. The sounds were not what had frightened her. The worst moment had been the first one, when she had believed the dark meant nothing was there.

“Still frightened?” Mrs. Wu asked June.

June wanted to say no. No was quick. No sounded brave. But the hallway behind her was still dark enough to hide every room.

“Some,” June said.

“Me too,” Mrs. Wu replied. “Without my glasses, I might put this fan on my nose.”

Milo laughed so hard above them that he coughed. Dad said he was afraid the flashlight would wake in the morning and be offended that nobody had invited it.

Mom said nothing about being afraid. She wiped the melon juice from June’s wrist. Then she moved the jar farther from the curtain and checked that it stood flat on the stool.

A breeze came through the gap between buildings. It was not like the steady push of the air conditioner. It touched June’s damp hair and left. It lifted the corner of a napkin, changed its mind, and disappeared. When it returned, it bent the candle flame low inside the jar.

“Will it go out?” June asked.

“It might.” Mom cupped her hands near the glass without touching it. “If it does, we will still be here.”

June watched the flame straighten. It did not grow simply because the building was dark. It kept one small circle bright.

She slipped off her sandals. The balcony tile felt cool under her feet. She walked two steps closer to the railing.

The courtyard was no longer an empty bowl. Phone lights moved beneath the tree. A few people carried folding chairs outside. The building manager crossed from the front desk toward the electrical room. Beyond the gate, the next block still had power. Cars passed through an intersection, and their headlights slid slowly over the walls.

The dark had not taken the world. It had moved familiar things into unfamiliar places.

Dad touched June’s shoulder. “Look up.”

## Above the Water Tank

At first June saw the black edge of the roof. Above it was a blue so deep it seemed to go on behind everything. On most nights, pale city light hovered over the rooftops. Tonight that glow was thinner, and small bright points showed through.

June tipped her head back.

“Are those all stars?”

“The ones we can see are,” Dad said. “I don’t know their names.”

Milo called from above, “I know the brightest one!”

Mrs. Wu answered at once. “What is it called?”

Milo was quiet for a long time. “The brightest one.”

Laughter fell from the twelfth floor to the eighth and then down into the courtyard. June laughed too. She did not need every star to have a name. Some were sharp. Some were faint. One disappeared when she looked straight at it, then returned when she looked a little to the side.

Mom turned off her phone light. Dad turned off his. The candle was the only yellow glow on the balcony.

June almost asked them to turn the phones on again. She felt the request waiting behind her teeth. Before she spoke, the dark began to change. Her eyes found the pale curve of Mom’s cheek, Dad’s hand resting on one knee, and the slow opening and closing of Mrs. Wu’s fan. More stars seemed to arrive above the roof.

One bright point sat just beside the water tank on the opposite building. June held up one finger. From where she stood, the star was only a fingertip away from the tank. She knew her finger was not measuring the true distance. She liked the measurement anyway.

“Where were they before?” she asked.

“Right there,” Mom said. “Tonight they are easier for us to see.”

June thought of her box building. With the lights on, she had worried about one crooked paper window. With the lights off, the whole tower had vanished. Yet in that same darkness she had heard a building full of people and found a sky above its roof.

The elevator started with a low hum.

Across the courtyard, windows flashed on in a row—white, gold, blue-white. The air conditioner sighed awake. The refrigerator began its familiar song. The living-room lamp flooded the balcony, and June squinted.

Someone shouted, “Power’s back!”

Dad stood. “I’ll check the freezer.”

Mom leaned toward the candle.

June put one hand lightly on her wrist. “Wait.”

Mom looked at her.

“One more star.”

Dad had reached the doorway. He came back and switched off the living-room lamp. One apartment across the courtyard went dark again. Then another. Above them, Milo called, “Who can find the faintest one?”

The air conditioner was running. The elevator was moving. The city glow was already climbing back into the sky. Inside the glass jar, the candle had shortened to a warm bead of light.

June rested her head against Mom’s shoulder and chose the smallest star beside the water tank.

She kept it to herself.
