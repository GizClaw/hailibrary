# The Acorns Hazel Could Not Count

## After One Hundred

The first acorn dropped while Hazel was hanging upside down from a white oak twig. It struck a root, bounced once, and came to rest between two red leaves. Hazel sprang down and turned it in her paws. The shell was sound. The nut felt heavy.

“One,” she said.

Another acorn thumped into the grass. Then another. A breeze moved through the crown, and the old oak began letting go of its summer work.

Hazel remembered the lean part of the previous winter. Snow had sealed the ground. She had dug at three places and found three empty holes. By nightfall she had been chewing bitter bark, her belly pinching tighter with every bite. This year she meant to put a wall of acorns between herself and that hunger. A wall needed a number.

She buried one nut on the north side of a mossy rock. She tucked one beside a fallen log and another under the roots of a fern. She never put too many together. A single store could be found by a mouse, a squirrel, or any other nose with time to spare. Many small stores gave a thief many chances to fail.

“Seven, eight, nine.” After each shallow hole, Hazel pressed the soil down with her hind paws and nudged the count one place higher.

Rook watched from a low branch. He was an old crow with one pale feather at his throat and a blue shine across his folded wings. “If you say every number aloud, the thieves can keep count too.”

Hazel snapped her mouth shut. “How much did you hear?”

“Thirty-two through forty-seven. You used forty-one twice.”

“I did not.”

Rook tipped his head. “The wind must have said it.”

Hazel found a flat white stone. Whenever she buried ten acorns, she scratched a line along its edge. At dusk, six lines showed pale through the dirt. She counted them twice. The marks made winter seem like something she could close outside a door.

The next morning brought colder air and more falling nuts. Hazel ran from the beech trees to the creek bank, from the briar patch to the slope where a dead oak leaned. Each good acorn went into a separate patch of soft soil. She ate one with a worm hole and another whose shell had split. The sound ones were for snow.

Rook sometimes followed above her. He never offered to remember anything. When she passed the same stump for the third time, he only said, “You have grown fond of that piece of wood.”

“I am looking for number fifty-eight.”

“There was no wind when you buried it. Every leaf has moved since then.”

Hazel dug beneath a red leaf, then covered the hole in a hurry. Nothing was there. The sight of the loose soil tightened her belly, though she had eaten that morning.

She needed a better map.

## A Map That Moved

Hazel divided the woods into parts she could hold in her mind: this side of the mossy rock, the far side of the creek, above the fallen log, behind the briars. She added scratches to the white stone. She nipped small notches in dead bark. For three new caches, she arranged twigs like arrows, each point aimed at hidden food.

“Now nothing can get lost,” she told Rook.

He landed beside one of the arrows. A gust rolled its top twig halfway around. “Nothing?”

Hazel shoved the twig back. “None of my signs.”

Rain arrived in the night. Water ran down the slope, tamping some caches firm and cutting tiny channels through others. A deer mouse smelled two acorns beneath the briars and carried them off. Near the fallen log, a strange squirrel dug up another, ate half, and buried what remained beyond Hazel's usual path.

At dawn, Hazel burst from her nest. Her twig arrows lay apart. The bark notches remained, but fresh yellow leaves had covered the ground below them. Mud streaked the white stone. One scratch had deepened into a groove. A corner had broken away.

Hazel touched each mark. “Six, seven—that one is rain. It does not count. Eight? Were these two one line?”

“The stone is quiet this morning,” Rook said.

“I had ninety acorns. Maybe ninety-one. Now I do not know.”

“You know where to look when you are hungry.”

“I do not know how many are in each place.”

Rook pecked an empty cap from under a wet leaf. “Winter asks for food, not a total.”

That made Hazel angry because it was partly useful and did not give back a single missing number. She tried to carry the white stone to her nest. It was too heavy. On her third step, it dropped on her toes.

“Do not laugh.”

Rook tucked his beak under one wing. His shoulders shook anyway.

Hazel kept storing. She still spread her acorns across the woods, but she stopped returning to the stone for every ten. She learned the thick bend of one root, a split gray boulder, and a stump with three patches of moss. Nose near the ground, she noticed the smell of disturbed earth. Her paws felt where soil was soft. Sometimes the number slipped away, and she began again from the last one she trusted. Sometimes she began from the wrong one. Once, to her surprise, she did not begin again at all.

Late one afternoon, the shadow of a hawk crossed the leaves. Hazel had an acorn in her mouth. She could not reach her usual trees, so she dove into low brush beside a shelf of rock. She dug, dropped the acorn, swept soil over it, and raced through a narrow crack to safety.

When the danger passed, Hazel looked down from a branch. Wind turned the leaves of the brush silver-side up. She knew her acorn was somewhere below, but every clump looked like the next.

“One hundred three,” she whispered.

A moment later, she said, “Or one hundred two.”

Rook did not tease her. He looked at the darkening clouds. “Home first.”

## Holes Under Snow

The first snow only outlined the fallen leaves. Hazel found the acorn beside the mossy rock at once. She scraped away the loose earth, split the shell, and ate until warmth spread from her tongue to her chest.

The second snow covered the low grass. She recovered two acorns near the log and one beneath the fern roots. She did not always find what she sought. A mouse had left prints beside one empty hole. Another long search ended at a round stone. Still, she found more than she missed.

Deep winter hardened the snow into a crust. Twigs rang like glass when the wind knocked them together. One morning Hazel woke to a nest that held only clean shells. She went first to the white stone. She dug until snow clung to her whiskers, but she found no acorn.

“Wrong place,” she panted.

Rook was hunched on a branch above her. “You remember others.”

Hazel remembered the split boulder, but ice filled its crack. She remembered the stump with three patches of moss, but snow hid every patch. She ran toward the creek, stopped, and turned for the dead-oak slope. Each place looked almost known and entirely strange.

Then she remembered leaves flashing silver beneath the hawk's shadow. Low brush. A rock shelf. One acorn whose number had never stayed still.

“There,” she said.

Snow had pressed the brush close to the ground. Hazel dug beneath the first clump and found frozen roots. She dug beneath the second and found a curled leaf. At the third, one claw tapped shell.

Her acorn was still there. Another lay beside it. Perhaps she had buried both. Perhaps another squirrel had moved the second one. The woods had kept no label.

Hazel pushed one toward Rook.

“Crows do not live on acorns,” he said.

“You did help me remember.”

“A reminder is not worth half an acorn.”

“Then you owe me half.”

Rook opened the shell with his beak. They crouched under the snowy brush and shared the nut. The wind above them stayed cold, but the hard little fist inside Hazel's belly loosened.

Winter did not spare her because she had counted carefully, and it did not punish her because the count had failed. Every day she searched with her memory, her nose, the shape of roots, and whatever edge of leaf the wind uncovered. Some stores had been taken. Some she never found. Yet because the caches lay scattered, one empty hole did not empty them all.

During the deepest week of snow, Hazel stopped trying to calculate what remained. She had not stopped caring. When she found an acorn, she ate. Then she chose the next place to search.

## What Stayed Lost

When water began talking under the creek ice, Hazel was lean, but her tail was still full. On the first patch of bare ground, she uncovered half a blackened acorn and pushed it aside. Near the mossy rock she found her white stone. Rain and ice had softened some scratches and crossed others. No honest total remained.

Hazel turned the stone over. There was no acorn beneath it, only an earthworm retreating into the dark.

“You could count spring buds,” Rook suggested.

“No.” Hazel answered so quickly that she laughed.

Several days later, she crossed the dead-oak slope through the smell of wet soil and opening leaves. On the far side of the fallen log, a hooked green shoot had risen from the ground. A split brown shell still clung to its top. Hazel stopped. It was not grass. Two new leaves eased apart like paws reaching out of a bed.

She brushed leaves from the ground nearby and found a second shoot. A third stood above the creek. Behind the briars, two grew close together, one leaning toward the light.

“Who put acorns there?” Hazel asked.

Rook dropped from the sky and studied the shoots. “You are not the only creature who buries them.”

Hazel recognized the log, the split boulder, and the narrow rock shelf by the brush. She could not recognize an acorn after it had opened, sent a root down, and lifted leaves into the light. Some shoots might have come from her forgotten stores. Other nuts might have been moved by thieves or buried by strangers. Some would be eaten. Some would not survive a dry summer. These few had at least reached spring.

Hazel touched the nearest leaf, then pulled her paw back. It was tender, but every vein already showed.

“Who does it belong to?” she asked.

“If you eat it, it belongs to lunch,” said Rook. “If you leave it, it belongs to itself.”

Hazel glared at him. The answer was another joke, and once again it did not settle the question.

That afternoon, a hard wind crossed the woods. A dead branch fell beside the smallest seedling and pinned its outer leaves against the ground. Hazel had already run past when she heard them scrape. She returned, caught the narrow end of the branch in her teeth, and pulled. It was far heavier than an acorn. Three tries opened a patch of clear ground around the little oak.

From above, Rook called, “Was that the first tree or the fifth?”

Hazel sat in the damp earth, breathing hard. The seedling rocked in the wind but stayed upright.

“This one,” she said.

By early summer, tall grass filled the open places. Not every seedling remained. The one above the creek was gone, and only one of the pair behind the briars survived. The oak beside the fallen log grew more leaves, darkening from yellow-green to green. Hazel often passed on a branch route above it. Sometimes she stopped to knock a leaf-eating caterpillar away. Sometimes she only looked.

Autumn returned. The old white oak began dropping acorns again. The first one struck a root and rolled between two red leaves, as though last year's nut had come back along the same path.

Hazel picked it up and weighed it in her paws. Heavy. Sound. No worm hole. She carried it past the mossy rock and did not stop. She passed the white stone too. In a patch of loose earth farther from the old oak, she dug a shallow hole, set down the acorn, and covered it carefully.

Rook watched from a low branch. “No number?”

Hazel pressed the last soil into place. She looked at the roots, the rock shelf, and the leaves shifting in the wind. Then she looked at the young oak beside the fallen log, now tall enough to reach her chest.

“I remember this one,” she said.

Then she ran back to the old tree to catch the next.
