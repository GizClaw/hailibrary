# North by the Water Gauge

## A Line Too Thin

Daniel boarded the River Lark with a camera, a new rain jacket, and the private belief that steering a cargo boat could not be much harder than steering the simulator at the maritime museum.

His aunt May looked at his white sneakers. “First job: change those.”

“We haven’t left Hangzhou.”

“The deck is already slippery.”

By the time he returned in gray work shoes, the lines were aboard. The River Lark eased away from the wharf and moved north toward Gongchen Bridge. Daniel raised his phone, trying to fit all three stone arches into the frame.

“Back a little,” he called into the wheelhouse.

Aunt May kept one hand on the controls. “The boat doesn’t move for your picture. Stay inside the yellow line.”

Daniel glanced down. His heel was inches from a coil of heavy rope. He stepped inward just as the bridge passed overhead. Vendors’ calls and bicycle bells vanished inside the arch. The engine note rolled around the stone, and then morning light spilled across the deck.

It was Daniel’s first working voyage with his aunt. On a map, he had drawn a clean blue stroke from Hangzhou to Beijing. He planned to post one episode each day under the title One Canal, Half a Country.

Aunt May had studied the map and asked one question. “How long is your school break?”

“Twelve days.”

“Then you will see a small piece of that line.”

The River Lark was carrying bagged construction material to Jiangsu. Its diesel engine sent a steady tremor through the soles of Daniel’s shoes. Tea shivered in the crew’s cups.

The canal refused to look the way he had prepared for it to look. Warehouses stood beside willows. Cranes rose behind tiled roofs. A railway crossed high above an old stone bridge. Passing barges lifted the River Lark on their wakes and set it down again. Daniel had expected an antique ribbon of quiet water. He found a working route that would not hold still for his camera.

“What did boats carry long ago?” he asked, pointing the lens at his aunt.

She turned her face away. “Camera off while I’m steering.”

After lunch, she brought an old wooden grain measure from a cabinet. Its corners were worn round, and a fine crack crossed one side. It had belonged to Daniel’s grandfather. Daniel propped up his phone and waited for a perfect opening line.

“The Grand Canal was not dug all at once,” Aunt May said. “Waterways were built in sections from very early times. In the Sui dynasty, they became a great north-south transport system, and later dynasties kept changing and repairing it. Grain and other important goods traveled along it. By the thirteenth century, more than two thousand kilometers of artificial waterways linked five major river basins.”

Daniel stopped recording. “That part is good.”

“There is another part. Not every sack of grain went all the way from Hangzhou to Beijing. The canal did not stay the same for two thousand years.”

“You keep saying what it wasn’t.”

“Because your blue line is too straight and too thin.”

He was ready with an answer, but a side channel entered from the west. In less than a minute, the water around them became twice as wide. His line had no room for side channels, warehouses, bridge shadows, or the waves of vessels passing the other way.

That evening, Aunt May handed him the waterproof voyage log. “At tomorrow’s lock, write the dispatch time and the water-gauge readings when we enter and leave.”

“I need to film the lock.”

“Log first. Film second.”

“My phone can save the numbers.”

She opened the book and flattened a page. “The equipment saves data. You will record what you see and hear. On a working boat, when someone accepts a job, the rest of us plan as if it will be done.”

Daniel tucked the log under his arm. It felt like a pretend task invented for a visiting nephew.

## A Stair Made of Water

The next morning, boats formed a slow line in the lock approach. Radio calls gave vessel names, sizes, tonnage, and cargo. When their turn came to report, Aunt May provided the River Lark’s information and waited for instructions.

Daniel counted boats from the passenger area. Coal lay under black covers. Another vessel carried stone. Dark red containers rose behind a low wheelhouse. A narrow boat edged half a length toward an opening, then backed away.

“There is plenty of water,” Daniel said. “Why can’t it go first?”

“The lock operator is arranging vessels that can enter safely together. A gap near the gate is not an invitation.”

The wait lasted longer than Daniel expected. He filmed the bow for ten minutes. Almost nothing in the frame changed, so he began adding music to yesterday’s bridge scene. A deckhand named Mr. Wu had to remind him to write the dispatch time. Daniel squeezed the numbers into the log too quickly.

At last, the gates opened for their group. The River Lark moved at low speed between towering concrete walls. Crew members prepared the lines. Gates closed behind the stern, and the engine sound came back from every hard surface. Wet marks stood far above the wheelhouse roof. Daniel felt as if the boat had entered a giant box with no lid.

Water entered the chamber through passages below the surface. Brown-green swirls turned beside the hull. Without wings or cables, the River Lark rose with a leaf floating near the wall.

“Are pumps pushing us up?” Daniel asked.

“A usual lock uses water from the higher reach,” Aunt May said. “Water flows into the chamber, so the surface rises. For a boat going down, water leaves toward the lower reach, and the boat sinks with it.”

Daniel watched the gauge and called out a reading. The hull shifted. His pencil made a long tail across the page.

“Why can’t the gate open now?”

“The chamber is still lower than the water beyond that gate. We wait until the levels match.”

He aimed his camera at the seam between the gates. He wanted them to open on the strongest beat in his music. The water rose far more slowly than his video required. He looked from screen to wall and nearly missed the second reading.

At last, the churning eased. The gate opened without drama. The water that had seemed high above them now continued at the same level beneath their hull. Behind the River Lark, another group waited at the foot of the watery step.

Daniel had forgotten to press record.

That afternoon, they approached a seven-arch stone bridge. Each arch made a dark circle with its reflection. Daniel had just arranged the circles on his screen when Mr. Wu called from the deck.

“Daniel, check the starboard fender.”

Daniel watched the bridge slide toward the edge of his frame. He lowered the phone and inspected the fender. Its line was secure. The job took less than a minute, but the wake had already broken the reflected arches behind them.

“I knew nothing was wrong,” he said.

Mr. Wu pushed his gloves into a back pocket. “Finding nothing wrong is not the same as checking for nothing.”

Daniel did not record that sentence. It sounded like something adults kept ready for children.

## The Box He Did Not Move

On the third afternoon, the sky turned a dusty yellow. Poplar leaves along the bank hung still, while a deep gray band gathered in the distance. A weather notice appeared on the wheelhouse display. Aunt May sent Mr. Wu to inspect the hatch covers and tarpaulin.

“Daniel, put the blue tool case under shelter.”

He was editing the lock sequence. In three seconds, the gate would open with the music. “Got it.”

“Now.”

“Right away.”

He dragged the clip a fraction to the left. The beat missed. He tried again. When the gate finally opened at the perfect instant, every poplar outside the window had bent in the same direction.

The first drops struck the steel deck like tossed beans. Daniel remembered the case and ran aft. It was still where Aunt May had pointed. Each time the boat rolled, it slid a few inches. Beside it, one tie on the blue cargo cover had come loose. Wind entered beneath the edge and lifted the whole sheet. The rope cracked against the coaming.

Daniel reached for it.

“Inside the yellow line!” Aunt May shouted from the wheelhouse.

The tool case slid toward a drain. Daniel took one step across the boundary. The free rope whipped past his face and struck the rail. He froze. A few inches closer and it would have hit him. A loop around his ankle could have pulled him down before the next gust allowed him to stand.

Aunt May reduced speed and kept the boat steady. Mr. Wu fastened his life vest and approached low from the sheltered side. He did not leap for the cover. With a boat hook, he pulled the case away from the drain. Then he pinned the loose rope and secured the tarpaulin.

“Spare line!” he called.

Daniel stepped behind the boundary and tried to pass it to him. His hands shook. He threw the coil while still holding the end, and Mr. Wu had to wait while he untangled it and tried again.

Ten minutes later, the dark cloud had moved east. Water streamed from the cover into the canal. Aunt May checked every tie before opening the blue case to make sure the tools and gauges inside were dry. Only then did she come to Daniel.

“What did I ask the first time?”

“Put the case away.”

“What did you answer?”

“Got it.”

“So I counted it as handled.”

Daniel studied his wet shoes. He could blame the sudden weather, the tie that had already been loose, or the person who left the case there. Every excuse contained a small truth, but none could turn “Got it” into a case under shelter.

“I wanted to finish the edit.”

Aunt May did not raise her voice. “If you can’t do a job, say so. If you don’t understand, ask. When you accept it and leave it undone, the rest of us don’t know we must fill the hole.”

Mr. Wu wrung rain from his gloves. “And when the captain tells you to step back, step back. Don’t give us a second danger made out of good intentions.”

Daniel expected to lose his phone and spend the voyage below deck. Instead, Aunt May gave him a towel and the log.

“Dry off. Then record the gust, the speed reduction, and the delay.”

Rain had blurred several lines on the page. Daniel wrote slowly. For the first time, he understood that the log held more than numbers. A blank could be a task that everyone believed someone else had completed.

That night, he opened his unfinished documentary. In the first clip, he stood below Gongchen Bridge and announced, “The most important quality in a real crew is responsibility.” His voice was loud and certain, as if he had understood for years.

Daniel deleted the clip. After a moment, he restored it from the trash. Deleting it felt too much like hiding. He followed it with the empty deck before the storm: the blue case waiting in the wrong place, one fat drop landing on its lid. He had no thrilling rescue footage because he had not been filming then. He added one plain sentence.

“The case should have been inside before the rain. I said I would move it, and I didn’t.”

He added no music.

## A Bridge, a Crane, and a Bowl of Noodles

The farther they traveled into Jiangsu, the busier the water became. Before sunrise, navigation lights moved through the mist. Boats carrying coal and construction material queued for locks. Cranes lifted cargo beside towns waking along the banks. Vessel names came over the radio in dispatch order.

Daniel began leaving his phone in the cabin while the crew prepared lines. He checked times, repeated instructions, and asked before he filmed. He stopped answering, “Right away.” Instead he said, “I will move the blue case, then come back for the gauge.” If he was already working, he said, “I need two minutes to finish this reading. Is that soon enough?”

The first time, it felt like admitting he was not useful. Aunt May merely checked the clock. “Two minutes is fine. Finish that first.”

At another lock, the vessel ahead entered while the River Lark remained outside. Daniel assumed the operator had wasted open space. Aunt May told him to compare the other vessel’s width, its line position, and the signal. After watching, he changed his note from unnecessary delay to waiting for the next chamber. Space that looked empty to him was not always a safe place for another boat.

They moored near an old stone bridge to take on supplies. Mrs. Zhou from a waterside noodle stall carried three steaming bowls aboard. She had grown up beside the canal. She remembered wooden boats and had watched motor cargo vessels grow larger.

“Are new boats and ports destroying the old canal?” Daniel asked.

Mrs. Zhou tapped her chopsticks on the bowl. “Which canal? The bridge, the water, the port, or my brother’s boat?”

“All of it.”

“That question is larger than my washbasin. Eat before the noodles stick together.”

Across the water, a crane lowered bagged goods onto a pallet. An egret hunted among reeds below the old bridge. The stone was real, and so was the new crane. The smell of diesel was real, and so were the scallions in his soup. Daniel could no longer decide which thing to cut out so his film would look more like an ancient canal.

Mrs. Zhou blew across her tea. The bank had been rougher and more crowded when she was young, she said. The channel had been improved. Houses and wharves had changed. Some old things remained; others did not. She offered no final answer. She only told Daniel to finish the last noodles in his bowl.

He opened his first map. The blue route was too narrow to contain even one town. He zoomed in until the line spread apart and names appeared—bridges, locks, side channels, ports. He opened the title box for his film, but left the cursor blinking in an empty space.

## The Last Entry

Near their destination in Huai’an, a terminal crane began unloading the River Lark. The blue tarpaulin lay rolled and tied. Pallets of construction material left the hold one after another.

Daniel wanted to photograph the old wooden measure beside the empty cargo space. Then he worried viewers might believe it had measured grain on a particular historic voyage.

He found his aunt. “Can I film it if I explain that it only belonged to Grandpa?”

“Yes.”

“Will you explain it on camera?”

Aunt May was checking the delivery paper. “After I sign. I’ll say only what I know.”

It was the first time she had agreed to appear. Daniel put the phone away and waited without reminding her.

When unloading ended, Aunt May handed him the voyage log. “Verify the delivery time.”

Daniel checked the terminal clock, the wheelhouse display, and the last page. “Ready.”

She held on to the pen. “Finished, or ready to begin?”

He checked once more and wrote the time. “Finished.”

On the return voyage, they entered a lock after sunset. Red and green signals stretched across the black water. When the River Lark was secured inside, the gates closed behind it. Water flowed from the chamber toward the lower reach, and the boat descended beside the wet concrete wall.

Daniel kept his phone in its case. From inside the yellow line, he read the gauge aloud. Going down made the wall’s height easier to feel. The top moved farther away, and wet marks that had been near the deck climbed above his head. He recorded each reading, called it to his aunt, and repeated it for confirmation.

The lower water still lay below the chamber, so the gate remained closed. Daniel did not ask why it could not hurry. He watched the two levels draw together until the rush of water softened and the dark reach beyond became one surface with the water under the hull.

The gates began to part.

Aunt May kept one hand on the controls and nodded toward the log. Daniel wrote the last reading in the box marked CREW OBSERVATIONS. His pencil passed the crooked line from the first lock and the words blurred by rain. He did not tear out the page or write around it.

The next morning, Gongchen Bridge was still far to the south. Daniel sat in the cabin editing. The wooden measure, stone arches, cranes, waiting vessels, empty deck in the rain, and marks on the water gauge followed one another across his screen. He changed the title to North by the Water Gauge.

Before exporting the film, he added one short shot. The voyage log lay open by the window. Its ruled lines traveled north, bent around bridges and locks, paused at a rain stain, and continued beyond it.
