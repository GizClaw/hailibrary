# The Valley of Borrowed Shadows

Li enters the deep valley at dawn with an amber prism and a field case. She has come to examine the town's increasingly unreliable shadow clock.

The stone tower casts two long shadows across the empty plaza. Li says, “Two shadows mean there may be a second light source.”

Tama carefully marks both moving edges with separate colored cords. Tama says, “The stronger shadow points west. The faint one points elsewhere across the plaza.”

Some residents blame a second sun; others point toward the ridge machinery. Li raises her prism. “If either guess is right, it should tell us what to look for next.”

Li compares the shadows closely. One has a sharp gray edge, while the other is faint and slightly green.

The greenish shadow shortens quickly across Tama's marked cords. During the same measured minutes, the natural sun shadow changes only a little.

Li holds her prism in a dusty beam crossing the plaza. The split colors show that the beam carries ordinary reflected sunlight.

The beam rises toward an immense curved shape on the ridge. Tama says, “Then the second light may come from above, not from another sun.”

Tama shows Li the old circular clock face in the center of town. Its two pointer shadows disagree, so residents cannot read the hour reliably or plan shared work.

Li and Tama climb a maintained stone stair instead of crossing the steep slope. The reflected beam remains visible above them.

At the ridge, Oren guards a vast mirror partly covered by cloth. Oren says, “People here are used to winter light. They stopped asking where it came from.”

Rows of enormous curved mirrors face different parts of the valley. Their original water channels once turned them together as the sun moved through the winter sky.

Oren rotates one mirror by a small amount. A warm reflected beam slides across the lower plaza.

Far below, the faint shadow moves at the same moment. Li calls to Tama, “Mark the time. The shadow moved when Oren moved the mirror.”

Oren reveals cracked water channels beside the old mechanism. Oren says, “I adjusted mirrors by hand to keep the ridge greenhouse warm.”

His physical model shows sunlight redirected around a rock wall toward a shaded greenhouse. Without that added energy, young food plants develop more slowly during the coldest winter weeks.

Tama looks from the confused clock toward the distant greenhouse. “If we turn the beam off, the greenhouse loses heat. If we leave it here, the clock stays wrong.”

Li places two light tokens between the ridge and valley models. Li says, “We need to send light to the greenhouse without letting it cross the clock.”

Residents from both communities gather and compare the shared physical model. They reject turning every mirror off and choose a separate receiver beyond the clock plaza.

The mirrors rotate away from the clock plaza. Their light now reaches a pale stone receiver connected to the ridge system.

The clock tower casts one clear shadow again. Above it, the ridge receiver glows and the greenhouse still receives the light it needs.

Li departs at sunrise while Oren and Tama inspect the repaired controls. Tama hands Oren the two-shadow model. “At noon, we check the clock and receiver together.”
