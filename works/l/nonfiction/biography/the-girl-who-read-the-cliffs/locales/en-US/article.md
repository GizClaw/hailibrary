# The Girl Who Read the Cliffs

In 1799, Mary Anning was born in Lyme Regis, a small English town beside steep cliffs. Her family had little money. Her father taught Mary and her brother Joseph to find and clean fossils.

Rain and waves kept changing the cliffs. Rockfalls exposed fresh stone, but they could kill. Years later, in October 1833, a cliff fall killed Mary's dog Tray at her feet. Mary wrote that she had almost met the same fate.

Back in 1811, Joseph found a huge fossil skull below a cliff. Mary returned to uncover more bones. By early 1813, much of a skeleton about five meters long had emerged from the rock.

The bones looked like no animal the townspeople knew. Mary cleaned and prepared the heavy fossil for sale. After her father died in 1810, the money helped support her family. The fossil later traveled to London, where men of science studied it.

Some scientists first thought the animal was a crocodile. Later study showed that it was an ichthyosaur, an extinct sea reptile. This was the first largely complete ichthyosaur skeleton to be uncovered and scientifically described.

The scientific report brought attention to the strange animal. Yet the report did not name Mary as its finder. Readers saw the fossil and the men's ideas, but the report left out Mary's careful work.

Mary kept searching. She also studied anatomy and geology, made exact drawings, and compared one bone with another. With little formal schooling, she built knowledge from books and many finds. Visitors and scientists began asking for her opinion.

In December 1823, Mary found another important fossil. Its bones remained connected in their natural places. It was the first complete, articulated plesiosaur skeleton known to science. The animal, unlike any living creature people knew, had a very long neck and a small head.

Mary drew the bones in their places and shared the drawing. In France, the famous scientist Georges Cuvier studied a copy. He thought the neck had too many bones, so he wondered whether the fossil was fake.

More drawings were sent, and the Geological Society held a special meeting in 1824. Mary was not invited. The evidence showed that the fossil was real. Cuvier no longer doubted it.

Scientists described the unusual sea reptile. The long line of connected neck bones, which had caused doubt, became important evidence. Once again, men presented the discovery while the woman who found and prepared it stood outside their group.

Several barriers helped keep Mary's name out of public view. She was a working-class woman with little formal education. The Geological Society did not allow women to join. Buyers and male scientists often received public attention for fossils that Mary found, cleaned, and studied.

Still, many visitors and scientists respected Mary's knowledge during her life. They came to her shop and asked what she had observed. Museums kept her fossils, even when old records did not clearly show her part in each discovery.

Today, fossils Mary found are displayed in the Natural History Museum's Fossil Marine Reptiles Gallery. Visitors to Lyme Regis should search for loose fossils on the beach with an adult. They should stay away from the bottom of cliffs, new rockfalls, and mudflows, and watch the tides.
