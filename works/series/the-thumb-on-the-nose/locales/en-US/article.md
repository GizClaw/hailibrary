# The Thumb on the Nose

## A Telegram in the Rain

Rain had polished the stones outside the Royal Museum of Natural History in Brussels when Émile heard boots on the stairs. He was nineteen, and his morning's work was to number the toe bones of a cave bear. Louis De Pauw entered with a telegram pressed flat beneath one broad hand. A wet corner clung to his sleeve.

“Bernissart coal mine,” he read. “Bones in a fault, three hundred twenty-two meters down. Pyrite is already damaging them. They want someone from the museum tomorrow.”

Émile looked toward the display case across the room. It held a cast of an Iguanodon tooth, worn along the edge like a tough leaf. More than fifty years earlier, the English physician and geologist Gideon Mantell had used teeth and scattered bones to describe Iguanodon in 1825. Its name meant “iguana tooth.” In a famous reconstruction nearby, the animal crouched like an enormous rhinoceros with a pointed horn on its nose.

That picture had been Émile's first careful museum drawing. He had made every line heavy and certain. His instructor had said it looked as if Émile had seen the living animal.

“Take me with you,” Émile said.

De Pauw glanced at his clean cuffs. “A mine is not a drawing room.”

“That is why I should see it.”

De Pauw gave no answer. He pushed the cave bear toe back across the table. Yet beside the plaster, boards, and rolls of cloth loaded before dawn, Émile found a miner's lamp set aside for him.

The lift cage dropped through darkness. Water shone briefly on the shaft walls, layer after layer. The white opening overhead shrank to a coin and then closed. Coal grit found Émile's teeth. Far below, the timber supports made low, damp groans.

A miner named Jules led them into a clay-filled pocket. His lamp moved across a line of brown joints. “These came first. Not one bone and not a heap. They lie as if they fell together.”

De Pauw crouched without touching anything. He passed the light along ribs, vertebrae, a pelvis, and a folded hind limb. Many bones remained near the places they had occupied in the animal. A loose bone could fit many imagined bodies. A skeleton whose parts still kept company could refuse most of them.

Émile began a field sketch of the forelimb. He tore it out and started again. In the first version, without noticing, he had left room near the skull for the horn.

Jules saw the crumpled page. “Are you drawing what is here or what waits in your cabinet?”

“What is here,” Émile said, rubbing out a dark curve.

## The Bone Beside the Hand

They could not haul an Iguanodon out whole. De Pauw marked sections around each group of bones. The workers cut through clay, covered each block with damp paper, cloth, and plaster, and packed it in wood. Golden grains of pyrite glittered inside the fossils. The mineral looked handsome under lamplight, but air and moisture could help it split the bones. Every exposed surface started a race between careful recording and decay.

Late one shift, grit pattered from the roof. Jules put out a nearby flame and ordered everyone behind a support. When the dust settled, De Pauw insisted that the passage be braced before work continued. Émile stared at half-exposed finger bones and felt time scratching at him.

“Another day may cost us the surface,” he said.

“Another step may bury a worker,” De Pauw replied. “The bones waited more than a hundred million years. They do not need you to prove your devotion with a funeral.”

They returned after the timbers were secure. Beside the wrist lay a thick pointed bone. Émile's heart jumped. It looked remarkably like the horn in Mantell's old reconstruction.

But it lay at the hand.

He drew its position before anyone lifted it. Then he measured the narrow end, the broad base, and the distance to each neighboring bone. On the opposite side of the same skeleton, another pointed piece appeared near the other wrist.

“Two horns?” Jules asked.

“Or no horns,” Émile said.

The answer frightened him more than the mine. A horn was printed in books, modeled in stone, and praised in the first drawing he had ever displayed. A mark in a field notebook was only graphite. He darkened the outline anyway.

Crates traveled from Bernissart to Brussels through several seasons. The museum workroom filled with numbered boxes. Damp clay, glue, and plaster made a smell that settled into every coat. Émile matched each block to the sketches made underground. One copied number could separate neighboring bones forever.

He pinned his drawing of the pointed bone to the wall. Beside it he pinned the old picture of the horned Iguanodon. Instead of an answer, the two sheets became questions facing each other.

A visitor passing the open workroom stopped at the older model. “This new mine will prove the horn was real, won't it?”

Émile opened his mouth. “We are still cleaning the bones.”

“But Mantell put it there.”

“Mantell proposed that place.” Émile heard himself replace proved with proposed. The words were close in length. They were not close in weight.

## What Mantell Could See

That evening Émile opened Mantell's published papers and plates. The great achievement of 1825 had begun with teeth. Their form supported the startling idea of a giant plant-eating reptile. Later bones allowed Mantell to change the body he imagined. The pointed bone, however, had not arrived with a label. Setting an isolated piece on the nose was an interpretation made from limited material, not an observation of a living animal.

Émile wrote in the work ledger: POINTED BONE—POSITION UNRESOLVED. The capitals looked too confident, so he drew a thin line beneath the last word.

As more crates opened, uncertainty did not vanish. It became precise. On the first skull, there was no convincing surface for the spike to join. The second skull offered none either. Pointed bones kept appearing with forelimbs, sometimes as a pair. Émile laid out three hands according to their recorded positions. Three middle fingers were stout. The outer finger was long and narrow. The pointed bone occupied the remaining place.

He did not force it down. A familiar shape could tempt a preparator to make a bone obey the desired picture. Matching surfaces had to decide whether it belonged.

“Please ask Mr. De Pauw to come,” he told a porter.

De Pauw arrived with plaster in his beard. He looked at the arranged hands, then at Émile rather than the bone. “What have you changed?”

“Nothing yet.”

“Good. Show me why you want to.”

Émile began with the field drawings. He showed the spike beside the wrist in the clay, the matching bone on the other side, and the absence of a place on either skull. De Pauw turned the broad base toward the first digit without joining it.

“Write all of that,” he said. “Especially the part that disappoints the picture.”

## A Skeleton That Would Not Hold Still

By 1882, the most complete specimens were ready to be assembled. No ordinary room could contain the work, so it moved into the high former Saint George chapel. Wooden scaffolding climbed toward the vaults. Ropes descended from the beams, and fossil bones hung in rows like members of an orchestra waiting for a conductor to choose the tempo.

Louis Dollo, a young scientist, took up the study of the Bernissart animals. He separated neighboring bones and approached their joints from several angles. If a fit seemed easy, he inspected it again. De Pauw asked how each fragile fossil could bear weight. Dollo asked how the parts explained the whole body. Émile carried drawings and tools between them while both men called his name from different levels of the scaffold.

The old Iguanodon had been made a massive animal on four straight legs. The Bernissart pelvis, spine, and unequal limbs demanded a different account. Dollo argued then for a mainly two-legged pose and compared parts of the skeleton with large flightless birds. Scientists in later generations would revise the steep, tail-dragging mount again. In that chapel in 1882, however, the team could answer only the questions their own evidence reached.

The hand remained the sharpest question. One afternoon Émile brought the base of the spike toward the first digit. The surfaces followed the same curve and settled together like a swollen door finally entering its frame.

“No pressure,” De Pauw warned, supporting the wrist.

“I am not pressing.”

“You are pressing with your breath.”

Dollo climbed down from the opposite platform. He compared the other hand, then asked for the record of a second skeleton. This was not one accidental placement. There were paired spikes, no sockets on the skulls, and repeated positions at the first digits. Three independent clues locked together. The old nose horn had nowhere left to stand.

Émile did not feel triumphant. Across the chapel hung the copy he had preserved since his apprenticeship. Its ink lines were still dark and beautifully controlled. Move the spike to the hand, and the picture became wrong in public. Everyone who had admired it could see.

“We could mount the other parts first,” he said.

De Pauw studied him. “The bones can wait. Your ledger can wait. Neither one will write the conclusion for you.”

## Four Drawings

That night, one work lamp burned beneath the chapel vault. Émile spread the old reconstruction on a table and rested an eraser at the animal's nose. He remembered the vertebrae lying in order below the mine, and the empty space he had unconsciously reserved beside the first skull he drew. Errors did not require dishonesty. Sometimes an error grew beside a lonely piece of evidence, then a beautiful drawing fed it.

He did not destroy the old sheet. He laid clean paper beside it. The first new drawing showed the spike alone and assigned it no position. The second showed it beside the hands of several skeletons. The third compared left and right hands and the joining surfaces. Only the fourth showed a reconstruction: the spike as the first digit, a modified thumb.

In the morning, he handed all four drawings to Dollo. “I put the answer ahead of the evidence before.”

Dollo turned back to the old horned animal. “Do not erase this one.”

“Keep the mistake?”

“Keep the reason it was possible, and the reason we changed it. Otherwise people will imagine the correct answer was always sitting on the table.”

De Pauw brought a plaster cradle holding the second hand. Together they placed the corresponding bones. A spike projected from each thumb position; the nasal bones offered no seat for either one. Whether the spikes served in defense, contests, or work with plants could not be settled from shape alone. Émile wrote PURPOSE UNCERTAIN on the draft label. He resisted every more exciting ending.

Before the public mounting, a knot on one support rope slipped. The wrist cradle tipped. Émile reached toward the fossil, but De Pauw caught the tray with his shoulder.

“Wedge!” De Pauw shouted.

Émile did not grab the bone. He drove a wooden block beneath the platform, steadied the plaster base, and called for Dollo to shorten the upper rope. The thumb spike swung once between the three men and stopped.

When Émile stepped back, sweat covered his palms. De Pauw was breathing hard. “This time you saved the position before trying to be a hero.”

“You said the bones could wait.”

“I said people should not be buried. After four years, you have understood half the lesson.”

## The Empty Place

When the mount was complete, an enormous Iguanodon rose inside the chapel. Its posture was still a probable posture, not a final answer sealed in iron. Visitors tipped their heads back beneath it. Some walked directly to the hand, searching for the bone that had once occupied the animal's nose.

A boy laughed at Émile's old picture. “How could they put a thumb on its face?”

Émile almost said, Because they had only fragments. Instead, he led the boy to the first of the four new drawings and covered the others with a folder.

“If this were the only bone you had found, where would you put it?”

The boy held a finger to his forehead, then turned his own hand over. “I don't know.”

“That was what they lacked,” Émile said. “Not intelligence. More bones.”

The boy moved along the sequence of drawings. At the final sheet, he raised his thumb and rotated it beneath the fossil spike. He did not laugh again.

After closing time, late sun entered the high windows and picked out pale scars left by the scaffold. Émile filed the horned drawing with the others. On the table beside the new reconstruction, he left the eraser worn down by years of corrections. It had not removed the past. It had only made a small empty place at the edge of the page, ready for whatever evidence came next.
