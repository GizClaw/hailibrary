# The Open Stone

## The Empty Date

On April eighteenth, Mina carried a paper bag of apricots through the tower gate and found Mr. Sayeed staring at an empty sky.

The old gate tower rose above a square of market roofs and new apartment blocks. Wind lifted the pale undersides of the locust leaves. Sparrows argued in the gutters. Pigeons pushed themselves into the air with busy wings. Nothing crossed the sky with the long, backward-curving wings Mina had come to see.

She set the apricots beside the ticket window and opened the keeper's clothbound ledger. Twenty-three springs filled its ruled pages. Last year's first swift had arrived on April sixteen. The year before, three had appeared on the fourteenth. One cold spring, the first mark did not come until the twenty-fifth.

“Maybe they missed the tower,” Mina said.

Mr. Sayeed oiled the brass lock without looking up. “The birds don't read my dates.”

“You said they knew this place.”

“Knowing a place doesn't remove every storm between here and there.”

Waiting felt different this year. Steel scaffold poles lay stacked against the west wall. Blue barriers leaned beneath the trees. In May, a crew would repair the tower. Winter rain and frost had loosened mortar, and a piece had fallen near the ticket window on a day the tower happened to be closed.

Mina unrolled a copy of the work plan on the stone lion. Arrows pointed to pale scars in the wall. Beside the east cornice, a note said that open joints were to be cleaned and sealed.

She had watched swifts vanish through some of those joints last summer. On the plan, every gap looked like damage. If the birds returned late, would the empty openings look useless to everyone else?

Near closing time, Mr. Sayeed raised one hand.

Mina heard a high, hard cry. Two dark shapes swept over the roof ridge, curved around the bell opening, and climbed. A third dropped from above with wings like narrow sickles. It did not settle on the tiles. It joined the other two, and all three raced around the tower as if drawing loops too quickly for the eye to keep.

“Three!” Mina ran into the courtyard.

Mr. Sayeed finally smiled. He wrote a three beneath April eighteen, then kept his pencil on the page. “Three so far.”

By dusk, eleven had returned. They chased insects in the air and skimmed low over the moat to drink. Only during the breeding season did they need the deep spaces under the tower's eaves. Swifts gave nearly all their lives to the sky. The tower was not their cage, Mina thought. It was a door they had crossed half the world to find.

She kept that thought to herself. One swift folded its wings toward the east corner and disappeared beneath a loose stone.

The next morning, Mina brought a red pencil.

## What a Red Circle Proves

For twelve afternoons, Mina and Mr. Sayeed watched from the ground. They did not climb to the nests or reach into openings. The swifts entered too fast for careless counting, so Mina made a mark only when she saw a bird cross the edge of a stone and vanish.

She numbered each possible entrance. Along the paper's edge she added columns for time, direction, entry, and exit. One afternoon, two swifts crossed in front of the bell opening. Mina struck the page four times with her pencil.

“Four birds. Two holes.”

Mr. Sayeed handed her the binoculars. “Which holes?”

The wall had already gone still. Mina pointed once, then lowered her hand.

“There could have been four,” he said. “But ‘could have been’ does not belong in the ‘seen’ column.”

Mina refused to erase the marks. Mr. Sayeed did not argue. He swept fallen blossoms from the ticket window while the broom whispered over stone. At last, she rubbed so hard that the paper thinned beneath one circle.

“What if I miss an entrance and the crew seals it?”

He stopped sweeping. “Then we watch longer. We don't protect a real nest by inventing one.”

Seven entrances survived their rule. Three lay under the east cornice. Two were above the bell opening. One hid behind a carved drainspout, and another sat deep in the southern shade. Mina darkened a circle only after she saw birds use the same place on different days. Three faint marks remained at the edge of the map with a question beside each.

They could not see inside. Early on, adults sometimes carried light feathers and plant scraps caught in the air. Later, they left with empty mouths and returned again and again with their throats full of insects. Mina guessed that chicks had hatched. Twice she leaned toward the wall, and twice Mr. Sayeed caught the strap of her backpack.

“They came a long way,” he said. “They didn't come back for us to inspect them.”

“How long a way?”

In the keeper's room, he unfolded a migration map. Researchers had fitted tiny light-recording tags to swifts breeding in Beijing. When some of those birds returned the next year, the recovered tags revealed a vast journey. The tracked birds made an annual round trip of about thirty thousand kilometers. They first traveled northwest through Mongolia and Central Asia, continued toward the Red Sea, and reached wintering areas on the southern African plateau before returning in spring.

Mina traced the route without touching the page. “They fly back from Africa and find a stone where their door was. What do they do then?”

“I don't know.” Mr. Sayeed folded the map. “Tomorrow, bring what you do know to the meeting. Leave the question marks as question marks.”

## Seven Openings on a Plan

The construction meeting took place in the keeper's room. The foreman, Lena Ortiz, placed her yellow helmet on her knees. Gray dust clung to her short hair. She showed everyone the place where mortar had fallen, then slid a close photograph of the east wall across the table.

“Water enters this crack,” she said. “When it freezes, the joint opens farther. People walk below it every day. Leaving the wall alone is not a safe choice.”

The city conservator explained that an old wall could not simply be covered like a new one. The crew needed compatible mortar and careful methods. Mr. Sayeed opened his ledger to twenty-three years of arrivals and departures. Mina placed her seven red circles on top.

“Birds are using these now.” Her voice came out small, so she tried again. “We saw them enter on different days.”

Lena turned each observation page. “I believe what you saw. But scaffolding changes how a bird approaches, and a red dot cannot tell my crew whether the stone around a nest is sound.”

“So you will seal all of them?”

“I did not say that.” Lena met her eyes. “I said your map is evidence, not a repair plan.”

Mina looked toward Mr. Sayeed, expecting him to defend her. Instead, he asked whether the crew could begin on the unused west wall. Could the scaffold leave a clear flight path to active openings? Could work near nests wait until the young had flown, while the most dangerous stones were supported from the side?

Lena drew several lines on the plan. The scaffold would have to change. The job was scheduled to finish in June. Workers and materials had been booked. No one in the room could decide the proper size and depth of a protected nesting space.

“Then we bring in someone who can,” the conservator said. “A bird specialist and the structural engineer, together.”

The meeting did not end with a speech. They measured safe distances, compared calendars, and made calls about deliveries. Mina sat until the chair edge left a red line across her leg. Lena never promised to save seven openings. When she left, however, she took Mina's worn map with her.

There was no answer the next day, or the day after. Each afternoon Mina asked Mr. Sayeed. Each afternoon he said, “They are working it out.”

“The chicks won't stop growing while they work it out.”

“The wall won't stop falling because we hurry.”

On the fourth day, Lena returned with a bird specialist and an engineer. They watched the flight paths from the ground, photographed the edges of the openings with a long lens, and examined cracks away from the nest chambers. Mina tried to follow them, but Lena stopped her at the safety line.

“Your map told us where to look,” she said. “Now the birds and the wall have to tell us more.”

Two days later, a revised schedule appeared at the ticket window. The crew would start on the west wall. The east scaffold would stand back from the main approach routes. Occupied areas would wait. Dangerous stones that could not yet be repaired would receive temporary protection.

Mina read the schedule twice. Her name was nowhere on it.

“It shouldn't be,” Mr. Sayeed said. “The swifts didn't sign it either.”

She was preparing to be offended when she noticed seven fine blue circles on the new work plan. Lena had drawn each one around Mina's red marks.

## Nest Four in the Rain

By late May, pale safety mesh covered most of the tower. The open east side looked like a deliberate tear. Swifts banked beside the mesh, skimmed the scaffold poles, and slipped into the nest openings. At first Lena looked up at every sharp call. Soon she could move a drill aside without lifting her eyes.

Visitors complained about the closed walkway. One man asked through the barrier why a few birds caused more trouble than a whole tower. Lena checked a worker's safety rope and did not answer. Mina did not answer either. The only honest reply seemed to be the work itself: which section waited, which delivery moved, which opening required another hour of watching.

In June, rain fell for three days. Before opening on the fourth morning, Mr. Sayeed found fresh dust beneath the east corner. A crack ran from the edge of the stone above Nest Four into the mortar. Yesterday it had been no wider than a grass stem. Now Mina could have fitted her smallest finger into it.

Lena closed the east courtyard. “That block shifted. If it falls, it may tear open the nest chamber with it.”

A dry, urgent whisper came from inside the wall.

Mina stood beyond the barrier with rain on her face. “You said you would wait until the chicks flew.”

“I said we would wait while it was safe.” Lena looked as if she had not slept. “It isn't safe now.”

“Sealing the hole isn't safe for them.”

“That is why nobody moves until the engineer arrives.”

The bird specialist and engineer came while the rain thickened. A lift could not stand close to the weakest ground, so they compared photographs beneath the eaves. Removing the block would secure the wall but open an active nest. Doing nothing might allow the block to fall before the chicks left. The engineer proposed fixing the surrounding stones from the side, then sliding a narrow brace beneath the loose one without blocking the entrance or entering the chamber. The courtyard would remain closed. The brace could still fail.

The conservator asked about the cost. Lena named it and explained how the next two weeks would have to change.

Rainwater streamed from the tiles while they considered the two risks.

“Do it,” Lena said.

“It still needs approval,” the conservator reminded her.

“Then call now.” Lena sealed her phone in a clear bag. “I am not dropping the wall, a visitor, or that nest because one of them was easier to leave off the plan.”

By afternoon, the rain softened. Lena climbed the scaffold in a harness. Mina had been sent outside the gate, where she could see only a piece of the east wall through a gap in the barrier. A hammer tapped once, waited, and tapped again. Adult swifts circled the tower. Their cries seemed to cut the wet air.

One parent carrying insects rushed toward Nest Four, saw the lift, and turned away. It circled and tried again. Lena took both hands from the wall. The engineer froze. The swift passed within inches of her helmet, folded its wings, and entered the old opening.

Lena waited until its short forked tail was gone before lowering her hands.

At dusk, the brace held. A small marker beside the crack would show any further movement, and the courtyard remained closed. Mr. Sayeed wrote the time in his ledger. Then he gave Mina the pencil.

She added no new circle. Beside Nest Four she wrote, “Entrance still in use.”

## The Door They Left

The brace survived the next rain. Every day Mina checked the crack marker before she watched the entrance. She stopped asking Mr. Sayeed to guess when the chicks would emerge. Waiting had not grown shorter, but it was no longer empty.

One July evening, a small dark face appeared at Nest Four. It withdrew, then appeared again. An adult called overhead. The young swift launched and dropped so sharply that Mina gasped. Just above the market awnings, it leveled its long wings, climbed past the trees, and joined the circling adults.

“Two!” Mina said as another shadow crossed the opening.

Mr. Sayeed offered her the binoculars. “Watch the tail.”

She followed the shadow halfway around the tower. It was the same young bird circling back. Mina accepted the pencil and wrote, “One fledgling, confirmed.”

Mr. Sayeed did not praise her. He only held the page down when the wind tried to turn it.

A week later, the bird specialist confirmed that the young had left that section. Lena removed the temporary brace and completed the east wall. The known entrances stayed open, with loose material secured behind them. The crew also added safe nesting spaces high beneath the cornice, close to places the swifts had already chosen. From the square, the openings looked like a few narrow shadows.

On the last construction day, Lena returned Mina's cleaned plan. A fine blue ring surrounded the first red circle, the one rubbed thin by her eraser. The engineer's small check mark sat beside it. Rain had wrinkled one corner of the paper.

“We watch again next year,” Lena said. “Leaving a space does not guarantee a bird will choose it.”

“I know.” Mina did know. The map could not order the sky to follow it. It could only keep people from pretending they had seen nothing.

In September, the evening cries grew fewer. The last group circled the tower several times, rose above the roofs, and scattered toward the northwest. Tracked Beijing swifts often began their long migration in that direction, traveling through Mongolia and Central Asia and toward the Red Sea before reaching southern Africa. No one on the ground could know where each bird would pause that night.

Mina climbed to the open first-level terrace and watched until the dark shapes dissolved into dusk.

“Will they come back next year?” she asked.

Mr. Sayeed opened the ledger to a clean page. “I don't know.”

Wind moved beside the repaired stones. The seven dark openings made no sound, but they had not been sealed. Mina placed both hands on the ledger to hold its lifted corners while Mr. Sayeed wrote the time of the year's last confirmed sighting.
