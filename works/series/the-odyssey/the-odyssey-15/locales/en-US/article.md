# The Road Back to Ithaca

In Sparta, Telemachus lay awake beside the sleeping Pisistratus. Menelaus had welcomed him generously, yet his thoughts kept returning to the men eating in his father's hall. He had come away to learn whether Odysseus lived. Every day spent searching also left his house unwatched.

Athena stood beside him in the dark. It was time to go. Penelope's father and brothers were pressing her to marry Eurymachus, she said; he had given the richest gifts. Athena warned that a remarried woman cared for her new husband and forgot her earlier children and their dead father. Telemachus must get home before property was carried away, and entrust his possessions to a reliable maid until he had a wife of his own.

There was a nearer threat too. The suitors had a ship waiting in the strait between Ithaca and Samos to kill him. He must sail night and day, well away from the islands, with the wind his protecting god would provide. When he reached Ithaca, he should send the crew and ship to town, go himself to Eumaeus, and have the swineherd carry word of his safety to his mother.

Telemachus shook his companion. “Get up. Let's harness the horses.”

Pisistratus opened his eyes to darkness. “We can't drive home yet. Wait for daylight, and let Menelaus send us off properly.”

So Telemachus waited, though he slept no more. At dawn he asked his host for leave. Menelaus would not hold a guest who wanted to go. He would gladly show the young men other cities and gather gifts for them, but Telemachus shook his head. His property needed him. The king ordered breakfast and brought out a cup and a silver mixing bowl with a golden rim, a treasure he had received in Sidon.

Helen came carrying a robe she had embroidered. “Keep this for the woman you marry. Until then, let your mother look after it.”

The cloth was folded among the gifts. The travelers ate, and the horses were brought round. Menelaus poured farewell wine and asked them to carry his greeting to Nestor. As they stood beside the chariot, an eagle swept past on their right with a great white goose in its claws. People rushed after it, shouting from the farmyard. The eagle climbed beyond them.

Pisistratus asked what the sight meant. Before Menelaus could decide, Helen answered. The bird had come from the wild heights and seized its prey in a familiar yard. To her, it meant that Odysseus would come home and strike the men who had taken over his house. Perhaps he was already there, preparing.

“May it be as you say,” Telemachus answered.

They drove all day, slept at Pherae, and reached Pylos the next day. Close to the harbor, Telemachus turned to his friend. Nestor would insist on bringing him indoors. One meal would lead to another, and he could not bear another delay.

“Leave me at the ship. Please explain it to your father.”

Pisistratus understood. He helped unload the gifts at the stern, then urged Telemachus to sail before Nestor heard he had arrived. The chariot went toward the palace. Telemachus called his crew aboard.

He was praying to Athena when a stranger approached. The man, Theoclymenus, belonged to a family of seers. He had killed a kinsman in Argos and fled from the dead man's powerful relatives. Now the ready ship offered his chance to get away.

His ancestor Melampus had been driven from Pylos by Neleus. Neleus took his property while Phylacus held him prisoner for a year over Neleus's daughter and the guarded cattle; a Fury had laid grief upon him too. Melampus had escaped, brought the cattle back, avenged the wrong and won the girl for his brother. He then settled and ruled in Argos. In that family, Amphiaraus had been favored by Zeus and Apollo yet killed at Thebes because of a woman's gifts. Dawn had carried the beautiful Cleitus away to live among immortals. Theoclymenus's father, Polypheides, became Apollo's greatest seer after Amphiaraus died; after quarreling with his own father, he had moved to Hyperesia to prophesy there.

Telemachus told him his name and his father's. He had gone searching for news of Odysseus, he said, though he feared the man was dead.

“Take me with you,” Theoclymenus asked. “The people pursuing me will not stop here.”

Telemachus gave him a place beside the stern and laid his spear on the deck. The ropes came free. Athena sent a following wind, and soon the shore slipped away. The crew worked through darkness and daylight while Telemachus watched the water ahead. Somewhere near his island another ship was waiting for him.

While the son was at sea, his father sat at supper in Eumaeus's hut. Odysseus still wore the old beggar's face. He had learned much from his host, but wanted to know whether the welcome would last. He proposed going to town in the morning. The suitors might give him scraps in return for useful work. He knew how to chop wood, tend a fire, carve meat, and pour wine.

Eumaeus stopped him. “Those men won't thank you for working. They want handsome young servants around them. Stay here. When Telemachus returns, he'll give you clothes and help you on your way.”

Odysseus thanked him, then asked about the absent king's parents. Was either still alive?

Laertes lived, Eumaeus said, but sorrow had driven him away from ordinary company. Losing his wife had deepened the wound left by his son's disappearance. Odysseus's mother had died longing for him.

The swineherd spoke of her with a tenderness he had not shown when describing the palace. She had brought him up beside her daughter, Ctimene. When the girl married on Same, she gave Eumaeus clothes and shoes and sent him to work in the country, loving him no less. He had still been welcome in the house then. Now, with the suitors in control, he could scarcely get a kind word from Penelope. Servants liked visiting their mistress, talking, eating in her house and carrying something back to the country. Those visits had cheered them.

Odysseus listened without telling him why each name hurt. At last he asked how Eumaeus had come to Ithaca as a child. Had his home been taken in war?

The others could sleep if they wished, Eumaeus said. He and the stranger had time for one more story.

“My father was a king. Our island was called Syra. I was too small to know how far away the rest of the world was.”

His nurse had come from Sidon, the daughter of a rich man named Arybas. Taphian raiders had taken her from her family and sold her into his father's household. When a Phoenician merchant ship came to the island, one of its men seduced her as she washed near the vessel. After sleeping with her, he offered to carry her home. Her parents were still alive, he told her. The sailors swore to protect her on the voyage.

She agreed to leave secretly. None of them must speak to her in public, she warned: if her master suspected them, he would imprison her and have the sailors killed. She would take gold to pay her way. And she offered the little boy in her care, the king's son, whom the merchants could sell at a good price.

The ship traded there for a year. At last a man came to the house with a necklace of gold and amber. While Eumaeus's mother and the servants admired it, he gave the nurse her signal. That evening the father was away at a gathering. The nurse took three cups and led the child by the hand down to the harbor. The ship was ready. Before anyone came to fetch him, they had put out to sea.

“Six days and nights we sailed,” Eumaeus said. “On the seventh, Artemis struck the woman down. They threw her body into the water. I was left with men I didn't know.”

The vessel reached Ithaca. There Laertes bought him. The child of one island's king became a servant in another king's household. Anticlea cared for him, and he grew up, but the ship never carried him back to his first home.

Odysseus had heard a true story this time. He answered with sympathy, but said Zeus had given Eumaeus good as well as evil: he now had a good master who fed him well, while the stranger still wandered begging. Odysseus kept his own name hidden. Eumaeus's memories and the stranger's questions kept them awake until only a little of the night remained.

The ship carrying Telemachus reached Ithaca safely. No enemy had caught it. After breakfast on shore, the young man told his companions to take the vessel and gifts to town. He would visit the herdsmen first.

Theoclymenus asked where he should go. Telemachus could not easily welcome him at home: he would be away, and his mother stayed upstairs, out of the suitors' sight. Instead he suggested Eurymachus's house, praising him as the leading man and most persistent suitor. Only Zeus knew, he added, whether the suitors would die before any wedding. As they spoke, a hawk passed on their right carrying a dove. Feathers drifted down between them and the ship. The seer drew Telemachus aside.

“Your house will remain powerful here. I read that plainly in the bird.”

Telemachus hoped he was right. He called to Piraeus, a trusted companion, and asked him to care for the traveler until he could come himself. Piraeus agreed, and Theoclymenus went aboard again with the crew.

Telemachus tightened his sandals, took his spear, and turned inland toward the swineherd's hut.
