# The Last Ship

We returned to Circe's island and did what I had promised Elpenor. His body and armor burned together. We raised a mound beside the sea and planted his oar above it.

Circe brought food to the shore. That night, while the men slept beside the ship, she drew me aside to hear what had happened among the dead. When I finished, she told me what lay ahead.

The Sirens would sing to us first. Men who stopped to listen never returned to their families. Their meadow lay among the remains of those they had drawn ashore.

“Fill your men's ears with wax,” she said. “You may listen if you wish, but have them bind you to the mast. Whatever you ask them to do when the singing starts, they must not release you. If you beg, they must tighten the ropes.”

Beyond the Sirens there were two ways to sail. One led through the Wandering Rocks, where ships were broken in the water and fire. Only the Argo had passed safely, with Hera helping Jason. The other way lay between Scylla and Charybdis.

Scylla waited high in a cave, with six heads on long necks. Each head could take a man from a passing ship. Beneath the opposite rock, Charybdis sucked the sea down and threw it up again. If we were caught in that swallowing water, the whole crew would be lost.

“Keep close to Scylla's side and row hard,” Circe told me. “Six men lost is better than all.”

“Can't I fight her while we pass?”

“She cannot be killed. Don't waste time putting on armor. She could reach down a second time while you wait. Call to her mother, Crataiis, to stop that second attack.”

Then she spoke of the Sun's island, Thrinacia. Its cattle and sheep belonged to the god: seven herds and seven flocks, fifty animals in each. They neither bred nor died away, and his daughters Phaethusa and Lampetie tended them. If we harmed them, the ship and my companions would perish. Tiresias's warning had followed me back into daylight.

At dawn we sailed. I told the men about the Sirens and asked them to bind me. Soon the wind dropped. We lowered the sail and took to the oars. I stopped their ears with wax, and they fastened me upright against the mast.

The two singers saw us coming.

“Odysseus, come closer. We know what the gods made the Greeks and Trojans suffer. Hear us, and you will leave wiser. We can tell you what is still to come in the world.”

I wanted the ship turned towards them. I struggled against the bonds and signaled to be set free. The rowers bent harder to their work. Eurylochus and Perimedes came to the mast and bound me more tightly. Only when the voices were out of reach did they remove the wax and untie me.

Then a roar spread over the water. Spray climbed ahead of us, and the men let their oars go in terror. The ship lost its way. I went among them, urging them back to their places.

“We got out of the Cyclops's cave,” I said. “Take the oars again. Steer away from that boiling water and hold close to the rock.”

I had not told them about Scylla. I thought they would stop rowing if they knew. And despite Circe's warning, I put on my armor and stood at the bow with two spears. I searched the cliff for something I could fight.

Charybdis drew the sea into a turning hollow. For a moment we could see dark mud at its bottom. As we stared, Scylla struck from the other side.

Six men were lifted out of the ship. I saw them above us, reaching down, calling my name. She carried them to her cave and ate them alive as they screamed and reached towards me. I could not help a single one. Nothing I saw on all those voyages hurt me more than their hands reaching for me.

We escaped the strait and approached Thrinacia. I could already hear the cattle from the ship. I remembered both warnings and ordered the helmsman to keep going.

Eurylochus faced me.

“You may be made of iron. We're not. We need sleep and a meal on shore. What if a storm catches us in the dark? Let us stop tonight and sail in the morning.”

The crew sided with him. I could not make them go on. I made each man swear that he would kill none of the Sun's animals. We would eat only the stores Circe had supplied.

They promised. We landed near fresh water, cooked supper and talked of the men Scylla had taken. We cried until sleep came.

That night a storm rose. In the morning we hauled the ship into a sheltered cave. I repeated the warning about the cattle. They promised again. But for a month the winds kept us on the island. Our grain and wine ran out. We caught fish and birds, and searched for anything we could eat.

One day I went inland to pray for a way off. Away from the men, sheltered from the wind, I prayed until the gods sent me to sleep.

Eurylochus spoke while I was gone.

“We can't starve beside all this food. Sacrifice the best cattle to the gods. If we get home, we'll build the Sun a rich temple. If he sinks the ship instead, I'd rather drown at once than die slowly here.”

The others agreed. They drove the cattle in, prayed and killed them. Without grain or wine, they used leaves and water for the offering. When I woke and walked back, I smelled meat roasting before I saw the shore.

I cried out to the gods who had sent that sleep. Then I went from man to man, demanding how they could have done it. But the animals were already dead.

Later Calypso told me what followed among the gods. Hermes had brought her the news. Lampetie reported the slaughter to her father. The Sun demanded payment for his cattle and threatened to leave the sky and shine among the dead. Zeus promised to strike our ship when it put to sea.

Even on the island we saw signs of what we had done. The hides moved, and meat on the spits made the lowing sounds of living cattle. Still, for six days the men ate from the herds. On the seventh, the wind eased and we sailed.

Once land had disappeared, a black cloud covered us. A squall broke the mast's supports. It fell onto the helmsman and killed him. Then Zeus's lightning struck. The ship spun, burning and breaking apart. My companions were thrown into the sea. None of them survived.

I held on until the hull fell away from the keel. The mast floated near it, with a length of strong hide rope still attached. I bound the two timbers together and climbed onto them.

The wind changed. All night it carried me back towards Charybdis. At sunrise, the whirlpool swallowed my makeshift raft. I caught the fig tree above it and hung there, with nowhere to rest my feet. I waited for the mast and keel to rise again.

At last they appeared. I let go, fell into the water beside them and hauled myself back on. I paddled with my hands. Zeus kept Scylla from seeing me as I went by.

For nine days I drifted. On the tenth night I reached Calypso's island. She took me in. What happened there, Alcinous, I told you and your queen yesterday.

I have brought the story back to that shore. I do not want to begin it all again.
