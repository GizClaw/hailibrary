# The Scar in the Firelight

The last of the suitors had gone. For the first time that evening, Odysseus could hear the fire.

He waited beside it in his borrowed rags while Telemachus watched the door. They had one task to finish before Penelope came downstairs. The helmets, shields, and spears hanging along the walls had to disappear.

“If anyone asks, tell them the smoke was spoiling the metal,” Odysseus said. “And tell them you don't want weapons within reach when the wine starts an argument.”

Telemachus called Euryclea and asked her to bolt the women in their room. When she asked who would carry the light, he said the stranger must earn his food. Then father and son carried the weapons into the storeroom. Athena walked before them with a golden lamp, and its light shone over the walls and roof beams.

“Look at the beams.”

Athena's light shone over the wood.

“Hush,” his father said. “Ask no questions. This is how the gods work. Go to bed now. Your mother will want to question me.”

When the last spear was put away, Odysseus sent his son to bed. He remained by the hearth. Soon he would speak to his wife, and he would have to endure her not knowing him.

Penelope came down with her attendants. They cleared the tables and brought fresh wood. Melantho again ordered the stranger out, threatening to drive him off with a burning stick. Odysseus warned her that wealth and favor could vanish. Her master might still return, and even if he died, his son was old enough to notice what the women did. Penelope rebuked the maid. She had expressly said she meant to question this guest. Eurynome brought him a chair with a fleece.

Odysseus sat opposite his wife, still concealed by his rags.

“You have been in this house all day,” she said. “Now tell me who you are.”

“Ask me something else. My home is a long way away, and talking about it only makes me sorry.”

“Then we have that much in common.” She glanced toward the empty tables. “Those men eat here because they want me to choose a husband. Every day I find another way to make them wait.”

She told him about the shroud she had begun for Laertes, Odysseus's elderly father. She had said she could not marry until the cloth was finished. In daylight the loom clicked, and everyone could see the weaving grow. At night she unwound the threads.

For three years she had kept that small piece of freedom. Then a servant had betrayed her. The suitors had caught her at the loom and made her finish.

“Now my son is old enough to see what they're taking from him,” she said. “My parents want me to decide. I have run out of ways to keep the house as it is.”

Odysseus kept his hands still beneath his cloak.

“But I asked about you,” she continued. “You must have had parents. A place where people knew your name.”

He called himself Aethon, son of Deucalion and younger brother of Idomeneus of Crete. Odysseus had arrived there on his way to Troy, he said, looking for Idomeneus, who had sailed already. Aethon had fed him and his men during twelve days of contrary winds. They had left on the thirteenth. He invented this life as she listened and wept. He pitied her, but kept his own eyes still and dry.

The lie had to stand on something she could test. He let her ask.

“What was he wearing?”

He saw her watching him more closely now.

“A purple cloak, folded double. A gold clasp held it. There was a little hound on the clasp, gripping a fawn. The work was so fine that the animals almost seemed to move.”

Penelope lowered her eyes.

“And his shirt?”

“Light and smooth. People looked twice at it.”

“Who was with him?”

“A man called Eurybates. A little older than he was, round-shouldered, dark, with curly hair. Your husband trusted him more than the others.”

She covered her face. She had folded that cloak. She had chosen the clasp. Across the narrow space between their chairs, Odysseus could hear her trying to control her breath.

She knew the clothes and clasp: she had given them to her husband herself. The stranger would be an honored guest now, she said, although she despaired of ever welcoming Odysseus home.

“At least you saw him,” she said at last.

“I have heard more recent news as well.”

He mixed things that had happened to him with invented reports. Odysseus's men had killed the Sun's cattle, he said, and Zeus had wrecked the ship, drowning every companion. The Phaeacians had rescued and enriched its sole survivor. He claimed Odysseus had delayed coming home to gather more wealth. King Pheidon of the Thesprotians had shown him that treasure and had a ship ready. Odysseus himself had gone to Dodona to ask Zeus's oak whether to return openly or in secret. He swore by Zeus and this hearth that her husband would return.

“Before this moon is gone and the new one comes,” he promised.

Penelope looked at him through wet lashes.

“I would reward news like that. But I have had too many nights to learn how easily a hope can keep someone awake.”

Still, she ordered a good bed prepared for the guest. He declined it. He was used to rough sleeping places, he said. When she offered to have a servant wash his feet, he would accept only an older woman, someone who knew what hardship did to a body.

Penelope called Euryclea, who had held Odysseus on the night he was born. The nurse wept for her missing master. He had honored the gods, yet had not been allowed home. Perhaps foreign women were mocking him as these women mocked the guest.

The old nurse brought a basin. Before she knelt, she studied the stranger's face.

“I have seen plenty of travelers,” she said. “None has looked so much like him.”

“Other people have told me that.”

He shifted his leg out of the firelight, but it was already too late to refuse her.

Her hands moved over his foot and ankle. Then her fingers stopped at the scar above his knee.

That scar came from a visit to his mother's family on Mount Parnassus. His grandfather Autolycus, skilled in theft and false oaths through Hermes's favor, had named the newborn Odysseus for his anger against many people. When the boy grew, he came for the gifts his grandfather had promised. On a hunt with his uncles, he followed the hounds with a spear. A boar rushed from thick cover, tore his leg above the knee without reaching the bone, and fell dead when Odysseus drove his spear through its shoulder. His uncles bound the wound and spoke a charm over the bleeding. Once he healed, the family sent him home with gifts, and he told his parents how he had been hurt.

His foot slipped from her hands. The basin struck the floor, spilling warm water around her knees.

Tears filled her eyes. She caught his beard. “You are Odysseus. I didn't know until I touched you.”

She looked across at Penelope, wanting to tell her. Athena kept the queen's attention elsewhere. Odysseus seized the nurse's throat with his right hand and pulled her close with his left.

“You nursed me at your breast. Do you mean to destroy me now I've come home after twenty years? Tell no one. If I kill the suitors and then kill the women, I won't spare you either, though you are my nurse, if you betray this.”

Euryclea answered that nothing could bend or break her silence. She would be firm as stone or iron. She offered to name the women who had behaved badly and those who were innocent. Odysseus refused. He could judge them himself. She must keep silent and leave the rest to the gods.

She fetched more water, washed him, and rubbed his feet with oil. He moved back toward the fire and covered the scar with his rags.

Penelope spoke of her sleepless nights and the choice that tormented her: stay with her son and guard the house, or leave with a suitor. Her grown son now urged her to marry, angry at the wasting of his property. Then she asked the stranger to explain a dream.

“I dreamed there were twenty geese feeding in my yard. I liked to watch them. Then an eagle came down and killed them.”

She paused, disturbed by the telling.

“In the dream I cried, and my women gathered round. But the eagle returned and spoke from a roof beam. It said this was a true vision: the geese were the suitors, and it was my husband, come to kill them. Then I woke and saw the geese still feeding safely.”

“There is your answer,” he said. “Your husband gave it himself. Every suitor will die.”

“Dreams pass through two gates,” she answered. “Those from ivory deceive us. Those from horn come true. I wish this one had come through horn, but I don't believe it did.”

The fire settled. Penelope sat without speaking for a while.

“Tomorrow I will bring out his bow.”

Odysseus waited.

“I'll set up twelve axes. The man who can string the bow and send an arrow through them will be the man I marry.” She touched the edge of her chair. “Then I will leave this house.”

He had held that bow so often that his fingers seemed to remember its weight.

“Let the contest happen,” he said. “Odysseus will return before any of those men can do what you ask.”

Penelope could have gone on talking, but she knew people needed sleep. Her attendants followed her upstairs to the bed she had so often wet with tears.

She wept again for her husband, until Athena closed her eyes in sleep.
