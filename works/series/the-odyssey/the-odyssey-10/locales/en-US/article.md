# The Bag and the Open Door

On Aeolus's island, Alcinous, I had a way home put into my hands. His island floated on the sea behind a wall of metal. His six sons were married to his six daughters, and all lived and feasted with their parents. For a month we stayed with his household, and he never tired of asking about Troy. When I asked to leave, he gave me something more useful than gold.

Zeus had placed the winds in his keeping. Aeolus shut the troublesome ones inside an oxhide bag, bound its mouth with silver cord and laid it aboard my ship. Only the west wind remained free to carry us towards Ithaca.

For nine days I kept the helm. On the tenth, land rose ahead. We could see fires burning at home. Then weariness overcame me, and I slept.

My companions looked at the closed bag. They thought Aeolus had filled it with treasure for me.

“He keeps getting gifts,” one said. “We've come the same distance. Why shouldn't we see what's in there?”

They undid the cord. The winds burst out, caught every sail and drove us away from the shore we had almost reached. I woke into their cries and the storm. For a moment I wanted to throw myself overboard. Instead I pulled my cloak over me and endured the voyage back.

Aeolus stared when I returned to his threshold. I told him what had happened and asked him to help once more.

“No,” he said. “If the gods have driven you back after the passage I gave you, I won't set myself against them. Leave my island.”

So we rowed. On the seventh day we reached the country of the Laestrygonians. Cliffs enclosed a harbor with a narrow mouth. The other captains led their ships inside, where the water lay smooth. I moored mine outside, near a rock, and sent three men to ask who lived there.

A girl fetching water directed them to her father, King Antiphates. His wife was huge enough to terrify them. She called the king. He seized one of the visitors and began to eat him. The other two ran for the ships.

The alarm brought giants onto the cliffs. Rocks crashed down into the harbor. Ships crushed against one another. The giants speared my men like fish and carried them home to eat. I cut our cable and shouted for the oars. We got beyond their reach. None of the ships inside came out.

We arrived at Aeaea with one vessel left. For two days we lay on the shore, too worn down to do much more than breathe. Then I climbed inland and saw smoke rising through thick trees. On my way back I killed a stag and brought it to the men. At least we could eat before facing another unknown house.

When I told them about the smoke, they remembered the Cyclops and the harbor. Still, we needed to learn where we were. I divided us into two parties. The lot fell to Eurylochus, who led twenty-two men into the woods.

They found Circe's stone house. Wolves and lions moved about it, but instead of attacking, the animals rubbed against them like dogs. Circe's magic had tamed them. From inside came a woman's singing and the sound of a loom.

Polites, one of the men I trusted most, urged the others to call to her. The door opened. She invited them in, and all but Eurylochus followed. He waited outside, uneasy.

She gave the men food and wine mixed with drugs meant to make them forget home. Then she touched them with her wand. Their heads and bodies became those of pigs, though their minds and memories remained human. She shut them in a sty and threw them the food pigs eat.

Eurylochus had not seen the change. He knew only that no one came back out. When he reached the ship, he could hardly get the words past his tears.

I took my weapons and asked him to guide me there. He gripped me with both hands.

“Don't make me go back. We can still save the people here. You'll disappear too.”

“Stay, then,” I told him. “I have to find them.”

Near the house a young man met me and took my hand. It was Hermes. He told me where my companions were and pulled a plant from the earth. Its root was black, its flower white. The gods called it moly.

“Keep this with you,” he said. “Her drink won't change you. When she strikes you with her wand, rush at her with your sword as though you mean to kill her. She'll offer you her bed. Don't refuse, if you want your men freed. But first make her swear by the gods not to harm you. Otherwise, once you're naked, she may rob you of your manhood.”

I went on alone. Circe welcomed me and brought her drink. I swallowed it. When the wand touched me, I was still a man.

“Off to the sty,” she ordered.

I drew my sword and sprang at her as if to kill her. She cried out and caught my knees. Hermes had once told her that Odysseus would come, she said. Now she knew me.

“Put the sword away. Come to my bed. We can learn to trust each other.”

“You've turned my men into pigs,” I answered. “Now you want me naked in your bed, where you could take away my manhood. Swear you won't harm me, or I won't lie with you.”

She took the oath. Only then did I put myself in her hands and share her bed. Afterwards her servants bathed me and brought clean clothing. Food was set before me, but I left it untouched.

“Do you still distrust me?” Circe asked. “I have sworn.”

“How could I eat while my companions are still trapped? Free them and let me see them with my own eyes. Then I can eat.”

She went to the sty and used another drug. The bristles fell away. My men stood before me again, younger and stronger in appearance than before. They recognized me, caught my hands and wept so loudly that Circe herself was moved.

She sent me to bring the rest from the ship. On the beach they crowded around me in relief. I told them to secure the vessel, hide our gear and come to the house where their friends were safe.

Eurylochus tried to stop them.

“He'll take us there to become animals too. Remember the cave. His stubbornness cost our companions their lives.”

He was my kinsman, but I considered drawing my sword and cutting off his head. The others intervened. Let him stay with the ship, they urged. I should take them to their friends. In the end Eurylochus came as well, frightened by my rebuke.

The two parties met in Circe's house and cried in each other's arms. She asked us to rest and eat until we had recovered. We were still carrying every disaster with us, she said.

Days passed into months. A year went by with food, wine and shelter. Then my men called me aside.

“Have you stopped thinking about Ithaca? If we're ever going back, we have to leave.”

That night I asked Circe to help us on our way. She did not try to keep us. But the voyage she described was one I had never imagined taking.

“First you must go to the dead,” she said. “Ask Tiresias how to get home. Persephone has left the prophet his understanding, even there.”

I sat up and wept. How could a living man sail to that place? Who would guide us?

“The north wind will carry your ship over Oceanus. Land by the groves at the edge of the dead, where the rivers meet. Make offerings there. The spirits will gather, but hold them back from the blood until Tiresias has answered you.”

By dawn she had told me what to do. I went through the house waking the men. Amid those preparations, Elpenor fell from the roof where he had slept after drinking. Roused by the noise, he had forgotten the stairway. He broke his neck and died. I would hear his account of it among the dead.

The others gathered, thinking we were finally sailing towards Ithaca. I had to tell them where we were going first. They fell to the ground in grief, then rose and followed me to the shore. Circe had brought the animals for the offering, passing among us unseen.

We loaded the ship for a voyage none of us had wanted to make.
