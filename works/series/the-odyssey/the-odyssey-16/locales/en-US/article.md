# A Father in the Doorway

The dogs heard Telemachus first. Instead of barking, they crowded around his legs. Inside the hut, Odysseus lifted his head from the breakfast fire.

“Someone you know is coming,” he told Eumaeus.

The young man appeared in the doorway. Eumaeus dropped the bowls he was holding and caught him in his arms. He kissed his head and his eyes, weeping so hard that he could scarcely speak. Odysseus remained beside the fire while another man welcomed his son home.

“I thought I would never see you again,” Eumaeus said at last.

Telemachus held him, then asked whether his mother was still in the house. She was, the swineherd assured him, still grieving, still waiting. He took the young man's spear. Odysseus began to rise from his place, but Telemachus waved him down.

“Stay there, stranger. There's room for me.”

Eumaeus spread brushwood and a sheepskin for him, brought bread and yesterday's meat, and mixed the wine. When they had eaten, Telemachus asked about the guest. Eumaeus repeated the Cretan story he had heard and put the stranger under his young master's protection.

Telemachus looked troubled. He could provide clothes, food, a sword, and sandals. He could pay for the guest to stay here until a journey could be arranged. But taking him to the palace was another matter. He was not strong enough to protect an old man from the crowd of suitors.

“Do your own people oppose you?” Odysseus asked. “Have you no brothers to stand beside you?”

“No brothers. My father was an only son, and so am I. The men in our house have come from all the neighboring islands. They are consuming everything while my mother cannot bring herself to choose one of them. Soon they may finish with me too.”

The stranger's anger showed through his disguise. If he were young again, or Odysseus's son, or Odysseus himself, he would go and destroy those men, even at the risk of having his head cut off. He would rather die fighting in his own hall than watch guests insulted, enslaved women dragged about indecently and the household's goods wasted every day. Telemachus could hear the force in him, but force alone would not solve the problem of numbers.

He turned to Eumaeus. His mother must learn that he was safe. The swineherd should speak to her privately and come straight back. Eumaeus asked about Laertes, who had been wasting away since his grandson left. Telemachus wanted him told as well, but without delaying this errand. Penelope could send a trusted woman to the farm.

Eumaeus fastened his sandals and left. Once he was well away, Athena stood outside the hut. Telemachus could not see her. Odysseus could, and the dogs shrank back, whining. She beckoned him beyond the yard.

“Tell your son,” she said. “Plan together to kill the suitors, then go to town. I'll join you in the fighting.”

Her wand touched him. The worn clothes became a clean tunic and cloak. His cheeks filled, his skin regained its color, and his beard darkened. He went back into the hut looking like the man who had left Ithaca.

Telemachus turned his eyes away. “Are you a god? A moment ago you were old. Don't harm me.”

“I am your father.”

Odysseus kissed him. The tears he had held back fell to the floor. His son stared, then drew away from the hope being offered.

“No man can change like that. Some god is making me believe what I want, so it will hurt more afterward.”

Odysseus sat down where the beggar had been sitting. “Athena changed my appearance. There is no other Odysseus coming after me. I have been away for twenty years, and this is how I have come home.”

Telemachus put his arms around him. Both cried aloud. The father who had left an infant and the son who knew him through other people's stories held each other while the morning passed outside.

At last Telemachus wanted to know how he had reached the island. Odysseus told him about the Phaeacian ship, the sleep that had carried him across the sea, and the gifts now hidden in a cave. Athena had sent him here so father and son could plan to kill the suitors.

“How many?” he asked.

Telemachus counted fifty-two from Dulichium, twenty-four from Same, twenty from Zacynthus and twelve from Ithaca: a hundred and eight suitors. The Dulichians had six servants, and there were Medon, a singer and two carvers as well. He knew the stories of his father's courage and cleverness. But two men against so many was not a battle he could imagine winning.

“We need help.”

“Will Athena and Zeus be enough?”

Telemachus looked at him. Those were powerful allies, though far above the house where the danger waited.

Odysseus began with what they themselves must do. The son would return to town the next morning. Eumaeus would bring the father later, once more disguised as a beggar. Whatever happened in the hall, Telemachus must keep his anger hidden. If they mocked his father, threw things at him, or dragged him away, he must not rush to defend him and expose them both.

When Athena prompted him, Odysseus would nod. At that signal, Telemachus must remove the weapons from the hall and lock them in the storeroom. If anyone asked, he could blame the smoke that blackened them, or say he feared a drunken quarrel. He must leave a sword and spear for each of them and two oxhide shields where they could seize them.

No one else must know that Odysseus had returned. Not Eumaeus, not Laertes, not even Penelope. First they needed to discover which servants could be trusted.

Telemachus listened, then challenged that last plan. Going round every farm to test the men would take too long. The suitors would keep eating their way through the estate meanwhile. They could test the women in the house now, he said, and leave the men until later, if Zeus truly supported them. Odysseus had found a son who could keep a secret and also question his judgment.

Meanwhile, the ship had reached town. Its crew sent a messenger to Penelope, and Eumaeus arrived on the same errand. The messenger announced the good news before the waiting women. Eumaeus gave the private message as instructed and left at once.

The suitors heard that their prey had come home. Before they could send word to the men lying in ambush, the waiting ship itself entered the harbor. Antinous came ashore furious. They had watched the headlands by day and stayed aboard through the night, yet Telemachus had slipped past them.

He proposed killing the young man on his farm or the road before he could call an assembly and expose their plot. The people might otherwise drive the conspirators into exile. After the murder they could divide his property, leaving the house to Penelope and whichever man married her. If they would not kill him, Antinous said, they must stop consuming his goods and court her from their own homes. Amphinomus objected to killing a king's son before asking the gods. If Zeus approved, he would kill Telemachus himself and urge the others on; if not, they must stop. The others accepted this and went back to the palace.

Penelope had learned of the plot from Medon. She came down with her attendants, stood by a pillar, and faced Antinous.

“Your father once joined Taphian pirates in raiding the Thesprotians, who were at peace with us. Our people wanted to kill him and take his wealth. He fled here, and Odysseus protected him. Is this your repayment? You eat his wealth, pursue his wife, and plan to kill his son. Stop it. Make the others stop.”

Eurymachus answered before Antinous could. Telemachus had nothing to fear from the suitors, he promised. He remembered sitting on Odysseus's knee and being fed from his hand. He would defend the son of that house with his own spear, although no one could save him if death came from the gods. He spoke warmly while keeping his part in the murder plot concealed.

Penelope went upstairs again. Grief and fear followed her, until Athena brought sleep.

At the hut, father and son prepared supper together. Before Eumaeus returned, Athena restored the stranger's wrinkles and ragged clothes. The swineherd came in to find the same two people he had left that morning.

Telemachus asked whether the ambush party had returned. Eumaeus had not stopped to find out. From the hill above town, however, he had seen a ship full of men with shields and spears. He thought it might be theirs, but could not be certain.

Telemachus gave his father a small smile where Eumaeus could not see it. They finished their meal and lay down to sleep.

For Eumaeus, the old stranger was still a guest. Beside the fire, Telemachus now knew his father.
