# The Name Across the Water

King Alcinous, the man your singer has been naming is sitting beside you. I am Odysseus, son of Laertes. My home is Ithaca. You would see a rough island, with woods climbing its mountain. I would see the place I have been trying to reach ever since we sailed from Troy.

Calypso offered me a home. Circe would have kept me too. Neither could make me forget my own. To tell you how I came here alone, I must begin when I still had twelve ships.

We first landed at Ismarus, a city of the Cicons. We attacked it, killed men, carried women away and took its wealth. I divided the plunder, then ordered everyone aboard. But my men stayed on the beach, drinking the stolen wine and roasting cattle. While they feasted, people who had escaped our attack called for help inland.

The reinforcements came in the morning. We fought by our ships until the day's light began to fail. Six men from each vessel died before the rest of us got away. We called our dead companions' names across the water and sailed on.

Then a storm tore our sails. After sheltering on land, we tried again, but wind and current swept us past the route home. For nine days we were driven over the sea. On the tenth we reached the Lotus-eaters.

They did not attack the three men I sent to meet them. They shared their food. That was enough. After eating the lotus, my scouts no longer cared whether they saw Ithaca again. They wanted to stay and eat more. I dragged them back, weeping, and tied them beneath the rowing benches. I hurried the others aboard before anyone else tasted it.

We came next to an island full of wild goats, near the country of the Cyclopes. There was meat and fresh water, and our ships could rest safely. Across the channel we saw smoke. We heard sheep and voices. I wanted to know who lived there.

Leaving the other ships, I crossed with my own and took twelve men ashore. I carried a skin of strong, sweet wine. Maron, a priest of Apollo, had given it to me at Ismarus, where I had spared him, his wife and their child.

We found a cave with cheeses on racks, young animals in pens and bowls ready for milking. Its owner was out with the flock.

“Take some cheese,” my men urged. “We can drive the animals to the ship and leave.”

I wanted to meet the owner. I expected a guest's welcome, perhaps a gift. So we ate his cheese and waited in his house.

He came back carrying a load of wood and threw it down with a crash. He was enormous, with one eye in his face. We shrank into the shadows while he brought in the animals he meant to milk. Then he rolled a boulder across the entrance. Even before he saw us, I knew we could never move it.

He finished his work and lit the fire. Its light reached us.

“Who are you?” he demanded. “And what brought you into my cave?”

I told him we were returning from Troy and had been driven off course. We had fought under Agamemnon, I said, the king whose army had destroyed that city. I asked for help, and reminded him that Zeus protected strangers asking for shelter.

“Zeus doesn't decide whom I spare,” he said. “Where have you left your ship?”

I told him Poseidon had wrecked it. The lie kept the men waiting on shore out of his reach.

He seized two of my companions, smashed them against the ground and tore their bodies apart to eat them. We cried and raised our hands to the gods, unable to stop him. When he slept, my hand went to my sword. Then I looked at the stone. Kill him, and we would be locked inside with his body.

At dawn he ate two more men, rolled the boulder aside and drove his flock out. He shut us in again as easily as a man closing a box.

We had to make him open that door while he was still alive. Near the pens lay a length of olive wood, large enough to serve him as a staff. We made a stake from it and hid it. Four men were chosen by lot to help me. We spent the day waiting for his return.

That evening he brought all the sheep inside. He ate two more companions. Six of the twelve I had led into that cave were gone.

I carried him a bowl of Maron's wine.

“I brought this hoping you'd help us on our way,” I said. “Try it.”

He drank, held out the bowl and asked for more. After the third bowl his words grew heavy.

“Tell me your name. I'll give you a guest's gift.”

“My name is Nobody. That's what my parents and my friends call me.”

“Then Nobody can wait until I've eaten the others. That's his gift.”

He toppled backwards and vomited wine and human flesh before sinking into sleep. We brought the stake from its hiding place, heated its point and drove it into his eye. His cry filled the cave. We let go and scattered as he tore it free.

The neighboring Cyclopes heard him. They came to the entrance and called, “Polyphemus! Who's hurting you?”

“Nobody!” he shouted. “Nobody is killing me by trickery!”

If nobody was attacking him, they answered, it must be an illness sent by a god. He should pray to his father, Poseidon. Then they went away.

For a moment I almost laughed. My false name had kept his neighbors away. But Polyphemus still held the only way out. He moved the boulder himself and sat in the doorway, reaching for anyone who tried to pass.

The sheep gave me another thought. I bound them in groups of three and fastened a man beneath the middle animal, hidden by the others. For myself I chose the biggest ram and clung to the wool beneath its belly. We waited until morning.

As the flock pushed outside, Polyphemus felt along their backs. His hands passed above us. My ram came last, moving slowly under my weight. He stopped it.

“Why are you hanging back? You're usually first. Are you sorry for me? If you could speak, you'd tell me where Nobody is hiding. I'd smash his head against the floor, and that would repay some of what he's done to me.”

I held on. At last his hand lifted, and the ram carried me out.

Beyond the yard I crawled free and untied my men. We drove the sheep down to the ship. The sailors were glad to see us, then began to cry for those who were missing. I waved them quiet. We could grieve when the oars had taken us away.

But once we had water between us and the cave, I was the one who broke the silence.

“You thought you could eat your guests and escape punishment!” I shouted.

Polyphemus tore loose a great rock and hurled it towards my voice. It fell close behind the ship. The wave drove us back towards shore, and I had to push us off with a pole while the men rowed desperately.

When we were farther out, they begged me to say nothing more.

“He can still hear you. The next rock will break the ship.”

I had saved us by hiding my name. Now I could not bear to leave him without it.

“If anyone asks who blinded you,” I called, “say Odysseus did it. Son of Laertes. From Ithaca!”

He groaned. A seer had warned him that Odysseus would take his sight. He had expected a great warrior to overpower him, not a small man to make him drunk. Now he offered hospitality and his father's favor if I would return.

I answered with another threat. I wished I could send him to the dead as surely as I knew that even Poseidon would not restore that eye. He raised his hands towards the sky.

“Poseidon, if I am your son, keep Odysseus from his home. If he must reach it, let him come late, alone and in another man's ship. Let trouble wait inside his house.”

Poseidon heard. Another rock struck the water, this time driving us onward. We reached the island where the other ships waited and shared out the sheep. I offered the great ram to Zeus, but the god did not accept my plea. He was already considering how to destroy my ships and companions.

Next morning we put out again. The men who had escaped took their places at the oars, and the sea opened before the twelve ships.
