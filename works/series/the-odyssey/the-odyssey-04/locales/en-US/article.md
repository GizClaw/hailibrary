# News Across the Water

Telemachus and Pisistratus reached Menelaus's house during the celebration of two marriages. His daughter Hermione was being sent to Achilles' son, and his son Megapenthes was marrying a Spartan bride. A servant asked whether to receive the strangers or send them elsewhere.

Menelaus angrily rebuked him. “How many doors were opened to us on our way home? Bring them in.”

The horses were fed, the chariot put aside, and the young men taken to wash. Clean clothes waited for them. In the hall, bronze, silver, ivory and gold shone on every side. The king offered them food before asking their names. After eating, Telemachus leaned towards Pisistratus and whispered that the gods must live in a place like this.

Menelaus heard.

“Not even this house belongs beside theirs.”

He had spent nearly eight years coming home, gathering treasure in distant countries while his brother was being murdered. He would have given much of it away to recover the men who had died at Troy. There was one he missed more than all the others: Odysseus, who had worked so hard for them and vanished without a trace.

Telemachus wept and lifted his cloak to hide his face.

Helen entered and looked at him. She asked Menelaus whether this could be Odysseus's son. The hands, the head, the eyes were familiar. Pisistratus answered for his companion, explaining that Nestor had sent them in search of news.

Menelaus had once imagined bringing Odysseus, his people and possessions to Argos. He would have sacked one of his subject cities to make a new home for his friend. They would have lived near each other until death. The gods had denied them that happiness.

Soon they were all weeping. Pisistratus thought of Antilochus, the brother he had never known. At last he asked gently whether they might finish eating. There would be time tomorrow to mourn. Menelaus agreed, and the meal began again.

Helen mixed a magical Egyptian drug into the wine. For the rest of the day, a drinker could not weep, even at the sight of a parent dying or a child cut to pieces. Then she told the visitors how Odysseus had wounded himself and dressed in rags to enter Troy as a beggar. She alone recognized him. She washed him and found clothes for him, but he would not speak of his purpose until she swore not to betray him before he escaped. Then she heard the Greek plans. He escaped with information after killing many Trojans.

“By then I wanted my home, my daughter and my husband again,” Helen said. “I regretted that Aphrodite had brought me away. The Trojan women mourned. I was glad he had got away.”

Menelaus remembered another occasion.

“And at the wooden horse, you nearly brought us out of hiding.”

He described the warriors hidden inside the horse to destroy Troy. Helen had walked around it with Deiphobus, calling them by name in the voices of their wives. Menelaus and Diomedes wanted to answer or leap out, but Odysseus restrained them. Anticlus began to speak; Odysseus covered his mouth and held it shut until Athena drew Helen away.

Telemachus lamented that such courage had not saved his father.

“May we sleep?” he asked at last. “I would like to speak again in the morning.”

They slept in the gatehouse. At dawn Menelaus came to sit with him, and Telemachus told him about the suitors. The king was angry. They were like young deer in a lion's den, he said: Odysseus would return and kill them. Then he promised to repeat what Proteus had told him.

“I heard something about your father from Proteus,” he said. “Listen to how I came to question him.”

On the way back, Menelaus had been trapped for twenty days on Pharos, off Egypt. No useful wind came. The supplies dwindled, and his men wandered along the shore trying to catch fish. One day a goddess found him alone. She was Idothea, daughter of the sea god Proteus.

“Are you going to remain here until there is nothing left to eat?” she asked.

“Show me how to leave. I would have gone long ago if I knew.”

She told him that her father knew what kept the ships there. At noon Proteus came ashore among his seals and slept. Menelaus must bring three strong men, catch the god, and refuse to release him through all the shapes he tried.

The next morning she hid the four men under seal skins. The smell was almost enough to drive them out before the trap was set. Idothea put fragrant ambrosia beneath their noses, and they lay still while the seals gathered. Proteus came, counted them among his animals, and slept.

Menelaus and his companions seized him. A lion struggled in their hands, then other fierce creatures. Their grip seemed to close on running water, then on the trunk of a tree. They held through each change until the old man returned to his own shape.

“What do you want from me?” he demanded.

“A way home.”

Proteus told Menelaus to return to Egypt and make the offerings he had failed to complete. Only then would the voyage succeed. The king hated the thought of going back, but accepted it. Before letting the god leave, he asked about the other commanders.

Ajax had survived a wreck, then boasted that the gods could not drown him. Poseidon split the rock beneath him, and he drowned. Agamemnon had kissed his native soil with joy, but Aegisthus's paid lookout had watched for his return for a year. Aegisthus hid twenty men beside a feast and lured him there. They butchered the king; all the followers of Agamemnon and Aegisthus died in the fighting too.

Menelaus had fallen weeping on the sand when he heard that. Proteus urged him to hurry home. Aegisthus might still be alive; if Orestes had already killed him, Menelaus might at least arrive for the funeral.

But there was a third man, alive and unable to return. Menelaus insisted on hearing his name.

“Odysseus,” Proteus answered. “Calypso keeps him on her island. He grieves there, with no ship and no crew to bring him home.”

Now Telemachus had the testimony of a god who had seen his father alive, grieving in Calypso's captivity.

Menelaus finished the account. Proteus had also foretold the king's final refuge in Elysium, because Helen was Zeus's daughter. Then the sea god vanished. Menelaus returned to Egypt, made the offerings and raised a memorial to Agamemnon. Afterwards a fair wind carried him home.

He wanted Telemachus to stay longer, and offered a chariot, three horses, and a cup as farewell gifts. The young man thanked him but asked to return to the crew waiting at Pylos.

“The horses would be wasted on Ithaca,” he said. “Our paths suit goats better. May I take something I can carry instead?”

Menelaus smiled and agreed on a silver bowl edged with gold, made by Hephaestus and given to him by the king of Sidon. More guests were arriving. The house filled with preparations for another meal.

On Ithaca, Noemon was looking for his ship. He asked Antinous when Telemachus would bring it back from Pylos. He needed it for a journey of his own.

“He actually sailed?”

Noemon had lent the ship willingly. The young men aboard were among the best in Ithaca, and Mentor had gone with them. Or someone who looked like Mentor. That was strange, because Noemon had also seen the old man in town afterwards.

When the shipowner left, Antinous and Eurymachus angrily gathered the others. They had assumed the boy was out on a farm. Instead he had found men and a vessel without them, and might cause trouble before he was grown.

“We'll meet him on the way back,” Antinous said. “I need a ship and twenty men.”

They would wait in the passage near Ithaca and kill Telemachus before he reached home. The others approved. They did not know that Medon, a household servant, had overheard them.

Medon went upstairs to Penelope. She thought he had come with another demand from the feast. Before she could finish her complaint, he told her what the men intended.

For a moment she could not speak. Her son had sailed away. Her son was to be murdered. The two pieces of news arrived together.

“Why did he leave me? Does he mean to die with no one left to preserve his name?”

Medon could not explain whether a god had urged him or the wish was his own. After he left, Penelope sank to the floor among her women. She blamed them for not telling her: she would have stopped the voyage or died rather than let him go. She wanted someone to fetch Dolius, her gardener, and send him to Laertes. Perhaps the old man could gather help.

Euryclea came forward.

“I knew. I gave him the stores.”

“Kill me or let me live here, as you choose, but hear the truth. He made me swear to wait ten or twelve days, unless you asked. He didn't want your tears to spoil your beauty. Don't add this to Laertes's grief. Wash, change your clothes, and pray to Athena. She can save him even from death.”

Penelope did as she advised. She asked the goddess to remember Odysseus's offerings and protect their son. Below, the suitors guessed that she was preparing for marriage. Antinous ordered them to stop talking so loudly and ready the ship. Armed men assembled by the sea and waited for dark.

Penelope lay upstairs without eating. She kept imagining the unfamiliar coasts her son must cross, the strangers he must meet, the men already waiting to harm him. Eventually she slept.

Athena sent a dream in the shape of her sister Iphthime. The figure came into the room and told her that the gods would bring her son back.

“He has never faced such things,” Penelope said. “He is alone among people he doesn't know.”

“Athena is with him. She sent me to you.”

Penelope reached beyond that comfort towards the question she had carried for years.

“And my husband? Is he alive?”

The dream gave no answer about Odysseus. It vanished, and Penelope woke steadier, still without the news her son had heard in Sparta.

Out at sea, the suitors brought their ship into shelter beside the rocky island of Asteris. There they waited for the returning sail.
