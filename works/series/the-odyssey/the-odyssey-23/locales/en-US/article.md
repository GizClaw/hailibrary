# The Bed That Could Not Be Moved

Euryclea hurried upstairs, laughing. Joy had put new strength into her old knees. She bent over Penelope's bed and called her awake.

“Your husband is home! The suitors are dead. Come and see him.”

Penelope had not slept so deeply since he left for Troy. Now the nurse had woken her with something too cruel to believe. The gods must have robbed the sensible old woman of her mind. Anyone younger would have earned a fierce scolding for such a story.

But Euryclea insisted. The abused stranger was Odysseus. Telemachus had known and kept his father's secret until revenge was possible.

Penelope sprang up and embraced the nurse, crying with joy. Then doubt caught her again. How could one man have overcome so many?

“I didn't see the fighting,” Euryclea said. “We were shut in our room, huddled together, hearing the cries. Your son fetched me afterward. His father stood among the bodies, bloody as a lion. You would have been glad to see him. The dead have been carried to the gatehouse. He's cleansing the house with sulfur and wants you to come. Wife and son are alive, and he has punished his enemies at home.”

Perhaps a god had killed them, Penelope answered. Their conduct deserved divine anger. Odysseus himself was dead far away. She could not yet let herself believe otherwise.

The nurse offered the scar as proof. She had felt it while washing his feet and had been prevented from telling anyone. If the story was false, Penelope could put her to the cruelest death she could devise.

“No one understands everything the gods intend,” Penelope said. “But I'll go down. I will see my son, the dead men, and whoever killed them.”

On the stairs she could not decide whether to question this man from a distance or run into his arms. Once below, she sat across the fire from him. Odysseus looked down and waited. Sometimes she searched his face; sometimes the filthy clothes made him strange again.

Telemachus could bear it no longer. What wife would keep away from a husband returned after twenty years? Her heart was stone.

“My son, astonishment has left me unable to speak,” she answered. “If this is really your father, we shall know each other. There are signs that belong to us and no one else.”

Odysseus smiled. Let her test him, he told their son. She would decide soon enough. They had another danger to consider first. Even a man who killed one enemy might have to flee his country. They had killed the leading young men of Ithaca. Their relatives would seek revenge.

Telemachus left the plan to his father. They would follow him as long as their strength lasted.

“Wash and dress,” Odysseus said. “Have the women dress too. Let Phemius play for dancing. Anyone in the street will think a wedding is being held. We must reach the woods on my land before news of the deaths gets out. There we'll decide what the gods allow us to do.”

Soon music and footsteps filled the house. Outside, people heard and blamed the queen for remarrying instead of guarding her absent husband's property. They knew nothing of what had happened within.

Eurynome bathed Odysseus, rubbed him with oil, and brought clean clothes. Athena made him taller and stronger, with thick curling hair, giving his head and shoulders the beauty of worked silver brightened with gold. He returned to his chair opposite Penelope.

“Twenty years, and still you won't come near me,” he said. “Nurse, make me a bed. I'll sleep alone. Her heart is iron.”

Penelope answered evenly. She remembered how he looked when he sailed. She was neither scorning him nor boasting over him. Then she turned to Euryclea.

“Bring his bed out of the chamber he built himself. Lay the blankets and fleeces on it outside.”

Odysseus flared with anger.

“Who moved my bed? No man could do that without cutting it loose. I built it myself. An olive tree grew inside the court, thick as a pillar. I built stone walls and a roof around it, fitted the doors, cut the branches away, and shaped the standing trunk into a bedpost. I bored the wood and built the rest of the bed from it, inlaid it with gold and silver, and stretched red leather across it. Is it still there? Has someone cut the tree at its roots?”

Penelope could hold back no longer. She ran to him, threw her arms around his neck, and kissed him through her tears.

“Don't be angry. The gods kept us from being young together and growing old together. I've feared someone would come with a story and deceive me. Think of Helen. Had she known the Greeks would follow and bring her back, she would not have yielded to a foreign man. A god drove her into that wrong, and our suffering followed. But you know our bed. Only you, I, and the maid my father sent with me have seen it. Now I cannot doubt.”

Odysseus broke into tears too and held his wife. To Penelope, he was like land reached after a shipwreck, when the swimmer at last feels solid earth beneath exhausted feet. She could not loosen her arms.

Athena held back the night. Dawn was not yet allowed to rise from the Ocean and harness her horses.

At last Odysseus spoke of something still beyond their reunion. Their troubles had not ended. In Hades, the prophet Teiresias had laid another long journey before him. For now, he wanted them to go to bed.

Penelope asked to hear it. If she must know eventually, why not now?

He told her reluctantly. He must carry an oar inland until he met people who knew nothing of the sea and did not put salt in their food. A traveler would mistake the oar on his shoulder for a winnowing shovel. There he must plant it in the ground and sacrifice a ram, a bull, and a boar to Poseidon. Then he must come home and offer sacrifices to the gods in turn. The prophet had promised a gentle death from the sea when he was old and at peace, with his people happy around him.

“If the gods give you peace in old age,” Penelope said, “then perhaps there will be rest from suffering.”

That journey had not yet been made. Euryclea and Eurynome prepared the bed by torchlight. The nurse went to her own rest; Eurynome led the couple to their room and withdrew. Husband and wife returned joyfully to the love of their marriage bed. In the hall, Telemachus and the herdsmen stopped the dancing, sent the women to rest, and lay down themselves.

After making love, the couple spoke of the years apart. Penelope told him what it had meant to watch the suitors fill their house, slaughter the animals, and empty the wine jars. Odysseus told both the suffering he had endured and the suffering he had caused. She listened without sleeping, wanting all of it.

He began with the raid on the Cicones and the land of the Lotus-eaters. He told her how the Cyclops ate his companions and how he blinded him; how Aeolus welcomed him and helped him sail, only for a storm to carry him away from home again. At the Laestrygonian city, all his ships but one had been destroyed with their crews.

He spoke of Circe's spells, then the cold house of Hades, where he consulted Teiresias and met old comrades and his mother. He told of the Sirens' song, the perilous rocks, Charybdis, and Scylla. His men killed the Sun's cattle; Zeus struck their ship, and every companion died. Alone he reached Calypso. She kept him on her island and offered marriage and an ageless life, but could not win his consent. After further hardship he reached the Phaeacians, who honored him, gave him gold, bronze, and clothes, and sent him home by ship.

There his story ended. Sleep finally took him and eased his grief.

When Athena judged that he had had time with his wife and time to rest, she released Dawn. Odysseus rose from their bed and told Penelope to care for the property still in the house. As for the lost herds, he would seize livestock from other people and compel the Achaeans to supply the rest until his yards were full again. Now he would visit his father in the wooded country.

“Word of the killings will spread at sunrise. Stay upstairs with your women. Receive no one and ask no questions.”

He put on his armor and woke Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius. They armed themselves, opened the gates, and followed him out.

Although daylight had come, Athena hid the four in darkness and led them quickly from the town.
