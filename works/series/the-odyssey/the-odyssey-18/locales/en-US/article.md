# Firelight in the Hall

Another beggar arrived before Odysseus could speak with Penelope. He was called Irus, an errand runner about town, large in body and accustomed to finding food at the palace. He saw the stranger on the threshold and ordered him away.

“Move, old man. They want me to throw you out.”

Odysseus looked up. “There's room for both of us. I'm not taking anything that belongs to you. Leave me alone, or old as I am, I'll bloody your mouth and chest.”

Irus threatened to knock his teeth out. The quarrel drew Antinous, who laughed and called the others over. Here was an entertainment for the evening: let the beggars fight. The winner could choose a rich portion from the supper cooking at the fire, eat at their table, and have the doorway to himself. No other beggar would be admitted.

Odysseus pretended reluctance. An old man could hardly beat someone younger, but hunger drove him to try. First, however, the suitors must swear not to strike him while he fought. They gave their word. Telemachus added his own protection as the host of the house.

Odysseus tied his rags about his waist. Broad shoulders and strong thighs appeared beneath them, and Athena gave his limbs still greater power. The laughter changed. The onlookers began to doubt Irus's chances. Irus heard them and trembled. Servants tied up his clothes by force and pushed him forward. Antinous threatened to ship him to King Echetus if he lost. That king would cut off his nose, ears, and genitals, and feed the genitals to the dogs.

Odysseus considered killing him, then chose to hold back. A killing blow might expose him. Irus struck his right shoulder; Odysseus answered with one blow below the ear that broke bone. Blood poured from Irus's mouth. He fell in the dust, groaning and kicking, unable to get up. The suitors roared with laughter. Odysseus dragged him by a foot to the gate, propped him against the wall, and put a staff in his hands.

“Stay here and keep the dogs and pigs away. Try to rule over the other beggars again, and you'll suffer worse.”

He returned to the threshold with his bag. The suitors praised him and said they would send Irus to Echetus. Antinous supplied the promised meat. Amphinomus brought two loaves and a cup of wine, wishing that better days might follow the stranger's troubles.

Odysseus accepted the food, but his answer was a warning. He too had once trusted his wealth and strength, he said, and had behaved badly in that confidence. A man did not know how quickly his circumstances could change. The suitors were consuming an absent man's property and dishonoring his wife. That man would return soon.

“May you get home before he does,” he told Amphinomus. “I would not have you meet him here. This won't end without blood.”

He poured an offering, drank, and handed back the cup. Amphinomus lowered his head. The words troubled him, yet he went back to his place among the others. Athena had already doomed him to die by Telemachus's hand.

Upstairs, Athena prompted Penelope to appear before the suitors. The queen told Eurynome she wanted to speak to her son. He must not trust men who concealed harm under pleasant words. Eurynome urged her to wash and put aside the marks of weeping before she went down.

Penelope would not prepare herself as though for a celebration. Her husband had taken her happiness with him when he sailed. She asked only for two women to accompany her. While Eurynome went to fetch them, Athena laid the queen in a gentle sleep and renewed her beauty and commanding presence.

The women's voices woke her. Penelope wished Artemis would let her die as gently as she had slept, ending her grief for her husband. Then she rose and went down between her attendants, holding her veil before her face. The suitors watched her from their seats, each longing to have her in his bed.

She addressed her son instead. “You are grown now. Why do you let a stranger be treated this way in our house? What if he had been badly hurt?”

Telemachus acknowledged her anger. He understood right and wrong, but these men pressed him on every side and he had no one beside him. Their sport had not turned out as they intended, however. The stranger had won. He wished Zeus, Athena, and Apollo would break every suitor's neck, leaving them as limp as Irus now lay at the gate.

Eurymachus interrupted with praise of her beauty and wisdom. If more men could see her, he said, still more suitors would come. Penelope returned him to the cause of her grief. Before leaving, Odysseus had taken her hand and warned that not everyone would come back from Troy. She must care for his parents. When their son was grown, she might marry whom she chose and leave this house.

Now the boy had become a man. A marriage she dreaded was drawing closer. But these suitors, she added, did not court her as they should. Men who sought a wife ought to bring gifts and food for her household. These men consumed what was already hers.

Odysseus listened from his low place. He was glad to hear her turn their desire into gifts, without meaning the encouragement they chose to hear.

Antinous said she should accept whatever was offered, but they would not leave until she chose a husband. Servants hurried out and returned with presents. There was an embroidered dress fastened with twelve gold pins, a chain of gold and amber, earrings, and other fine ornaments. Penelope withdrew upstairs, her attendants carrying the gifts after her.

The men resumed their music and dancing. When darkness came, three braziers were brought in. Dry wood burned, and the women took turns holding torches. Odysseus offered to tend the lights so they could go to the queen, talk with her, or work the wool. He could stay awake as long as the company wished.

Some of the women laughed. Melantho mocked him openly. Penelope had raised her and given her toys as a child, but Melantho showed little care for the queen's sorrow now. Eurymachus was her lover, and she spoke from among his friends.

“Has beating Irus made you think you belong here? Go and sleep somewhere else. A stronger man may yet throw you out.”

Odysseus turned on her. “I'll tell Telemachus what you've said. He'll have you torn limb from limb.”

The threat frightened the women. They left the hall, believing he might carry it out. Odysseus stood by the braziers with the torches, watching the men whose shadows moved across his walls.

Athena kept their insolence alive. Eurymachus began to mock the stranger's bald head, saying the room was lit by its shine rather than the flames. The others laughed. Then he offered the beggar work building stone walls and planting trees. There would be food and clothes, he said, but of course a man who preferred begging would refuse.

“Give us each a scythe,” Odysseus answered. “Let us mow all day and see who tires first. Or put us behind matched oxen and compare our furrows. Give me arms in battle, and you'll see whether I can stand in front. You think yourself great among the people gathered here. If Odysseus returns, those wide doors may seem very narrow when you try to get out.”

Eurymachus seized a stool. Odysseus moved down beside Amphinomus's knees as it flew. It missed him and struck the cupbearer's right hand. The man fell backward with a cry, and his wine jug rang against the floor.

The hall erupted. Men blamed the stranger for ruining their pleasure. Telemachus stepped forward and told them they had eaten enough. If they could not hold their wine without making a disturbance, it was time to go home and sleep. They bit their lips at his boldness.

Amphinomus supported him. No one should attack the stranger or any servant. Let them make their offerings, finish their wine, and leave the guest in the care of the house to which he had come.

A servant mixed the final bowl. The men poured their offerings and drank, then went away to their own homes.

The torches were still burning when the noise of the feast left the hall.
