# A Voice in the Hall

The war at Troy was over. Those who had survived battle and shipwreck had reached home, but Odysseus still longed for his wife and his island from Calypso's cave. The goddess wanted him for her husband and would not release him. His companions were gone; their own reckless eating of the Sun god's cattle had cost them their return.

On Olympus, with Poseidon away at a feast, Zeus spoke of another homecoming. Aegisthus had taken Agamemnon's wife and murdered him, although Hermes had warned that his son Orestes would avenge him. Now Orestes had killed the murderer.

“Men blame us for suffering they bring upon themselves,” Zeus said.

Athena accepted that judgment, but asked about Odysseus. He wanted only to see smoke rising from his own country. Had Zeus forgotten all his offerings at Troy?

“I have not forgotten him,” Zeus replied. “Poseidon is angry because Odysseus blinded Polyphemus, the sea god's son. He keeps him wandering, though he does not kill him. Let us agree on his return. Poseidon cannot resist us all.”

Athena proposed sending Hermes to tell Calypso. She would go to Ithaca, encourage Odysseus's son, and send him to seek news at Pylos and Sparta.

Carrying a bronze spear, she appeared at the palace gate as Mentes, chief of the Taphians. Penelope's suitors sat on the hides of cattle they had eaten, playing draughts while servants prepared another feast. Telemachus sat among them, imagining his father driving them out. He saw the stranger waiting and hurried to welcome him.

“Come in and eat. Afterward you can tell us your business.”

He put the spear in the rack beside his father's weapons and seated his guest away from the noise. Water, bread, meat and wine were brought. When the suitors had eaten, they made Phemius sing to them. Under the sound of his lyre, Telemachus spoke quietly to the visitor.

“They enjoy music cheaply, at a missing man's expense. If my father returned, they would wish for faster legs rather than more money. But I no longer believe the stories that he is coming. Tell me who you are. Did you know him?”

Athena called herself Mentes, a trading captain carrying iron abroad and hoping to bring copper back. Their fathers had been friends, she said; old Laertes could confirm it, though he now lived grieving in the country. She had heard that Odysseus was home. Finding him absent, she believed a god or captors must be holding him on some island.

“He is alive. I am no prophet, but I believe he will find his way back, even from iron chains. You have his head and eyes. Are you truly his son?”

“My mother says so. No one can know his own father for himself. I wish mine were a man who had grown old on his estate.”

Mentes asked about the feast. Telemachus named the neighboring islands and Ithaca's leading families. All these men wanted his mother. She neither refused them outright nor chose a husband, and meanwhile they were eating away his property. Soon, he feared, they would destroy him too. A death at Troy would at least have brought his father a grave and his son an honorable name. Instead, he had vanished.

“You need Odysseus here with his weapons,” the guest answered. “I knew him when he was seeking poison for his arrows. One man refused it for fear of the gods, but my father gave it to him. If that Odysseus stood in his doorway now, these men would have a bitter wedding.”

Whether his father returned for revenge lay with the gods. Telemachus had work of his own to do.

“Call an assembly tomorrow and make your case before men and gods. Tell the suitors to go home. If your mother means to marry, let her return to her father, who can arrange it and provide the marriage gifts. Then take a ship and twenty men. Ask Nestor at Pylos, then Menelaus in Sparta, who came home last of the Greeks.

“If you hear your father is alive and returning, endure another year. If he is dead, come home, build his grave mound, give him funeral honors, and arrange your mother's marriage. After that, consider how to kill these suitors in your house, openly or by a trick. You are past childhood. Remember the fame Orestes won by killing his father's murderer.”

Telemachus promised to remember. He offered a bath and a valuable gift, but Mentes would not stay. They could exchange gifts on another visit.

Then Athena flew away like a bird. Courage rose in Telemachus, and he knew a god had visited him. He went back to the suitors.

Phemius was singing of the Greeks' disastrous return and the suffering Athena had brought them. Penelope heard him upstairs and came down with two women. Veiled and weeping beside a pillar, she begged him to choose another tale of gods or heroes. This one renewed her grief for her husband.

“Let him choose his song, Mother,” Telemachus said. “The singer did not cause the disaster. Zeus gives men good and evil. Odysseus was not the only man lost at Troy. Go back to your loom and your servants. Speech belongs to men, and above all to me, because I am master here.”

Penelope returned upstairs, astonished by her son's words. She wept until Athena brought her sleep. Below, each suitor longed to share her bed.

Telemachus told them to stop their uproar and listen to Phemius. In the morning they must come to the assembly, where he would formally order them away.

“Feast in your own houses at your own expense. If you keep feeding on one man's property, I will call on Zeus. May you die in this house with no one left to avenge you.”

The men bit their lips at his boldness. Antinous hoped Zeus would never make him king of Ithaca.

“I would accept the kingship if the god gave it,” Telemachus replied. “Others may rule the island. I will rule my father's house and the slaves he won for me.”

Eurymachus agreed that his property was his own and promised no one would rob or attack him. Then he asked about the visitor and whether he had brought news.

“I believe my father is dead,” Telemachus answered. “I trust neither rumors nor the prophets my mother consults. The visitor was Mentes, an old friend of our family.”

He kept his recognition of the goddess to himself.

The suitors sang and danced until night, then went to their homes. Euryclea, who had nursed Telemachus as a baby, led him upstairs with torches. Laertes had bought her when she was young and honored her in his household, though he had not taken her to bed for fear of his wife's anger. She folded the young man's shirt and shut his door.

Under a woollen fleece, Telemachus lay awake, thinking of the voyage and Athena's counsel.
