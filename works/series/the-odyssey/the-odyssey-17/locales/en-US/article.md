# The Dog at the Gate

Telemachus left the hut at dawn. In front of Eumaeus, he treated the old stranger as someone whose problems he could not afford to take on. The swineherd should bring him to town later. There would be more people there from whom to beg.

Odysseus accepted the arrangement. First he would warm himself by the fire. His clothes were thin, and the path was rough and cold. Nothing in their leave-taking showed Eumaeus that father and son had recognized each other and planned their revenge.

At the palace, Euryclea saw Telemachus first and ran to him. The other women crowded round. Penelope came down from her room and held him, crying with relief and reproach. He had left without telling her. She had feared she would never see him again.

“Let me be home first, Mother,” he said. “I've only just escaped. Pray for us. I must fetch the traveler who came back with me.”

He went to the meeting place, avoiding the suitors' friendly words. Their welcome hid the same malice he had left behind. Piraeus arrived with Theoclymenus and offered to send over the gifts from Sparta. Telemachus asked him to keep them for now. If he were killed, he would rather Piraeus had them than the men in his house.

He brought the seer home, and the two washed and ate. Penelope sat opposite with her spinning. She had waited long enough to ask about the journey.

Telemachus told her what Nestor and Menelaus had said. Nestor had welcomed him like a son but had no news. Menelaus had learned from the old god of the sea that Odysseus was alive on Calypso's island, unable to leave without a ship or crew. That was the report he gave his mother. He kept what he had learned in the hut to himself.

Theoclymenus spoke with greater certainty. “Your husband is in Ithaca now. He is learning what these men have done and preparing to punish them. That is what the bird showed me.”

Penelope promised him gifts if his words came true. Hope had entered the room again, but she had heard hopeful voices before.

Out in the country, Eumaeus provided a staff for his guest. Odysseus slung the ragged bag over his shoulder and followed him down the steep road. Near town they reached a fountain shaded by poplars. Water fell cold from the rock, beside an altar to the nymphs.

Melanthius, the goatherd, caught up with them there. He was taking the best goats to the suitors' dinner. At the sight of Eumaeus and his guest, he began to sneer. The swineherd had found another mouth for the palace. The old fellow would rather beg than work. If he went near the house, they would throw stools at him until he left.

As Melanthius passed, he kicked Odysseus on the hip.

Odysseus held his ground. He considered killing Melanthius with the staff, or throwing him down and smashing his skull. Instead he forced himself to endure the blow. Eumaeus faced the goatherd and prayed aloud that the nymphs would bring Odysseus home to end this bullying. Melanthius threatened to sell the swineherd overseas. He wished Telemachus dead and declared the king would never return. Then he hurried ahead to eat with the suitors.

The palace walls rose before the two walkers. Odysseus stopped outside. He smelled roasting meat and heard Phemius beginning his song. He spoke like a stranger admiring the strong doors and the succession of buildings, though this was the house he had left twenty years before. Eumaeus offered to go in ahead of him. The beggar would follow.

A dog lying by the stable lifted his head.

Odysseus had raised Argos before going to Troy. Young men once took him hunting, and no creature he tracked could easily escape him. Now he lay on a heap of dung waiting to be carried to the fields, old, flea-ridden, and neglected. His ears moved at the voices. He saw his master, lowered them, and wagged his tail.

He could not stand up to reach him.

Odysseus turned his face away and wiped his eyes before Eumaeus noticed. “That dog has a fine build. Was he a hunter, or only kept for his looks?”

Eumaeus described the hound's old speed. He had belonged to the absent king. With no one caring properly for him, he had come to this. The swineherd walked into the house.

Argos had seen his master once more. As Odysseus went in, the dog died.

Telemachus beckoned Eumaeus to sit by him. A moment later, his father entered in rags and settled on the threshold, leaning against a post. Telemachus took bread and meat from his table and sent it through the swineherd. He also told the guest to ask the others for food.

Odysseus blessed the young man and ate while Phemius sang. When the song ended, Athena urged him to make the round of the tables and learn how each man would treat a stranger. She still intended that none of them should escape death. He held out his hands. Some gave, some asked where he came from. Melanthius told them Eumaeus had brought him.

Antinous turned on the swineherd. “Haven't we beggars enough? Must you invite another to eat your master's goods?”

Eumaeus defended himself, but Telemachus stopped the quarrel. He turned to Antinous with a courtesy that made the accusation plain.

“You guard my property so carefully. Then give him something yourself. I allow it.”

Antinous pulled out the stool under his feet. If everyone gave the beggar what he meant to give him, he said, the man would stay away for months.

Other hands had already filled Odysseus's bag with bread and meat. He could have gone back to his place. Instead he approached Antinous and asked him for a share. A man who appeared to lead the others ought to be generous. He himself had once owned a rich house, he said, and had fed travelers without questioning them. He began another version of his false history. In Egypt his men had disobeyed orders, killed men, and carried off women and children. The Egyptians retaliated, killing some raiders and enslaving the rest. He had been handed to a man who took him to Cyprus. His present rags, he claimed, were what remained of that life.

Antinous ordered him away. It was easy for the others to give, he said, since none of the food belonged to them.

Odysseus turned to go. “Yet you can't spare a piece of somebody else's bread. In your own house, I doubt you'd give a poor man a grain of salt.”

The stool flew across the space between them. It struck the top of his right shoulder. Odysseus did not stumble. He stood for a moment, shaking his head, then carried his bag back to the threshold.

He had been struck for asking to satisfy his hunger, he told the room. If the poor had gods to defend them, may Antinous die before his wedding. Antinous threatened to have him dragged hand and foot through the courts and flayed alive if he did not eat in silence. Even the other suitors objected. A traveler might be a god in disguise, one warned. Antinous would not listen.

Telemachus felt the blow as he watched his father. He kept his tears back and said nothing. Their plan still depended on his silence.

Upstairs, Penelope heard what had happened and wished Apollo would strike Antinous. Eurynome answered that, if their prayers were heard, not one suitor would see another sunrise. Penelope hated them all, she said, but Antinous most of all. She sent for Eumaeus. This stranger had traveled far. She wanted to ask whether he knew anything of Odysseus.

The swineherd praised the guest's stories. He had listened for three days and nights and still had not heard all his misfortunes. The man claimed to know her husband and said he was alive nearby. Penelope told Eumaeus to bring him. If Odysseus returned, she said, father and son could punish the men destroying their home.

Just then Telemachus sneezed, loudly enough to be heard through the house. Penelope laughed. To her, it meant that every suitor would be killed and not one would escape. If the stranger spoke truthfully, he would have a cloak and tunic.

Eumaeus carried the invitation down. Odysseus asked to wait until sunset. He knew about her husband, but the suitors made it unsafe to cross the hall. No one had protected him from the stool. Let her give him a seat by the fire later, and he would answer her questions.

Penelope accepted the delay. She understood why a man would avoid that crowd.

Eumaeus warned Telemachus to be careful, finished his meal, and went back to the pigs. He would bring animals for the next day's feast. The suitors turned to singing and dancing as evening drew near.

On the threshold of his own house, Odysseus waited for the light to fade.
