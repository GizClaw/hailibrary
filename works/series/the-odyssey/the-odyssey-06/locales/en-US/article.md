# The Girl Who Stayed

While Odysseus slept under the leaves, Athena visited a different room. Nausicaa, daughter of King Alcinous, dreamed that a friend had come to stand beside her bed.

“What about all those clothes?” the friend asked. “You won't want a wedding party arriving before they're clean. Ask for the cart tomorrow. We can take them to the river.”

The face belonged to a girl Nausicaa knew. The visitor was Athena.

The Phaeacians had once lived close to the Cyclopes, who raided their land. Their old king Nausithous had brought them away to Scheria and built them a new city. Now Alcinous ruled there. Ships stood ready in its harbors, but the stranger who needed one was still asleep outside the walls.

Nausicaa woke thinking of the dream. She found her mother spinning by the fire and caught her father before he left for a council meeting.

“May I take the big cart today? You need clean clothes for council, and my brothers need them for dancing.”

She said nothing about a wedding of her own. Alcinous understood the part she left out, and agreed to the cart and mules. Her mother packed food, wine and oil. Nausicaa loaded the clothes and took the reins, with the maids accompanying her.

At the river they released the mules to graze and carried the washing to the pools. They trampled the cloth in clean running water, competing to get it clean, then spread it over the stones near the sea. After washing themselves, they ate while the sun did the remaining work.

There was time for a ball game. Nausicaa sang as they played. Later, as they began folding the dry clothes for the journey back, Athena brought about one last throw. The ball passed beyond a maid's hands and splashed into deep water. Their cries reached the sleeper beneath the olives.

Odysseus pushed himself upright. Women's voices. Were they people who would help him, or would he find another danger? He broke a leafy branch to cover himself and came out of the bushes.

The maids fled at the sight of the salt-stained stranger. Nausicaa stayed. Athena had steadied her, and she watched him approach without following the others.

He considered clasping her knees as a suppliant, but feared that approaching so closely might offend her. He chose to speak from a distance.

“Lady, I beg your help. Are you a goddess or a mortal woman?”

He spoke persuasively. She resembled Artemis; if she was human, her family must delight in her, and happiest of all would be her husband. She reminded him of a young palm he had admired beside Apollo's altar at Delos. He wished her a husband and a peaceful, united household. Then he told her what he needed.

“I've spent twenty days at sea. Yesterday I reached this shore. You're the first person I've found. Will you show me the town? And spare a piece of cloth, even something you use for wrapping the washing?”

Nausicaa heard a man choosing his words despite his misery.

“You can have clothes,” she said. “And help. These are Phaeacian lands. My father is Alcinous.”

She called the women back. There was no need to abandon someone who had washed ashore with nothing. Strangers in distress were under Zeus's care. She asked them to bring food and drink, find a sheltered place by the stream, and provide oil and clothes.

They returned and brought a tunic, cloak and oil. Odysseus asked for room to wash alone. He was ashamed to uncover himself before young women. They withdrew.

He washed the salt from his shoulders and hair, used the oil and put on the clothes. Athena made him taller and stronger, with thick curls about his head. He sat a little apart on the beach.

Nausicaa admired him. She told her maids he now looked like a god and must have been sent by the gods.

“I would be glad to marry a man like that,” she said softly to her attendants. “If only he wanted to stay. But bring him his meal.”

Odysseus ate with an urgency he could not disguise. It had been too long since he had found food. Meanwhile the women finished packing the linen, and Nausicaa had the mules brought back to the cart.

He had asked for the road to town. Now she had to decide how to take him there.

“Follow us while we're in the fields,” she told him. “When we get close to the city, you must wait.”

She feared people would see them together and say she had chosen a foreign husband while rejecting Phaeacian suitors. She admitted that she too would condemn a girl who went about with men before marriage, against her living parents' wishes.

“I can show you how to ask for help. But I don't want to bring you into the city beside me.”

Along the road, she said, stood a grove of poplars sacred to Athena, with a spring and nearby ground belonging to her father. He should wait there until the cart had reached the house. Then he could enter the city and ask for Alcinous's palace. Anyone would know it.

She described the ships and the harbors, the market where sailors worked on ropes and oars. Her people were seafarers, proud of their ships rather than skilled with bows.

“But when you enter our house, go to my mother first,” she said. “You'll find her by the fire, spinning, with her women behind her. My father sits near her, but pass him and clasp her knees. If you win her help, you may reach home, however far away it is.”

She took her place on the cart and held the mules to a pace the people on foot could follow. Odysseus went behind with the maids.

As the sun lowered, they reached the grove. He stopped among the trees while the cart continued. He prayed to Athena. He had not seen her beside him in the storm; now he asked her to make these people willing to receive him.

The goddess heard, but did not appear openly. She feared her uncle Poseidon, who was still determined to prevent Odysseus's return.
