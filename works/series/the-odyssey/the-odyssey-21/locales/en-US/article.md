# One Clear Note

Penelope took the key upstairs herself. Beyond the locked door were things the suitors had not yet managed to consume: metal, fine cloth, and the bow her husband had left behind when he sailed for Troy.

It had been a gift from Iphitus, whom Odysseus met in Messene while seeking sheep and their shepherds taken from Ithaca. Iphitus was seeking lost mares. He gave Odysseus his father's bow and received a sword and spear in return. Their friendship never reached a visit to each other's house: Heracles later killed Iphitus, his own guest, and kept the mares. Odysseus had left the bow at home as a keepsake. Penelope lifted its case from the peg, laid it across her knees, and cried before she could bring herself to stand again. Athena had prompted this contest as a way to destroy the suitors.

When she entered the hall with the bow and arrows, the men stopped talking.

“You have spent years asking me to choose,” she said. “Here is how I will do it. String this bow and shoot through the twelve axes. The man who can do both will be the man I go with.”

Eumaeus and Philoetius wept at the sight of their master's weapon. Antinous ordered them to stop upsetting the queen. This would be a difficult contest, he said, even as he imagined winning it himself.

Telemachus stepped forward to arrange the axes. He set them firmly in a straight line, so an arrow could pass through their openings. Then he reached for the bow.

“I'll try too. If I can do what my father did, my mother needn't leave with a stranger.”

He tried once. The wood resisted him. A second attempt failed, and then a third. On the fourth, he felt the bow beginning to yield.

His father caught his eye.

Telemachus loosened his grip.

“Not yet, then,” he said, giving the watching men a reason to underestimate him again. “Perhaps I'm still too young.”

He set the bow down for the suitors.

Leiodes, the suitors' sacrificial priest, tried first. He alone hated their misconduct, but his hands were not strong enough for the bow. He warned that it would take the life and spirit from many of them. Better to die, he said, than lose the marriage they had pursued so long. Antinous angrily rejected the warning. One weak man's failure proved nothing. He called for a fire and some fat. They warmed the bow and rubbed it, but the other men failed too. Only Antinous and Eurymachus remained untested.

While their attention was on the contest, Eumaeus and Philoetius went outside. Odysseus followed them beyond the courtyard gates.

“If your master came back today,” he asked, “would you stand beside him, or beside the men in there?”

Philoetius wished aloud that Zeus would send him home. Eumaeus wanted the same thing. There was no hesitation between their answers.

“Then look at me.”

They stared at the ruined clothes and the aged face.

“I am Odysseus. Twenty years it has taken me to reach this place. You alone have prayed for my return. If the gods give me victory, I'll find you wives and give you homes and land near mine. You shall be my son's brothers and friends.”

He showed them the scar on his leg. They knew its story. Both men came toward him at once, holding him and weeping, and for a moment he let them.

Then he drew back.

“We can't stay here. Someone will notice.”

He needed their help. Eumaeus must put the bow into his hands, whatever the suitors said. He must also tell Euryclea to keep the women behind their doors, even if they heard groaning or fighting. Philoetius was to secure the outer gates.

They returned separately. Odysseus went back to his small place beside the doorway. Nobody in the hall knew what had changed outside.

Now Eurymachus was trying the bow. He warmed it, worked at it, and finally admitted defeat.

It was not only the thought of losing Penelope that angered him. There were other women he could marry. But people would remember that none of them had been strong enough to use the absent man's weapon.

Antinous offered an explanation that allowed everyone to stop trying.

“This is Apollo's festival. We shouldn't be holding an archery contest today. Leave the axes where they are. We'll make offerings, and tomorrow we'll try again.”

The suggestion was welcome. Wine came round. The men were ready to turn the afternoon back into a feast.

Odysseus spoke from his chair.

“Let me hold the bow before you put it away. I want to know what strength is left in these hands.”

The answer was an uproar.

Antinous accused him of drinking too much. Wasn't a meal good enough? He recalled the centaur Eurytion, who went wild with wine at Peirithous's house and had his ears and nose cut off before being dragged out. If the beggar strung the bow, Antinous threatened, they would ship him to Echetus. He would never come back alive.

Penelope interrupted.

“He is my son's guest. Why refuse him a try? He knows that I am not offering to marry him.”

Eurymachus explained the real fear. Someone would tell the story outside this house. All the noble suitors had failed, and a wandering beggar had done what they could not.

“People already know you're eating another man's stores,” Penelope said. “Why are you so careful about your reputation now? Let him try. If he succeeds, I'll give him clothes, sandals, a sword and spear, and safe passage wherever he wants to go.”

Telemachus took charge before the argument could go any further.

“The bow belongs to this house. I will decide who holds it.”

He told his mother to return to her loom and servants. The bow was men's business, and above all his, as master here. She looked at him, surprised by his firmness, then went upstairs with her attendants. There she mourned her husband until Athena brought her sleep.

Eumaeus picked up the bow. The suitors shouted that they hoped his own hunting dogs would tear him to death. Frightened, he put it down.

“Bring it here,” Telemachus shouted. “Or I'll stone you all the way back to the country. I wish I had as much strength over these men as I have over you.”

The suitors laughed, their mood toward the son easing. The swineherd carried on. He put the bow into Odysseus's hands, then went to deliver the message to Euryclea. Philoetius slipped outside and made the gates fast with a length of rope. When he returned, he kept his eyes on the guest.

Odysseus examined the bow slowly. He turned it in his hands, looking for damage. The men watching him began to make jokes. Perhaps he was a bowmaker. Perhaps he wanted to study it so he could make one of his own.

He did not answer them.

He knew the feel of this wood. He set the string, bent the bow, and fitted it into place. There was no struggle for anyone to laugh at.

Then he tested the string with a finger.

One clear note sounded through the room, like a swallow's call.

The suitors paled. Outside, thunder rolled. Odysseus welcomed the sign. Still seated, he picked up the arrow lying on the table, fitted it, and drew.

The arrow passed through all twelve openings.

For a moment, nobody moved.

“Your guest hasn't disgraced you, Telemachus,” he said. “My hands still have their strength. Now, while it's light, let these men prepare supper. Then there can be singing and dancing.”

He gave his son a small sign. Telemachus took his sword and spear and moved to stand beside the chair.

The bow was back in its owner's hands.
