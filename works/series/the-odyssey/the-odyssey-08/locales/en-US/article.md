# The Song Behind the Cloak

At dawn, Alcinous led the still unnamed stranger to the meeting place beside the harbor.

Athena went through the town in a servant's shape, urging people to come. She made Odysseus taller and broader, so they would admire him and he would succeed in the contests ahead. The king called for a new ship and fifty-two picked sailors.

The sailors prepared the ship and returned for a feast of sheep, pigs and oxen at the palace.

A servant brought Demodocus into the hall. The Muse had given him song but taken his sight. His seat was set against a pillar, his lyre placed where his hand could find it, and food and wine within reach. When the meal was over, he began a song of Troy.

The song told of Odysseus quarreling with Achilles at a feast. Agamemnon had welcomed the quarrel because Apollo's oracle had foretold it. It began the suffering Zeus brought upon Greeks and Trojans.

Odysseus pulled his cloak over his face.

Whenever the singer paused, Odysseus wiped his cheeks and reached for his cup. Whenever the song began again, the cloth rose with it. Only Alcinous, sitting close, heard the breaths he was trying to steady.

The king did not question him in front of the room.

“Let's go outside,” he said. “Our guest should see how we excel beyond other peoples at boxing, wrestling, jumping and running.”

The crowd moved to open ground. Clytoneus won the race, Euryalus the wrestling, Amphialus the jump, Elatreus the discus, and Laodamas the boxing.

Laodamas, the king's son, came over when his own contest was finished.

“Will you join us? The ship is ready. There's time for a throw.”

“Why press me?” Odysseus answered. “I am full of troubles, thinking of home rather than games. I came to ask your people for help.”

Euryalus answered with an insult.

“You look more like a greedy merchant than an athlete, thinking only of cargo and profit.”

Odysseus angrily told him that beauty did not bring wisdom. A plain man could win respect by speaking well; Euryalus had a handsome face and a fool's tongue. Odysseus had once been among the finest athletes, and despite war and shipwreck, he would answer this insult.

He went to the discuses without taking off his cloak. He chose one heavier than the others, swung it and let it fly. People stepped back at the sound. It struck beyond every mark the contestants had made.

A spectator walked out to show where it had landed. It was Athena in another shape, making certain that no one could mistake the distance.

The disguised goddess said even a blind man could find his mark, so far ahead of all the rest. Encouraged by a friendly voice, Odysseus challenged them to match his throw.

He offered to box, wrestle or run against anyone except Laodamas: a guest should not challenge his host's family. At Troy only Philoctetes had surpassed him with a bow. He would not compete with dead heroes such as Heracles or Eurytus, who had challenged gods; Apollo had killed Eurytus for challenging his archery. He could throw a spear farther than another man shot an arrow. Running alone worried him, because hunger and the sea had weakened him. No one answered.

Alcinous stepped into the quiet.

“We have seen what you can do,” he told the guest. “Now let us show you something else.”

Although he had praised their boxing and wrestling earlier, the king now said those were not his people's greatest skills. They excelled at running, sailing, music and dancing, and loved good meals, baths and beds. He sent for the lyre, and the best dancers took the cleared ground.

Demodocus sang of Ares sleeping with Aphrodite in her husband Hephaestus's bed. The Sun saw and told the husband. Furious, Hephaestus forged unbreakable chains too fine even for gods to see, spread them over the bed, and pretended to leave for Lemnos. Ares saw him go, hurried to Aphrodite and invited her to bed. She willingly joined him. The chains caught them so tightly that they could not move.

The Sun warned Hephaestus again. He returned and called the gods to see his disgrace. His wife despised him for being lame, he said, and preferred handsome Ares. He would keep them trapped until Zeus returned the gifts he had paid for his daughter. The male gods gathered and laughed; the goddesses stayed away in shame. The slow, lame craftsman had caught swift Ares, and now Ares must pay.

Apollo asked Hermes whether he would endure those chains to sleep with Aphrodite. Hermes said he would accept three times as many, with every god and goddess watching. The others laughed again. Poseidon alone pressed for Ares's release, promising payment. Hephaestus doubted a wrongdoer's pledge: what if Ares fled and left his debt behind? Poseidon promised to pay himself if necessary. Then Hephaestus released the lovers. Ares fled to Thrace; Aphrodite went to Paphos in Cyprus, where the Graces bathed, anointed and clothed her.

Odysseus and the Phaeacians enjoyed the song. Then Laodamas and his brother danced with a red ball, sending it high and catching it as their feet kept time. He watched the skill of their movements and praised them openly.

Alcinous was pleased. He asked the leaders to bring gifts for their visitor, and turned to Euryalus.

“You owe him something besides a fine throw to remember.”

Euryalus offered his bronze sword, with a silver hilt and ivory sheath, and apologized.

“I spoke badly. Take this, and let my words end here. I hope your journey is kinder than the one that brought you.”

Odysseus accepted the weapon and slung it over his shoulder.

“Good fortune to you too. May you never miss the sword you have given me.”

The thirteen leaders, including Alcinous, each gave a cloak, shirt and talent of gold. By evening Arete had packed the gifts into a chest, adding the king's clothes. Alcinous also gave a golden cup. The queen warned Odysseus to secure the lid against theft while he slept aboard. He tied it with a knot Circe had taught him, then went to his bath.

Warm water eased the weariness from his body. Clean clothes replaced those he had worn through the games. When he returned to the hall, Nausicaa was standing beside a pillar.

“Farewell,” she said. “Remember me when you are home. You owe me first for saving your life.”

He stopped before her.

“May Zeus let me reach home. I will honor you all my days as the one who saved me.”

Then he went to the table beside Alcinous. Demodocus was brought in again. Odysseus cut a good portion of meat and asked a servant to carry it to the singer. The songs had hurt him, but he did not blame the man who had made them live.

After supper he addressed Demodocus himself.

“You sing so accurately,” he said, “you must have seen those events or heard from someone who did. Sing the next tale well, and I will praise your divine gift everywhere.”

“Tell the story of the wooden horse,” Odysseus said. “The one Epeius made, with Athena's help. Tell how the men got inside the city.”

Demodocus began with the Greeks burning their tents and sailing away, while Odysseus and armed men waited inside the horse. The Trojans dragged it through their gates and argued over it. Break it open? Throw it from the height? Or keep it for the gods?

They kept it.

The hidden men came out and sacked Troy, ravaging its streets. Odysseus and Menelaus fought their way to Deiphobus's house, where the battle was fiercest. Athena gave them victory.

Odysseus wept like a woman embracing her husband as he died defending their home: enemies beat her back and shoulders and dragged her into slavery. Only Alcinous noticed the guest's tears and heard his sobs.

Alcinous rose.

“Demodocus. Leave the song there.”

The strings fell silent.

“This feast is for our guest,” the king said. “It should not make him suffer.”

He turned towards Odysseus. “You've asked us to carry you home. Tell us where that is, and what we should call you.”

Their ships, Alcinous explained, needed neither helmsmen nor rudders. They understood men's intentions and could find the cities of the world through cloud and fog. Yet his father had warned that Poseidon might punish their many escorts, wrecking a returning ship and burying their city beneath a mountain. The king left that threat to the god.

“Tell us where you have wandered and who welcomed or harmed you,” he said. “Why do the songs of Troy grieve you? The gods sent that suffering for later generations to sing about. Did you lose a relation there, or a companion as dear as a brother?”
