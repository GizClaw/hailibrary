# The Orchard and the Oath

Hermes led the dead suitors away from the house where they had fallen. His golden wand gathered them, and their thin cries followed him like the squeaking of bats. Beyond Oceanus and the country of dreams, they reached the meadow where the dead heroes waited.

Achilles was speaking with Agamemnon. Once the king had commanded their whole army. Now he stood among the men murdered with him at his homecoming.

“If you had died at Troy,” Achilles said, “we could have raised a tomb for you. Your son would have inherited that honor.”

Agamemnon remembered Achilles' own funeral. Greeks and Trojans had fought over his body until a storm ended the struggle. After they carried him to the ships and washed him, his mother Thetis had come from the sea with the nymphs. Her cries frightened the army until Nestor told them who was coming. The Muses had sung, and gods and mortals had mourned for seventeen days. On the eighteenth they burned the body with sacrifices, honey, and resin, while warriors circled the fire. His bones had been laid with Patroclus's in the golden vessel his mother supplied, with Antilochus's kept separately. A great mound had risen where sailors could see it from the water. Thetis supplied splendid prizes for funeral games, keeping his honor alive.

“Your name still lives,” Agamemnon said. “I survived the war and came home to be murdered by Aegisthus and my wife.”

Then he recognized one of the new arrivals. Amphimedon had received him and Menelaus as guests when they came to persuade Odysseus to sail for Troy. The effort had kept them a month in Ithaca.

“What has brought so many of you here together?”

Amphimedon told of their pursuit of Penelope, of the shroud she wove by day and undid at night for three years, and the maid who exposed her secret in the fourth. They had forced her to finish. He blamed the wife for the trap that had closed around them. He described the father and son's meeting at the pig farm, their separate arrivals in town, and the weapons hidden away. In his telling, Odysseus had made his wife arrange the bow contest. The stranger they had insulted had strung the bow, shot through the axes and turned on them, killing Antinous first. A god had helped him and his son. Their bodies still lay in the house, he said, unknown to their families, without washing, mourning, or burial.

Agamemnon heard the name of Penelope and thought again of his own return.

“Odysseus has a wife who kept faith with him,” he said. “People will remember her in song. Mine killed her husband. Her hateful song has shamed all women, even the good.”

While they spoke among the shadows, Odysseus reached his father's farm with Telemachus, Eumaeus and Philoetius. He gave the herdsmen his armor and sent the three ahead to prepare a meal. He wanted to find Laertes in the orchard and see whether the old man would know him.

The other workers were away gathering thorn branches for a fence. Laertes was alone, bent over a vine. He wore a patched shirt, leather coverings against the brambles and an old goatskin cap. The plants had received more care than he had given himself.

Odysseus stopped beneath a pear tree and wept. He could go straight to his father. He could end the waiting with a word. Instead he approached as a stranger.

“You tend these trees well,” he said. “Why do you leave yourself so poorly cared for? Tell me, is this Ithaca? I once entertained a man from here, a son of Laertes. I sent him away with gold, a silver cup, cloth, and four enslaved women he chose himself.”

Laertes began to cry.

“That was my son. If you had found him here alive, he would have repaid your kindness. Now no one knows where he lies. Neither his mother nor I could lay him out, nor could his wife close his eyes. When did you see him?”

Odysseus called himself Eperitus from Alybas, son of Apheidas. It was five years, he said, since his guest had sailed away. Birds had flown on their right as they parted, and they had expected to meet again. The name, homeland, and visit were all invented.

Laertes stooped, filled his hands with dust and poured it over his gray head. A groan came from him. Odysseus could bear the test no longer. He ran to his father, held him and kissed him.

“Father, it's me. I've come back after twenty years. The suitors are dead. I've punished them in my own house.”

Laertes looked at the face so close to his.

“Give me something I can know you by.”

Odysseus showed the scar the boar had left on his leg when he visited his mother's father. Then he looked around the orchard.

“I followed you here as a boy, asking the names of everything. You gave me thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees and forty fig trees. You promised fifty rows of vines as well, with grain between them and grapes of many kinds. You walked with me and pointed them out.”

Laertes' knees failed. This time he clung to his son, and Odysseus held him upright until he could stand. The old man gave thanks to Zeus, but fear returned with his strength.

“The town will come for us. Those men had families.”

“Come inside first,” Odysseus said. “Telemachus and the others are there.”

The meal was being prepared. The old woman who cared for Laertes washed him and dressed him in a good cloak. Athena lent strength and stature to his worn body. When he returned, Odysseus marveled at the change. Laertes remembered capturing the fortress of Nericum. With that old strength, he said, he could have stood beside his son yesterday and killed many suitors.

Dolius and his six sons came from their work, fetched by the old woman who cared for Laertes, the sons' mother. At the sight of Odysseus they stopped, astonished. He called them to the meal. Dolius took his hand and kissed his wrist.

“We had almost given up waiting for this day. Does Penelope know? Shall someone go to her?”

“She knows already,” Odysseus said.

The sons came forward to greet him, and the household sat together.

In town, the news had reached the families of the dead. They gathered outside Odysseus's house and carried their sons and brothers away. Ithacan families buried their own. The bodies of men from other islands were put aboard fishing boats to be taken home.

Then the mourners met in the assembly. Eupeithes, father of Antinous, rose in tears.

“He took our men to Troy and lost them with their ships. Now he has returned and killed our sons. Let him sail away, and we'll be shamed forever. I'd rather die than live without revenge. Come after him before he reaches the mainland.”

Medon and the singer Phemius had come from the house alive. The crowd stared at them. Medon stepped forward.

“I saw a god beside Odysseus, in Mentor's shape, encouraging him and attacking the suitors. This did not happen against heaven's will.”

Fear passed through the gathering. Halitherses, the old seer, spoke next.

“You would not listen when Mentor and I warned you to restrain your sons. You let them consume another man's property and dishonor his wife. Don't go after him now and bring more grief on yourselves.”

More than half the gathering left. The others stayed with Eupeithes, armed themselves and set out for the farm.

Above them, Athena asked Zeus whether the fighting must continue.

“Odysseus has had his revenge,” Zeus replied. “Let him remain king, and let both sides swear peace. We will make the families forget this bloodshed. They can live together again.”

Athena already wanted this settlement. She went down to Ithaca.

At the farm, the meal was over. Odysseus sent one of Dolius's sons to look outside. The young man saw the armed party approaching and called everyone to put on armor.

Odysseus, Telemachus and the two herdsmen armed themselves. So did Dolius and his six sons, and Laertes. The old men had to become warriors again. They went out through the gate, with Odysseus in front.

Athena met them in Mentor's shape. Odysseus turned to Telemachus.

“Today you'll stand beside me. Show the courage our family has been known for.”

“You won't find me failing you,” his son answered.

Laertes heard them and rejoiced to have both son and grandson before him, each eager to prove himself. Athena came close to the old man and told him to pray to her and Zeus, then cast his spear. She put fresh strength into him.

Laertes prayed and threw. The spear pierced Eupeithes' helmet. Antinous's father fell dead, his armor ringing against the earth. Odysseus and Telemachus struck at the men in front of them. They would have killed them all and allowed none to return home, had Athena's voice not stopped the battle.

“Men of Ithaca, enough! End this fighting before more blood is shed.”

Weapons fell from frightened hands. The attackers turned and ran towards the town. Odysseus shouted and rushed after them.

Zeus sent a fiery thunderbolt to the ground in front of Athena.

“Stop, Odysseus,” she commanded. “Do not bring Zeus's anger on you.”

He obeyed her gladly and stopped.

Then Athena, still wearing Mentor's face and speaking with his voice, bound the two sides to an oath of peace.
