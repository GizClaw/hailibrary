# The Doors Are Shut

Odysseus stood and let the rags fall from his shoulders. He moved into the doorway with the bow and emptied the arrows beside his feet.

“The contest is over,” he said.

Antinous had lifted a cup to his mouth. He was still thinking about drinking when the next arrow pierced his throat and went through his neck. He fell, pouring blood, kicked over the table, and scattered the cup and food across the floor.

The suitors sprang up. Some shouted that the stranger had made a terrible mistake. Others reached for the weapons that had always hung on the walls.

Their hands found nothing.

Odysseus looked at them from the doorway.

“You thought I would never get back from Troy. You consumed my property, forced the women here to sleep with you, and courted my wife while I lived. You feared neither gods nor men. Now you will die.”

They understood who was holding the bow.

Eurymachus pointed to Antinous on the floor.

“We have wronged you,” he said quickly. “But everything began with him. He wasn't interested in marriage. He wanted to kill your son and rule Ithaca. He's paid for it. Let the rest of us repay you.”

Each man would pay the value of twenty oxen, he offered, and give gold and bronze until Odysseus was satisfied.

“Give me everything you own or ever will own,” Odysseus replied, “and I still won't stop until I've killed you all. Fight or try to flee. Not one of you will escape.”

Eurymachus drew his sword. He called on the others to use the tables as shields and rush the doorway. If they could force their way out, someone could raise the town.

He charged first. Odysseus shot him before he reached the entrance.

Amphinomus came next, trying to break past the bow. Telemachus struck him from behind. The young man had no time to recover his spear. He hurried back to his father.

“We need the armor.”

“Go. Quickly.”

Odysseus stayed in the doorway while his son fetched shields, helmets, and spears for the four defenders. Eumaeus and Philoetius armed themselves beside him. At last the arrows were gone. Odysseus put the bow against the wall and took up a shield and spear.

A side door opened into a narrow passage. Odysseus posted Philoetius there, where one man could block an attack. Agelaus wanted someone to slip out and call the town, but Melanthius said the passage ran too close to the guarded entrance. He would fetch weapons instead.

Then Odysseus saw something that made him turn sharply toward his son. The men across the hall were getting weapons too.

Melanthius knew the passages of the house. He had slipped into the storeroom and carried arms to the suitors.

“I left the door open,” Telemachus said. “That was my fault.”

There was no time to rebuke him. Eumaeus saw the goatherd heading back for another load. Odysseus sent the two herdsmen to stop him while he and his son held the entrance.

Odysseus ordered them to bind Melanthius's hands and feet behind him, then suspend him near the rafters so that his pain would last. They waited beside the storeroom door, seized him as he emerged, and dragged him back by the hair. They tied him and hoisted him as ordered. Eumaeus mocked his “soft bed” and told him he would know when dawn came and it was time to bring goats. They shut the door and returned to the hall, leaving him alive in agony.

The suitors still outnumbered the men at the door. They had swords and, now, spears and shields. Odysseus had reached his own home only to fight for it from a narrow strip of floor.

A familiar figure appeared beside him. Mentor's face, Mentor's voice. But Odysseus knew Athena had come.

Agelaus threatened this new ally. After killing father and son, they would kill Mentor too, take his property, and drive his children and widow from the house and city.

The goddess did not retreat. She challenged Odysseus to show the courage he had brought to Troy. Then she took the shape of a swallow and perched above the fighting. She would help, but father and son still had to hold their ground.

Six men threw spears at once. Athena turned them into the posts, wall, and doors. The four defenders killed a man each and recovered their spears from the bodies. A second volley mostly missed, but Amphimedon grazed Telemachus's wrist, and Ctesippus cut Eumaeus's shoulder above the shield. Neither defender left his place.

Philoetius drove his spear into Ctesippus's chest. It was his return gift, he told the dying man, for the hoof thrown at Odysseus. The defenders killed others, including Agelaus and Leocritus. Around them, the room that had held the feasting became a place of overturned furniture and men trying to find a way out.

Athena raised her deadly aegis above the hall. Panic broke the suitors' resistance. They fled from one end of the room to the other, but the doors offered no escape. Odysseus and his men pursued them, striking them down until the floor ran with blood.

Leiodes knelt at Odysseus's knees. He had never harmed a woman here by word or deed, he pleaded, and had tried to stop the others. Would he die for being their priest? Odysseus answered that their priest must have prayed against his return and hoped to marry his wife and father children with her. He picked up Agelaus's fallen sword and cut off Leiodes's head while he was still speaking.

Not every man in the hall had chosen to be there.

The singer Phemius set down his instrument and knelt before Odysseus. The suitors had forced him to entertain them, he said. Telemachus could tell his father that.

“Let him live,” the son said. “He didn't come here to harm us. And Medon helped me when I was a child. Spare him too, if he's still alive.”

From beneath a covering near a seat, Medon emerged. He had been hiding there as the fighting moved around him. He appealed to Telemachus, then waited for the father's answer.

Odysseus sent the herald and the singer into the courtyard. They hurried out, frightened even after they had been spared.

The king walked through the hall, looking for anyone still hiding or able to rise. His clothes and skin were stained from the fight. The long tables had become obstacles he had to step around.

He asked Telemachus to call Euryclea.

The old nurse came in and saw what had happened. Relief rose in her so suddenly that she was about to cry out. Odysseus stopped her.

“Don't cheer over dead men.”

He said the gods' judgment and the men's own acts had brought them down. Then he asked which women had misbehaved and which were innocent. Euryclea said there were fifty women in the house and accused twelve of sleeping with the suitors and disrespecting her and Penelope. They had not disobeyed Telemachus, she added, because until recently his mother had not allowed him to command them. She wanted to wake Penelope, but Odysseus refused for now.

He ordered Telemachus and the herdsmen to make the twelve carry out the dead and wash the furniture. Then they were to take them into the narrow space outside and kill them with swords, ending their secret lovemaking with the suitors. The women came weeping. Under his orders they carried the bodies to the gatehouse, washed the tables and chairs, and removed the blood and dirt the men scraped from the floor.

When it was clean, the three men drove them into the narrow enclosure. Telemachus rejected his father's order to use swords. He would not give them a clean death, he said, because they had insulted him and his mother and slept with the suitors. He fastened a ship's rope overhead, high enough to keep their feet from touching ground, and hanged all twelve. Their feet struggled briefly, then were still.

The men brought Melanthius into the inner court. They cut off his ears, nose, and genitals, gave the genitals raw to the dogs, then severed his hands and feet. He died under this mutilation. They washed themselves and went back inside.

For now, Odysseus ordered the hall cleansed with fire and sulfur. The nurse offered him clean clothing, but he wanted the room dealt with first. Smoke moved through the spaces where the men had eaten and fought.

Then the women who had remained loyal came carrying lights. They had heard that their master was home. They crowded around him, taking his hands, touching his shoulders, trying to recognize the man who had been absent so long.

He knew their faces.

After all he had forced himself to hold back, Odysseus nearly wept.
