# The Swineherd's Spare Cloak

The dogs found Odysseus before the man at the hut did.

They came out from among the pens, barking hard enough to drown the pigs. Odysseus lowered himself and let his staff fall. A shout cut across the yard. Eumaeus dropped the piece of oxhide he had been shaping into a sandal and ran through the gate. Shouting and throwing stones, he drove the dogs back.

“Inside,” he said, breathing hard. “Questions can wait.”

Odysseus followed him past the pens Eumaeus had built during his master's absence.

In the hut, Eumaeus spread rushes on the floor and covered them with the thick skin he used for his own bed. He killed and roasted two young pigs and mixed wine for the meal. The stranger's name had still not been asked.

“May you get what you wish for,” Odysseus told him.

Eumaeus answered that even a poorer beggar would be welcome: strangers came under Zeus's protection. He feared the god and pitied the man. His own position was less secure under young masters, and he could offer only what he had.

He missed his master. Had Odysseus grown old at home, Eumaeus believed his work would have earned him a house, land, and a wife. Instead the war had taken him away. Eumaeus cursed Helen's entire family for the deaths it had brought. Hope had worn thin, but the work had not stopped. Every day the suitors demanded another of the best pigs for their feasts. The men who fed and guarded the animals got what was left. Eumaeus set the hot meat before his guest without apologizing for its size.

“It will fill you,” he said. “They haven't taken that from us yet.”

Odysseus ate and drank eagerly, saying nothing while he planned revenge. The swineherd was describing the waste of his own estate.

When Eumaeus offered him the wine bowl, Odysseus asked about the absent owner as if he had never heard of him.

“This master who bought you, you say he went to Troy. What was his name? I've traveled. I might know something.”

“Odysseus.” Eumaeus watched him over the bowl. “But don't discover a story just because I've given you supper.”

Strangers had come before, offering news in return for clothes and a warm place to sleep. Penelope questioned them through her tears. Eumaeus said he would never find so good a master, even if he returned to his own parents: he wanted to see them, but Odysseus's loss grieved him more. Once he had welcomed an Aetolian fugitive who had killed a man. The traveler claimed to have seen Odysseus in Crete with Idomeneus, repairing storm-damaged ships, and promised he would return in summer or autumn. The promised season had passed. No ship had brought the king home.

“I won't take payment now,” Odysseus said. “Wait until he comes. Then you can give me a cloak.”

“I would rather you kept the promise to yourself.”

Odysseus declared that he hated a man who lied because he was poor. Then he swore that the master would return before this moon ended and the next began, to take revenge on the men tormenting his wife and son. Eumaeus heard the oath without reaching for belief. He wanted it to be true. So did Penelope, so did old Laertes. Wanting had not brought their missing man through the door.

There was Telemachus to worry about now as well. The boy had gone looking for his father, and the suitors were waiting to attack his ship. Eumaeus could do nothing for him from this hut. His grief changed, as he spoke, from a man he thought dead to a young man he feared might be killed.

“Tell me about yourself instead,” he said. “What brought you here in those rags?”

Odysseus gave him a life that was not his own.

He claimed to be a Cretan nobleman's son by an enslaved concubine. His father had treated him like his legitimate brothers; after the father's death, those brothers had left him only a small share. Courage had won him a wealthy marriage, and nine raiding voyages had enriched him with plunder. He preferred ships and fighting to farming or raising children. The Cretans had made him and Idomeneus lead them to Troy. Nine years of war and a tenth-year victory had brought him home, but he had stayed only a month before taking nine ships to Egypt.

There, he said, his followers had ignored orders, killed men and carried women and children away. The Egyptians had attacked, killing some and taking others for forced labor. He had thrown down his weapons and begged at the king's knees. The king had protected him even from Egyptians still trying to spear him, out of fear of Zeus.

After seven years gaining wealth in Egypt, he claimed, a Phoenician had persuaded him to come home with him. A year later that trader had taken him aboard for Libya, supposedly to help with cargo, really to sell him. He had suspected it but could not avoid going. Zeus had wrecked the ship. Clinging to its mast, he had drifted nine days and reached Thesprotia on the tenth night. King Pheidon's son had found him cold and exhausted and brought him to his father, who clothed and sheltered him.

There, he claimed, Pheidon had shown him the treasure Odysseus had left behind, enough for ten generations. Odysseus had gone to Dodona to ask Zeus whether he should return openly or in secret, and a ship was ready to bring him home. Pheidon had sent the supposed traveler off first, towards King Acastus on Dulichium. But those sailors too had decided to sell him. They had stripped him, dressed him in rags and tied him up when they reached Ithaca. While they ate ashore, the gods had loosened his bonds. He had slipped down the rudder, swum away and hidden in woods until the searchers gave up. That, he said, had brought him to Eumaeus's door.

The story kept his true identity hidden.

Eumaeus listened all the way to the end. “You've had a hard journey. That much I can see. But you won't make the part about my master true by putting it in a longer tale.”

Odysseus tried once more. If his promise proved right, the swineherd should clothe him and send him onward. If it failed, he could order his men to throw the liar from a cliff.

Eumaeus set the bowl down. “No. You came into my house for shelter. I'm not turning your life into a wager.”

The pigs were coming home, loudly. Eumaeus left the stranger's bowl beside him and went to see them into the pens. Their argument was over for now.

The other herdsmen shut the animals into their pens. Eumaeus chose a fine boar for the evening meal, set aside a portion for the gods, and gave each man a fair share. The guest received a choice cut. Mesaulius brought the bread. Eumaeus had bought this man with his own money during Odysseus's absence, without consulting Penelope or Laertes. Odysseus thanked his host, who urged him to eat: the gods gave and withheld as they chose.

After supper, rain began striking the hut. The night had no moon. Wind pressed against the walls, and Odysseus felt it through the thin cloth Athena had given him. He could have asked plainly for a covering. Instead he chose another story, this one shaped to discover how far Eumaeus's care would go.

“There was a night outside Troy,” he began, “when I made the mistake of leaving my cloak behind.”

He described an ambush led by Menelaus, Odysseus and, he claimed, himself. The men lay among reeds in snow and a freezing wind. In his account, he nudged Odysseus beside him and admitted he could not bear the cold. Odysseus told him to keep quiet, then announced a dream from the gods: someone must run back and ask Agamemnon for reinforcements. Thoas sprang up, leaving his cloak. The shivering soldier took it and lasted until morning.

Odysseus finished and drew his own ragged cloth closer. “I was younger then. People thought a soldier worth helping.”

Eumaeus got to his feet. “Keep your next story until you're warm.”

He laid skins beside the fire and brought a heavy cloak kept for especially bad weather. It was for this night only, he explained. Spare clothing was scarce here. In the morning the guest would need his own things again. When Telemachus returned, he could arrange proper clothes and the next part of the journey.

Odysseus lay down, and Eumaeus covered him. The other men settled near the fire. The swineherd did not.

He put on his own thick cloak, took his spear, and prepared to sleep outside with the animals under the shelter of a rock. Odysseus watched him through the doorway. Even now, in rain and darkness, Eumaeus would not leave the pigs unguarded.

Under the borrowed cloak, Odysseus was pleased to see his servant still guarding his property in his absence.
