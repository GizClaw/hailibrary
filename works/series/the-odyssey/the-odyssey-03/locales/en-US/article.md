# The Old King's Table

The shore at Pylos was already crowded when the ship arrived. Smoke drifted above the people gathered for Poseidon's feast. Telemachus stepped onto the sand and looked for the old king among the many faces.

Athena, still disguised as Mentor, told him to approach Nestor and ask for the truth about his father.

“What am I supposed to say to him?” he asked Mentor.

“You will find some words yourself, and the gods will prompt the rest.”

Athena led him towards the gathering. Pisistratus, Nestor's son, welcomed them by the hand, made room on the sheepskins beside his father and brother, gave the visitors food, and offered the cup first to the older guest.

“We're honoring Poseidon. Will you join our prayer?”

Athena prayed for the king's household, for the people sharing the feast, and for the purpose that had brought the travelers. Then she passed the cup to Telemachus. He followed her example. Nobody asked him to explain himself with an empty stomach.

When they had eaten, Nestor turned to them.

“Now tell me where you've sailed from.”

“Ithaca. I'm looking for news of Odysseus. I'm his son.”

Athena gave Telemachus courage to ask plainly.

“If you know something bad, tell me that too. I don't want a kinder answer than the truth.”

Nestor remembered Achilles, Ajax, Patroclus, and his own son Antilochus. They had lost so many men in the long war. No one had matched Odysseus's resourcefulness; he and Nestor had always agreed in council.

“You sound like him,” Nestor said. “That's what surprised me.”

After Troy fell, Nestor told him, the victors had quarreled over leaving. Menelaus wanted to sail at once. Agamemnon wanted to stay and appease Athena, whose anger hung over the army. They called the meeting late, when men had been drinking, and the argument divided the fleet.

Nestor sailed with those who wanted home. Odysseus sailed too. But at Tenedos another quarrel broke out, and Odysseus turned back with some of the ships to rejoin Agamemnon. Nestor continued, unwilling to risk another delay. He reached Pylos safely.

“That is where my own knowledge stops,” he said. “After we parted, I did not see your father again.”

Nestor had heard reports of the others since returning. Some had brought their crews home. Agamemnon had reached his kingdom only to be murdered by Aegisthus, who had taken his place beside Clytemnestra. Later Orestes, Agamemnon's son, returned and killed his father's murderer.

Nestor praised Orestes and urged Telemachus to win a name himself. The young man wished the gods would let him take the same revenge on the suitors who were plotting his ruin, but believed no such happiness was meant for him.

Nestor had heard about the suitors. He asked why the people of Ithaca let them behave this way. Perhaps Odysseus would return. Perhaps Athena, who had stood so close beside the father at Troy, would help his son as well.

“I can't make myself believe that,” Telemachus said.

His companion answered before Nestor could.

“A god can save a man even from far away. I would rather suffer on the road and reach home safely than return quickly to be murdered.”

Athena reminded him that a hard return could still end safely. Agamemnon's shorter passage had ended in his own blood. The gods could bring help from far away, though even their love could not remove a mortal's appointed death.

Telemachus did not want to argue about hope. He asked how Agamemnon could have been killed at home, and why Menelaus had not stopped it.

Nestor said that had Menelaus found Aegisthus alive, dogs and vultures would have eaten him without a grave. While the army was at Troy, Aegisthus had pursued Clytemnestra. She resisted at first. Agamemnon had left a singer to guard her. Aegisthus abandoned him on a deserted island to die and be eaten by birds. Clytemnestra then willingly joined him. Aegisthus celebrated with offerings to the gods; together they betrayed the returning king.

Menelaus had been delayed elsewhere. Apollo killed his helmsman Phrontis at the steering oar, and he stopped to bury him. Then Zeus sent a storm that scattered his ships. Some were wrecked on Crete, although their men escaped. Menelaus reached Egypt with five vessels, gathering treasure far from the kingdom where his brother had been killed.

For seven years Aegisthus ruled. In the eighth, Orestes came back. Menelaus arrived home on the day of the funeral feast for Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, too late to prevent what had happened.

Nestor paused before returning to the young man's journey.

“Go to Menelaus. He has been places I never reached. But don't stay away from your own house longer than you must.”

He offered horses and a chariot, with one of his sons to drive, if Telemachus preferred the road to a sea passage.

By the time the feast ended, daylight had gone. The visitors rose to return to the ship. Nestor caught them before they could leave.

“Have I shown you so poor a welcome? Odysseus's son isn't sleeping on a deck while I have blankets in my house.”

Athena agreed that Telemachus should stay. Pretending to be Mentor, she said she would see to the young crew, then visit the Cauconians to collect an old debt. Nestor should send Telemachus onwards with his best horses.

Then Mentor's familiar shape rose from the ground. An eagle flew away above the gathering.

Nestor seized Telemachus's hand. He recognized Athena and told him that such divine favor promised greatness. He prayed for his family and promised her a young heifer with gilded horns.

After wine and offerings in the house, Telemachus slept in the room above the gate with Pisistratus.

Nestor rose early to fulfill his promise. A heifer was brought from the fields. A goldsmith covered its horns with gold, while the king's sons prepared water and the fire. Most of Telemachus's crew came from the harbor; two men remained to guard the ship.

After the prayers, Thrasymedes struck the heifer's neck with an axe. Nestor's wife and daughters cried out in delight; Pisistratus cut its throat, and the blood drained away. Portions burned upon the fire, and the rest was prepared for the meal. Meanwhile Polycaste, Nestor's daughter, washed Telemachus, anointed him with oil and gave him fresh clothes. He returned to sit beside the king.

After the meal, the horses were harnessed. Bread, wine and delicacies went into the chariot. Pisistratus gathered the reins, and Telemachus climbed in beside him.

They crossed the country all day, stayed that night with Diocles at Pherae, and set out again at dawn. The horses carried them on through the farmland as another evening drew near.
