# Home Behind the Mist

Odysseus had finished his story, but he had not finished watching the sun.

Its light moved slowly across Alcinous's hall. There was another meal, another song, another kind hand offering him something for the journey. He accepted the gifts and thanked their givers. Then his eyes went back to the doorway. Beyond it waited a ship, and beyond the ship, perhaps, his own house.

Alcinous had promised to send him home safely. He also required each lord to add a tripod and a cauldron to the gifts already packed. They could recover the cost through a levy on the people, he said; the donors need not bear the whole expense themselves.

Before sunset the king checked the cargo himself. Bronze vessels lay secured beneath the rowing benches. Clothing and gold were packed where they could not shift against the crew. Odysseus had arrived among these people with nothing. Now he would leave carrying more than he could lift alone.

At last the sun went down. He thanked his hosts for their gifts and escort, wishing them happiness and his own wife safety. After they poured an offering to the gods, he stood, placed his cup in Queen Arete's hands and said goodbye. He wished her peace with her family. She sent fresh clothing, provisions and his chest down to the shore.

The sailors spread bedding for him in the stern. He lay down without a word. The oars had scarcely begun to move before he slipped into sleep, so deep that the weariness of war and wandering left him.

He did not hear the water hurry past. He did not see the last star shining ahead of the dawn, or the two arms of land that opened to receive the ship. The Phaeacians knew this sheltered harbor on Ithaca. They carried him ashore in his bedding, laid him on the sand, and set his gifts beside an olive tree, safely away from the path. Then they turned their bow toward home.

Poseidon had watched the crossing too.

He had not been promised that Odysseus would wander forever. Zeus had already allowed the man to return. But the sea god had meant the return to cost him dearly. Now his own Phaeacian descendants had brought Odysseus home asleep, surrounded by treasure. Poseidon went to Zeus with his grievance. What would become of his authority if people could cross his waters as though his anger meant nothing?

Poseidon proposed wrecking the returning ship and burying the city under a mountain. Zeus advised him to turn the ship to stone near the shore, while the people were watching. He could bury the city afterward. The punishment was to be a public warning against carrying strangers home.

The people of Scheria were watching for their sailors. The ship came quickly toward them. Then its movement stopped. Hull, prow, and all the shape they knew became stone, rooted in the sea.

Alcinous remembered his father's warning. One day, he had said, the sea god would strike a returning ship because the Phaeacians carried strangers home so readily. Now that day had arrived.

“No more escorts,” the king told his frightened people. “Bring twelve fine bulls for Poseidon. Ask him to leave our city standing.”

They gathered at the altar. Far away, unaware of the price his passage had brought upon his hosts, Odysseus opened his eyes.

Mist stood between him and everything he wanted to recognize.

He sat up. There were trees, but their shapes looked strange. A road appeared and disappeared in the white air. The shore gave him no answer. For a moment the sea, all those years of it, seemed to have carried him nowhere.

He accused the Phaeacians of breaking their promise and called on Zeus to punish them. Then he counted the gifts under the olive, moving from the metal vessels to the clothing, checking the gold. Nothing was missing. He still believed his generous hosts had left him in the wrong country.

He was still walking beside the water when a young shepherd approached, neatly dressed, a light spear in one hand. Odysseus went to meet him, keeping the treasure in sight.

“I need help,” he said. “First, tell me where I am.”

The shepherd looked at him with mild surprise. “Ithaca. Have you never heard the name?”

He had heard it in storms, in dreams, in the silence after battles. He almost said so. Instead, the old habit rose before joy could pass his lips. A stranger might repeat anything he heard.

“I've heard it in Crete,” Odysseus said. Then he told a false story. He had killed Orsilochus, the swift-running son of King Idomeneus, he claimed. The prince had wanted his Trojan plunder, saying Odysseus had refused to serve under his father and acted as an independent captain. So he and a companion had ambushed Orsilochus by night and speared him. No one had seen the killing. He had paid Phoenician sailors to carry him to Pylos or Elis, but the wind had brought them here instead. While he slept, they had put his goods beside him and sailed on to Sidonia. The tale accounted for his wealth and flight while hiding his true identity.

The shepherd smiled.

The young face changed. Before Odysseus stood a woman with a clear, steady gaze, and he knew Athena.

“Even here?” she asked. “You have reached your own island, and you're still preparing another name to hide behind.”

“You change your face whenever you choose,” he replied. “Must I trust the first one you offer?”

She had praised the cunning they shared, his among men and hers among gods. But he had known her help at Troy. Then had come years when he could not find her anywhere, however badly he needed her. She had guided him among the Phaeacians, yes. But this shore still looked wrong.

“Show me,” he said. “Don't only tell me.”

Athena explained the mist. It hid him from the islanders while they made their plans. She had known he would return safely, though he would lose all his companions. She had stayed away because she did not want a quarrel with Poseidon, still furious over his blinded son. Now she pointed through the whiteness: the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave of the water nymphs where Odysseus had once brought offerings.

The mist loosened. The outline of wooded Neritum emerged beyond the shore. The cave was there, and the tree. They had not moved into a different world. He had come back to theirs.

Odysseus kissed the earth. Then he raised his hands to the nymphs and greeted them, promising offerings again if Athena preserved his life and brought his son to manhood.

Athena let him finish. “Your treasure first.”

She showed him where to store it in the cave. He carried in the gifts, and together they hid them beyond an idle passerby's reach. Athena closed the entrance with a stone. Only then did they sit beside the olive to speak of his house.

For three years, men seeking Penelope's hand had eaten through his supplies and behaved like owners. Penelope delayed them with encouraging messages and promises she did not mean. She still grieved for him. He could not walk through his gate and assume that the people inside would welcome him.

Odysseus thought of Agamemnon, who had survived the war only to be killed at home.

“Tell me how to take revenge. Stand beside me as you did at Troy, and I will fight three hundred men.”

“I will stand beside you,” Athena said. She promised that some of the men devouring his wealth would spatter the floor with their blood and brains. First she would disguise him, and he must go to Eumaeus. The swineherd had kept faith with his wife and son. He must listen and endure insults without revealing himself while they prepared to kill the suitors.

“And my son?”

“In Sparta, with Menelaus. He went looking for news of you.”

Odysseus turned sharply. “You knew I was alive. Why send him across the sea?”

“I sent him so that the journey would bring him a good name. He is well cared for there.” She did not hide the rest: some of the suitors had taken a ship out to wait for him. They meant to kill him on his way home. She expected them to fail, and some of the men consuming his estate to die first.

Odysseus accepted the disguise as part of their plan for vengeance. He would enter his own country as a man no one would think worth noticing, learn what happened in his house, and wait until he could strike.

Athena touched him with her wand. His skin creased, his bright hair thinned away, and the clear eyes in his face grew dim. She exchanged his good clothing for worn, smoke-stained cloth, put a rough hide over him, and gave him a staff and a torn bag.

Anyone looking at him would see an old beggar. Even his wife and son would not know him.

Athena left for Sparta. Odysseus turned from the road to his palace and took the path toward the pig pens.
