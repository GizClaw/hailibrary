# A Seat Beside the Fire

Nausicaa reached the palace, where her brothers unloaded the clothes and unharnessed the mules. In her room, Eurymedusa lit a fire and brought supper. The old nurse had been taken from Apeira and awarded to Alcinous as a prize.

Odysseus left the grove. Athena wrapped him in mist so the Phaeacians would not question or insult him. At the entrance to the city she appeared as a little girl carrying a pitcher. He asked her for the king's house.

“I'll show you,” she said. “He lives near my father. Don't stop to speak or question anyone. These people dislike strangers. But Poseidon gives them ships as swift as a thought or a bird.”

Hidden in the mist, Odysseus followed her past harbors, ships, meeting places and high walls. At the palace she told him to go boldly inside and seek Queen Arete.

“She and Alcinous both descend from Poseidon. He had Nausithous by Periboea, whose father Eurymedon ruined the giants he ruled and lost his own life. Nausithous's sons were Alcinous and Rhexenor. Apollo killed Rhexenor before he had a son, leaving only Arete, whom her uncle Alcinous married. Her husband, children and people honor her. She helps settle disputes. Win her kindness, and you may reach home.”

Athena left for Athens. Odysseus paused to admire the palace. Bronze walls, gold doors and silver pillars shone before him. Immortal gold and silver dogs, made by Hephaestus, guarded the entrance. Golden youths held torches above the seats. Within, women ground grain and wove. Outside, a walled orchard bore ripe fruit while new fruit grew; grapes were ripening, being gathered and pressed all at once. The gods had given Alcinous abundance through every season.

The chiefs were making their last drink offering to Hermes before leaving. Still hidden, Odysseus crossed the hall and clasped Arete's knees. At that instant the mist fell away. Everyone stared in silence.

“Queen Arete, I ask you, your husband and your guests to help me home. May the gods prosper you and let your children inherit your possessions and honors. I have suffered long, far from my friends.”

Then he sat in the ashes beside the hearth.

Echeneus, an elderly Phaeacian counselor, spoke first.

“It shames us to leave a stranger sitting there. Give him a seat, food and wine. Let us honor Zeus, who protects those who ask for help.”

Alcinous raised Odysseus by the hand and gave him the place beside himself where his favorite son Laodamas had been sitting. Servants brought water, bread and meat. While the stranger ate, the king ordered wine poured for Zeus.

“Go home tonight,” he told the chiefs. “Tomorrow we will gather more men, feast in our guest's honor, and arrange his passage. We must bring him safely to his own country. After that, he will meet the fate allotted him. Unless he is a god? They usually come openly to our feasts, for we are close to them, like the Cyclopes and the giants.”

“I am no god,” Odysseus answered. “I am a man who has suffered greatly. Even grief must yield to hunger. Let me eat, then help me leave tomorrow. I would be content to die after seeing my possessions, my slaves and my house again.”

The chiefs approved. After another offering, they left. Arete and Alcinous remained with the guest while servants cleared the meal. Arete recognized his clothes as work done by herself and her women.

“Who are you? Where did those clothes come from? You said you arrived from across the sea.”

Odysseus began with the distant island of Ogygia.

“Zeus struck my ship with lightning. All my companions drowned. I clung to the keel for nine days and reached Calypso's island on the tenth night. She received me and offered to make me immortal, but I would not accept. Seven years I stayed there weeping. In the eighth she let me leave, whether Zeus had commanded it or she had changed her mind.

“I left on a raft she supplied with food, wine and clothing, with a fair wind she sent. I sailed seventeen days. On the eighteenth I saw your mountains, but Poseidon broke my raft with a storm. I swam to the rocks, was driven back, and finally landed at a sheltered river mouth. I covered myself with leaves and slept all night and most of the next day.

“When I woke in the afternoon, your daughter and her women were playing by the water. I asked her for help. She was remarkably thoughtful for one so young. She gave me bread and wine, let me wash, and gave me these clothes.”

“My daughter should have brought you straight here,” Alcinous said.

“Don't blame her. She told me to follow with the women. I was ashamed and feared you might resent seeing me with her.”

He gave the king that account instead of repeating Nausicaa's warning about gossip.

“I don't take offense without reason,” Alcinous replied. “A man who thinks as you do would make a welcome son-in-law. Stay, marry my daughter, and I will give you a house and land. But no one will keep you against your will. Tomorrow I will arrange your escort. You can sleep while our men carry you, even farther than Euboea. Our sailors once took Rhadamanthus there to visit Tityus and made the outward voyage in a day.”

Odysseus rejoiced and prayed that Zeus would let the king fulfill his word, winning lasting honor and bringing him home.

Arete ordered a bed made above the gateway, with red rugs, coverings and woollen cloaks. The maids brought torches and called the stranger when it was ready.

Odysseus gladly went to rest. Alcinous slept in the inner house beside Arete.
