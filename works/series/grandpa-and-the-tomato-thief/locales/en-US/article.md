# The Tomato Thief on Trial

## The Empty Stem

Grandpa was salting a bowl of cold noodles when he noticed the empty stem. His hand stopped. The salt did not.

“Another one,” he said.

Maya looked through the kitchen window. Heat pressed the leaves flat against their stakes. Yesterday, four tomatoes had hung from the lowest cluster. Now there were three, and the torn stem still looked damp.

Grandpa set down the salt. “Mittens.”

Mittens belonged to Mrs. Alvarez next door. He was a white cat with two black ears and the steady, offended gaze of a judge. At that moment he lay on the garden wall washing one paw.

“Do cats like tomatoes?” Maya asked.

“Thieves don’t fill out menus.”

She tasted the noodles and reached for a glass of water. Grandpa tasted them too, then silently added half a cucumber and another handful of noodles to the bowl.

A paper calendar hung on the refrigerator. Grandpa had drawn a red circle around Saturday. Three men from the railroad crew he had worked with long ago were coming to lunch. All summer he had pinched side shoots from the tomato vines, tied the heavy branches, and carried water to the roots in the evening. He claimed store tomatoes were perfectly good. Still, he counted his own every morning.

He had promised his friends tomato noodles and two ripe fruits apiece to take home. At their last lunch, one man had brought homemade pickles and another had brought peach jam. Grandpa, fresh from treatment for his knee, had arrived empty-handed. He never mentioned that part. The next morning, however, he had limped into the shed and brought out his seed box.

“At this rate,” he said, looking at the bare stem, “each guest can smell a leaf.”

Maya turned her half-finished comic to a clean page. “Did you see Mittens take one?”

“A thief does not wait for an audience.”

“Then I’m writing ‘tomato missing,’ not ‘cat stole tomato.’”

Grandpa turned her pencil around and tapped the eraser on the page. “You write. I catch.”

One tomato had vanished Monday. Grandpa thought he had counted wrong. A second disappeared Wednesday, and he found white hairs under the fence. Mrs. Alvarez had brushed Mittens in her yard that afternoon; loose fur had sailed everywhere. Grandpa had kept that part to himself.

## The Washbasins

Now he fetched two old washbasins and propped them on sticks beside the tomato plants. String ran from each stick to the kitchen window.

“When Mittens touches a tomato, I pull.” He demonstrated. Both basins crashed down and captured his own hat.

“Then what?” Maya asked.

“Caught red-pawed.”

“His paws are white.”

“Get the camera.”

They watched all afternoon. The kitchen felt like a shut oven because Grandpa refused to run the fan. Maya made three columns in her notebook: time, sighting, trace. A sparrow landed at 4:13. The mail carrier passed at 4:37. At 5:02, Mittens appeared on the wall.

Grandpa gripped the strings.

The cat took two steps and froze. He was not watching the tomatoes. He stared at an overturned wooden crate near the drainpipe. Something rustled behind it.

Grandpa’s fingers twitched.

Clang!

Mittens flew over the wall. Mrs. Alvarez’s head appeared above it. “What happened?”

“A basin fell,” Maya said before Grandpa could answer.

Beside the empty trap, Grandpa found a perfect paw print in damp soil. He pointed as if he had discovered buried treasure.

Maya measured it. She found more prints by the wall, leading from Mrs. Alvarez’s yard to the crate. None reached the tomato bed.

“This proves he visited,” she said.

“A thief has to visit.”

“The mail carrier visited.”

“You think Mr. Lee stole my tomatoes?”

“No. That’s the point.”

Grandpa tucked a basin under each arm. “Tomorrow we use a trap that doesn’t faint before the criminal arrives.”

At dinner, Mrs. Alvarez passed a plate of corn fritters over the wall. Grandpa thanked her loudly, then shut the shed door on the washbasins. When she admired his tomatoes, he answered without offering her one, as he usually did.

Maya pushed the plate toward him. “If you already know who did it, why are we watching?”

Grandpa broke a crisp edge from a fritter. “So the thief can’t argue.”

“The cat can’t argue now.”

“So the cat’s owner can’t.”

The fritter in Maya’s hand stopped tasting as good. Until then, the case had felt like a summer game. Now she hoped they would catch nothing at all.

His second plan involved flour. He swept the path smooth, then poured a pale ring around the plants.

“Does Grandma know?” Maya asked.

“It’s old flour.”

“The date says next month.”

“It aged quickly.”

Rain came at midnight. By morning, the flour had become paste. No tomato was missing, but two snails were stranded in it. Grandpa carried them to the hedge on leaves, muttering that the method had only been a trial.

Maya recorded the result: rain, tracks ruined, no tomato missing. Grandpa scrubbed the path harder when he saw the word ruined.

That afternoon he called her to the loose gap under the fence. The white hairs still clung to one brick. He blocked the gap with a board and set three pebbles in front of it. By evening, the pebbles had not moved. Yet half a green tomato lay below the vines, its torn flesh marked by tiny teeth.

Grandpa began to say that the thief must already be inside the yard. Then he stopped. If Mittens had not crossed the blocked gap, the white hairs could not explain the new bite.

“Should I cross his name off?” Maya asked.

“Use a dotted line,” Grandpa said.

It was the first time he had allowed his answer to grow faint. Maya let him keep the dotted line.

## The Bean-Pod Alarm

For the third watch, he spread dry bean pods along the paths between the wall, the crate, and the vines. They would make a harmless crackle if anything passed. He and Maya hid in the tool shed with two stools and a flashlight.

After dark, the garden’s daytime noise folded away. Crickets worked in the corners. Damp soil smelled cool through the door crack. Grandpa shifted his stiff knee every few minutes, and a peppermint clicked against his teeth.

“Your mouth is louder than the bean pods,” Maya whispered.

He moved the candy into his cheek.

A white shape crossed the wall. Mittens dropped into the garden and went straight to the crate. His nose nearly touched the ground. Something gray-brown flashed behind the wood and vanished under the broad squash leaves.

Mittens froze with his ears pointed toward the leaves. Behind the crate, bean pods snapped and rattled.

Grandpa jumped up, struck his knee on the frame, and made a sound that was not quite a word. He stopped inside the doorway. The cat shot up the wall. The gray-brown animal disappeared.

Several bean pods lay crushed.

“There!” Grandpa rubbed his knee with one hand and pointed with the other. “What did I say?”

Maya crouched beside the broken pods. The cat tracks crossed the path sideways and stopped short of the leaves. Under a leaf she found two slender prints with long little toes, heading toward the squash.

“Don’t step here.”

Grandpa’s sandal hung above the print. He wobbled on one leg, then stepped back. Retreating those six inches seemed harder for him than saying a whole sentence.

Outside the shed, Grandpa picked up a broken bean pod and glanced at the wall. Mittens sat there washing his chest, as if someone else had just fled in panic.

“He was there,” Grandpa said.

“Yes.”

“And something else crossed the pods.”

“Yes.”

“But we still didn’t see what the other animal was.”

Maya wrote his words down without polishing them into a grand conclusion.

The next day, Maya showed the photographs to her science teacher. Instead of naming the animal, Ms. Green asked what lived nearby, came out at night, could hold food in its front paws, and could fit through the broken drain cover.

After school, Maya spread fine sand by the crate. She added no bait. The ripening tomatoes went into mesh bags.

Grandpa frowned at the bags. “How will we catch it if it can’t steal anything?”

“We’re trying to see it, not hire it.”

Mrs. Alvarez was hanging a sheet on the other side of the wall. Grandpa lowered his voice. “That cat was still in the garden.”

“Watching something.”

“Could be partners.”

Maya looked at him. He laughed once, then disguised it as a cough.

Before the next watch, they oiled the shed hinge. Grandpa tested it three times, but the door still gave one short squeak at the final inch. He glared at the hinge. Maya wrote, door makes noise.

“That counts as evidence?”

“If we hear a squeak tonight, we’ll know an animal may not have made it.”

Grandpa studied the notebook, borrowed her pencil, and added, peppermint also makes noise. His letters leaned like the snails escaping his flour.

## The Face in the Moonlight

Moonlight filled the tool shed that night. Mittens did not come. Near nine, a pointed nose appeared beside the crate. Round ears followed, then dark eyes set in a pale mask. A small Asian palm civet stepped out, gray-brown and cautious.

It ignored the covered tomatoes. Under the vines it found half a split fruit and held it between its front paws. After several bites, it carried the rest toward the drain.

Thin squeaks answered from inside.

“Babies?” Grandpa said, much too loudly.

The civet dropped the tomato. The shed hinge squealed as he leaned forward. In a blink she was back behind the crate.

Maya switched on the flashlight but did not approach. The broken drain opening hid under ivy. Something small moved inside. Grandpa knelt for a long moment, keeping the beam on the ground outside the hole.

“Not Mittens,” he said.

“No.”

“I never accused him to his face.”

“You accused him under Mrs. Alvarez’s window.”

Grandpa handed her the flashlight and stood. “Pick six tomatoes in the morning. The best ones.”

He did not go straight to bed. When Maya came for water, she found him at the kitchen table choosing a basket. The large one looked showy, he said. The small one looked cheap. He repaired the loose handle of the middle basket with a strip of cane.

Her notebook lay open beside him. Paw print, drag mark, and long-toed tracks faced one another across the pages. Under suspect: Mittens, Grandpa drew two heavy lines. Then he covered the words with a square of paper.

“Covering it doesn’t mean you never wrote it,” Maya said.

“I know.” He tightened the cane around the handle. “That’s why I still have to go tomorrow.”

## Six Tomatoes

At Mrs. Alvarez’s door, however, he hid the basket behind his leg. Mittens sat in her arms.

“We had a visitor,” Grandpa began. “An Asian palm civet with kits. I mistook the tracks. Also, one of my basins made a great deal of noise and frightened your cat.”

Mrs. Alvarez did not reach for the tomatoes. “I thought the basin fell by itself.”

Grandpa’s ears turned pink. Maya studied an ant crossing the step.

“I pulled early,” he said. “And I said things where you could hear them. Things I couldn’t prove. I’m sorry.”

Mrs. Alvarez accepted the basket. She held a tomato under Mittens’s nose. He recoiled so dramatically that all three people laughed.

When the laughter ended, Mrs. Alvarez set the basket by her door. “Those things you said made me angry. But I heard what you said today, too.”

Grandpa nodded. He did not explain the lunch or how much the first crop mattered. On the short walk home, his hand reached twice for the basket handle that was no longer there.

His old friends still ate tomato noodles on Saturday. After the six-tomato apology, the bowl did not look very red, so Grandpa sliced the cucumbers thin and placed the last two tomatoes on top. One friend asked about the harvest.

“I opened a case,” Grandpa said. “The fee was six tomatoes.”

Maya nudged his ankle under the table.

He looked at her and tried again. “I paid them to the neighbor I blamed.”

No one teased him. Instead, the men competed over stories of accusing the wrong dog, carrying off the wrong umbrella, and blaming rain for a leaking roof. By the end, not even the noodle broth remained.

A wildlife officer inspected the drain. The mother and kits needed time to leave, she said. The neighbors removed the crate, secured their trash, and installed a motion light away from the den. Nobody fed the civets or blocked the exit while the young were inside. Weeks later, when the family had moved on, the officer covered the broken opening.

Grandpa found two dried tomato stems beneath the crate. He dropped them into one of Maya’s evidence envelopes.

No more tomatoes vanished. Grandpa hung a small wooden sign beside the vines. On it he painted a tomato and a question mark.

“Is that to scare thieves?” Mrs. Alvarez asked over the wall.

“It’s to slow down the detective.”

Mittens leaped into the garden and sniffed the sign. Then he raised one back leg.

Grandpa rushed forward. “That evidence is not required!”

Maya laughed so hard that her notebook slipped out of her hands and landed open in the grass.
