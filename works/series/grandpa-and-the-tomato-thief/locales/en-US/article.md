# The Case of the Missing Tomatoes

Grandpa was salting a bowl of cold noodles when he noticed the empty stem. His hand stopped. The salt did not.

“Another one,” he said.

Maya looked through the kitchen window. Heat pressed the leaves flat against their stakes. Yesterday, four tomatoes had hung from the lowest cluster. Now there were three, and the torn stem still looked damp.

Grandpa set down the salt. “Mittens.”

Mittens belonged to Mrs. Alvarez next door. He was a white cat with two black ears and the steady, offended gaze of a judge. At that moment he lay on the garden wall washing one paw.

“Do cats like tomatoes?” Maya asked.

“Thieves don’t fill out menus.”

She tasted the noodles and reached for a glass of water. Grandpa tasted them too, then silently added half a cucumber and another handful of noodles to the bowl.

One tomato had vanished Monday. Grandpa thought he had counted wrong. A second disappeared Wednesday, and he found white hairs under the fence. Mrs. Alvarez had brushed Mittens in her yard that afternoon; loose fur had sailed everywhere. Grandpa had kept that part to himself.

Now he fetched two old washbasins and propped them on sticks beside the tomato plants. String ran from each stick to the kitchen window.

“When Mittens touches a tomato, I pull.” He demonstrated. Both basins crashed down and captured his own hat.

“Then what?” Maya asked.

“Caught red-pawed.”

“His paws are white.”

“Get the camera.”

They watched all afternoon. The kitchen felt like a shut oven because Grandpa refused to run the fan. Maya made three columns in her notebook: time, sighting, trace. A sparrow landed at 4:13. The mail carrier passed at 4:37. At 5:02, Mittens appeared on the wall.

Grandpa gripped the strings.

The cat took two steps and froze. He was not watching the tomatoes. He stared at an overturned wooden crate near the drainpipe. Something rustled behind it.

Grandpa’s fingers twitched.

Clang!

Mittens flew over the wall. Mrs. Alvarez’s head appeared above it. “What happened?”

“A basin fell,” Maya said before Grandpa could answer.

Beside the empty trap, Grandpa found a perfect paw print in damp soil. He pointed as if he had discovered buried treasure.

Maya measured it. She found more prints by the wall, leading from Mrs. Alvarez’s yard to the crate. None reached the tomato bed.

“This proves he visited,” she said.

“A thief has to visit.”

“The mail carrier visited.”

“You think Mr. Lee stole my tomatoes?”

“No. That’s the point.”

Grandpa tucked a basin under each arm. “Tomorrow we use a trap that doesn’t faint before the criminal arrives.”

His second plan involved flour. He swept the path smooth, then poured a pale ring around the plants.

“Does Grandma know?” Maya asked.

“It’s old flour.”

“The date says next month.”

“It aged quickly.”

Rain came at midnight. By morning, the flour had become paste. No tomato was missing, but two snails were stranded in it. Grandpa carried them to the hedge on leaves, muttering that the method had only been a trial.

For the third watch, he put one soft tomato under a tilted basket and covered the path with dry bean pods. He and Maya hid in the tool shed with two stools and a flashlight.

After dark, the garden’s daytime noise folded away. Crickets worked in the corners. Damp soil smelled cool through the door crack. Grandpa shifted his stiff knee every few minutes, and a peppermint clicked against his teeth.

“Your mouth is louder than the bean pods,” Maya whispered.

He moved the candy into his cheek.

A white shape crossed the wall. Mittens dropped into the garden and went straight to the crate. His nose nearly touched the ground. Something gray-brown flashed behind the wood and vanished under the broad squash leaves.

Mittens pounced. Bean pods snapped and rattled.

Grandpa burst from the shed, struck his knee on the frame, and made a sound that was not quite a word. The cat shot up the wall. The gray-brown animal disappeared.

The bait tomato was gone.

“There!” Grandpa rubbed his knee with one hand and pointed with the other. “What did I say?”

Maya crouched beside the basket. A drag mark ran from the tomato to the squash. The cat tracks crossed it sideways. Under a leaf she found two slender prints with long little toes.

“Don’t step here.”

The next day, Maya showed the photographs to her science teacher. Instead of naming the animal, Ms. Green asked what lived nearby, came out at night, could hold food in its front paws, and could fit through the broken drain cover.

After school, Maya spread fine sand by the crate. She added no bait. The ripening tomatoes went into mesh bags.

Grandpa frowned at the bags. “How will we catch it if it can’t steal anything?”

“We’re trying to see it, not hire it.”

Mrs. Alvarez was hanging a sheet on the other side of the wall. Grandpa lowered his voice. “That cat was still in the garden.”

“Chasing something.”

“Could be partners.”

Maya looked at him. He laughed once, then disguised it as a cough.

Moonlight filled the tool shed that night. Mittens did not come. Near nine, a pointed nose appeared beside the crate. Round ears followed, then dark eyes set in a pale mask. A raccoon stepped out, smaller and thinner than the ones Maya had seen near trash cans.

It ignored the covered tomatoes. Under the vines it found half a split fruit and held it between its front paws. After several bites, it carried the rest toward the drain.

Thin squeaks answered from inside.

“Babies?” Grandpa said, much too loudly.

The raccoon dropped the tomato. The shed hinge squealed as he leaned forward. In a blink she was back behind the crate.

Maya switched on the flashlight but did not approach. The broken drain opening hid under ivy. Something small moved inside. Grandpa knelt for a long moment, keeping the beam on the ground outside the hole.

“Not Mittens,” he said.

“No.”

“I never accused him to his face.”

“You accused him under Mrs. Alvarez’s window.”

Grandpa handed her the flashlight and stood. “Pick six tomatoes in the morning. The best ones.”

At Mrs. Alvarez’s door, however, he hid the basket behind his leg. Mittens sat in her arms.

“We had a visitor,” Grandpa began. “A raccoon with kits. I mistook the tracks. Also, one of my basins made a great deal of noise and frightened your cat.”

Mrs. Alvarez did not reach for the tomatoes. “I thought the basin fell by itself.”

Grandpa’s ears turned pink. Maya studied an ant crossing the step.

“I pulled early,” he said. “And I said things where you could hear them. Things I couldn’t prove. I’m sorry.”

Mrs. Alvarez accepted the basket. She held a tomato under Mittens’s nose. He recoiled so dramatically that all three people laughed.

A wildlife officer inspected the drain. The mother and kits needed time to leave, she said. The neighbors removed the crate, secured their trash, and installed a motion light away from the den. Nobody fed the raccoons or blocked the exit while the young were inside. Weeks later, when the family had moved on, the officer covered the broken opening.

Grandpa found two dried tomato stems beneath the crate. He dropped them into one of Maya’s evidence envelopes.

No more tomatoes vanished. Grandpa hung a small wooden sign beside the vines. On it he painted a tomato and a question mark.

“Is that to scare thieves?” Mrs. Alvarez asked over the wall.

“It’s to slow down the detective.”

Mittens leaped into the garden and sniffed the sign. Then he raised one back leg.

Grandpa rushed forward. “That evidence is not required!”

Maya laughed so hard that her notebook slipped out of her hands and landed open in the grass.
