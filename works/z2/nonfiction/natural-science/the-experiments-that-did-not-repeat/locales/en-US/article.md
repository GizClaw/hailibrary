# The Experiments That Did Not Repeat

In August twenty fifteen, Brian Nosek and hundreds of Open Science Collaboration contributors released the results of an unusually large coordinated project: independent teams had reconstructed one hundred published studies and run them again. Thirty-six percent of the replication tests crossed the conventional threshold for statistical significance. Headlines converted that number into a verdict about psychology, but Nosek and his coauthors had chosen to publish several indicators, emphasize uncertainty, and call the data an initial estimate. Those observations displaced the easy verdict with a harder question: what, exactly, had this collection of replications measured?

Replication is not photocopying. A new team recruits new participants, performs procedures again, and asks whether fresh observations support a prior claim. Even a faithful repetition cannot recreate the original sample, moment, or every social cue. Its purpose is to discover whether an effect survives variation that the claim should tolerate. Yet survival has no single mathematical meaning. Researchers may compare directions, effect sizes, confidence intervals, significance decisions, or a combined estimate. Before interpreting any replication rate, a reader must ask which criterion was used and which population of claims the project was designed to represent.

The Reproducibility Project: Psychology did not draw randomly from all psychological research. Its teams selected studies published in two thousand eight in three prominent journals, then attempted one hundred replications of key effects. The sample represented social and cognitive psychology more strongly than many other branches. This design enabled an unusually large coordinated comparison, not a census of a discipline and certainly not a measurement of all science. Its boundaries matter because a precise result about this set becomes misleading when it is retold as a universal percentage of truth.

Coordination was itself an experiment in method. At different sites, replication teams sought original materials, discussed protocols with original authors, reconstructed procedures, and planned samples with substantial power if the published effects were accurate. They had to choose what to hold constant and what local conditions could not be copied. The collaboration exposed protocols, data, and judgments for scrutiny, reducing ambiguity without erasing it. A translated instruction, participant pool, laboratory setting, or weakly reproduced manipulation could still alter an effect. Standardization made those methodological choices inspectable; it did not make human behavior a context-free constant.

The famous contrast was ninety-seven percent versus thirty-six percent. Almost all original studies reported a statistically significant key result, whereas thirty-six percent of replication tests did. Two other indicators were also sobering. Forty-seven percent of original effect sizes lay within the replication confidence interval, and the replication teams subjectively judged thirty-nine percent of effects to have replicated. These measures overlap but are not interchangeable. A confidence interval concerns compatibility with a range; a significance decision concerns a threshold under a model; a team's subjective judgment incorporates procedural and substantive information.

Magnitude shifted as well. On average, replication effects were about half the size of the original estimates. That contraction can arise when published results were selected partly because random variation made them look unusually large, a pattern often called the winner's curse. It can also reflect differences in samples, procedures, or contexts. The smaller mean therefore carried information that the thirty-six percent figure could not. Many effects did not simply switch from present to absent. Their estimated strength moved, often toward zero, with uncertainty around every estimate.

A nonsignificant result is not proof of no effect. Statistical tests respond to effect magnitude, sample size, measurement noise, and modeling assumptions. A weak but real effect may fail to cross a threshold in one study; a chance fluctuation may make an absent effect cross it in another. Conversely, not disproved is not a license to preserve every favored claim. When a careful replication produces a much smaller estimate, rational confidence should usually fall even if the evidence does not justify a binary declaration of falsehood. How far it falls depends on precision and prior evidence.

Precision changes the question. Suppose two studies estimate effects in the same direction, but the replication interval is broad because the measure is noisy. The new study may be inconclusive. If a large, well-controlled replication instead yields a narrow interval around nearly zero, it supplies stronger evidence against an effect of the originally claimed size. Both outcomes can be labeled as failed replications under a threshold rule, although they warrant different updates. Responsible interpretation asks which effect sizes remain compatible with the data, not merely which side of a line received an asterisk.

Procedure matters too, but the phrase context matters can explain too much. If a theory predicts that an effect depends on language, culture, timing, or participant expectations, those moderators should be stated and tested. If they are invented only after an unfavorable outcome, the theory becomes difficult to challenge: every success confirms it, while every failure reveals an unnamed boundary. A failed repetition can therefore expose either a false general claim or an incomplete one. Distinguishing them requires further evidence, not loyalty to the first result or the replication.

In twenty sixteen, Daniel Gilbert, Gary King, Stephen Pettigrew, and Timothy Wilson challenged the project's pessimistic interpretation. They argued that some replication protocols differed in consequential ways from the originals and that the analysis treated expected study-to-study variation too harshly. Their technical comment also questioned how the selected set supported a broad estimate of psychology's reproducibility. The criticism did not merely deny inconvenient data. It proposed a competing account: much of the apparent failure could be produced by procedural differences and statistical treatment rather than unreliable original phenomena.

The project team answered that the critique relied on assumptions its data did not support and that removing disputed cases did not erase the general decline in effect sizes. The authors emphasized that they had never offered one definitive replication rate. Their original paper had reported multiple criteria precisely because reproducibility cannot be reduced to one statistic. They also argued that protocol differences deserve empirical examination, not automatic classification as fatal flaws. A difference can matter, but claiming that it mattered in a specific case is itself a hypothesis.

The exchange revealed a deeper disagreement about what a replication project should estimate. One view treats psychological effects as sensitive relationships whose expression changes across settings; another worries that unlimited sensitivity lets theories evade severe tests. Both concerns are legitimate. Human behavior is not independent of context, yet scientific claims gain value by specifying which contexts they cover. The dispute cannot be settled by choosing universal law or everything depends. It asks researchers to predict relevant variation before seeing outcomes and to compare those predictions with data.

Academic disagreement was therefore not evidence that the project had failed. Public protocols and data allowed critics to identify disputed cases, reconstruct models, and publish counterarguments; the project team could answer on the same record. Openness made conflict more exact. It did not guarantee consensus, because statistical models encode different assumptions about selection, heterogeneity, and error. The useful question became narrower: under each defensible model, which conclusions changed, and which remained? Across the dispute, the claim that replication effects were generally smaller proved harder to dismiss than any single percentage advertised as a field-wide score.

Why might original literatures contain enlarged effects? One mechanism is analytical flexibility. Before analysis, a dataset can support many plausible decisions: when to stop collecting observations, which outliers to exclude, which outcome to emphasize, whether to add a covariate, and how to transform a measure. No single choice must be absurd or dishonest. The distortion arises when outcomes guide the choices but the final paper presents one selected path as though it had been fixed from the start. Readers then evaluate a concealed search as if it were one planned test.

Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn demonstrated this danger in twenty eleven through simulations and experiments. Flexible stopping, exclusions, outcomes, and covariates could greatly increase the chance of obtaining a conventionally significant result under a null model. The lesson was not that every researcher fabricated evidence. Repeated undisclosed searching changes the meaning of the final p-value. The calculation assumes a testing process; if the reported analysis conceals the process that selected it, readers evaluate the wrong procedure and underestimate how often chance could yield the reported result.

P-hacking became a compact name for outcome-dependent efforts to obtain significance, but the label can obscure degrees of intent. A researcher may consciously hunt for a publishable result, follow local habits without recognizing their statistical cost, or explore many patterns for legitimate discovery and then describe one as confirmation. Those conduct questions differ. The evidential problem is shared: unreported multiplicity makes a surprising result less surprising. Exploration remains valuable when it is identified as exploration and followed, where appropriate, by a new confirmatory test.

A p-value is often misinterpreted as the probability that a hypothesis is wrong. The American Statistical Association states that it is not. Under a specified statistical model, the value describes how incompatible the observed data are with that model; it does not assign truth probabilities to hypotheses, measure practical importance, or reveal effect size. Nor does passing a threshold transform a noisy estimate into a stable fact. Design quality, assumptions, missing analyses, measurement validity, and the consequences of error remain part of inference.

Correcting misuse does not require banning p-values. In twenty twenty-one, an ASA task force stressed that significance tests remain useful when properly applied and interpreted. Sound analysis must also describe uncertainty and variability, address multiplicity, and consider study design alongside other evidence. That statement resisted another seductive headline: that one flawed research culture could be repaired by deleting one statistic. Different inferential tools answer different questions and carry their own assumptions. Confidence intervals, likelihoods, Bayesian models, prediction, and decision analysis can improve reasoning, but none makes selective reporting, weak measurement, or vague theory harmless.

Publication incentives amplify selection. Journals have historically favored clear, positive, novel stories; researchers face career rewards for producing them. If null or complicated findings remain in drawers, the visible literature exaggerates success even when every individual analysis follows its stated rule. Small studies intensify the problem because only their largest chance estimates may cross a threshold. Replication then appears to shrink effects partly because the second study was not selected for having won the same sampling lottery. The mechanism is statistical and institutional at once.

One reform changes the order of decisions. In preregistration, researchers place a time-stamped, read-only plan in a repository before data collection or analysis. The record may be public immediately or kept under a temporary embargo. A useful plan specifies hypotheses, outcomes, exclusions, stopping rules, and analyses closely enough that later deviations can be recognized. It does not forbid discovery, lock scientists into known mistakes, or certify that a design is good. It preserves chronology: readers can tell which tests were proposed before outcomes and which questions emerged after the data were seen.

Preregistration works only as well as its specificity and use. A vague plan can leave nearly as much flexibility as no plan; an inaccessible plan cannot help readers; an unreported deviation restores the old ambiguity. Yet deviations are not automatically misconduct. Equipment fails, assumptions break, and unexpected structure may demand a better model. The remedy is transparent explanation, with planned and exploratory results distinguished. A timestamp does not replace judgment or guarantee truth. It gives judgment an auditable history.

Registered Reports move evaluation earlier. Editors who adopted the format chose to review the question and method before researchers knew the outcomes, then to offer in-principle acceptance to strong protocols. A null result alone would not revoke that decision. Because publication no longer depended chiefly on a dramatic answer, a tractable question and credible method carried more weight. The format is designed chiefly for confirmatory, hypothesis-driven inquiry, while still allowing researchers to report serendipitous findings separately. Reform has to match the inquiry.

Sharing materials, data, and code creates other forms of accountability, subject to privacy, consent, security, and ownership constraints. Materials help a new team reproduce procedures. Data and code let readers check whether reported analyses run as described and explore sensitivity to defensible alternatives. These resources do not prove that a measure captures its intended construct or that participants in another setting interpret it similarly. Transparency makes the research process inspectable; it does not guarantee that the chosen measures answer the right question.

Better measurement may matter more than a larger sample. If a questionnaire changes meaning across groups, precision around its score cannot rescue the intended inference. If an experimental manipulation barely changes the proposed mental state, a null replication may test implementation rather than theory. Conversely, repeated positive results with the same biased instrument can reproduce the bias. Replicability, validity, and generalizability overlap but are not synonyms. A method can be repeatable and wrong, valid in one population but not another, or too imprecise to decide.

Direct replication holds the original method as constant as practical and asks whether the result recurs. Conceptual replication changes operations while testing the same theoretical relation. Direct attempts diagnose whether a published procedure can deliver its result again; conceptual attempts probe whether the explanation survives new implementations. Neither is inherently superior. A conceptual failure may indict the theory or merely introduce a weak operation. A direct success may establish a narrow regularity without explaining it. Strong programs use both and state which claim each attempt can revise.

Multiple laboratories can separate local accident from broader pattern. Teams agree on a protocol, recruit across sites, and examine whether effects vary with measured contexts. Large collaborations improve precision and reveal heterogeneity, but shared procedures can also share a blind spot. If every site uses the same narrow population or flawed instrument, agreement does not establish universality. Variation should be designed, documented, and analyzed rather than treated as noise only after results diverge. Geographic breadth matters only when relevant differences become part of inference.

Meta-analysis combines estimates across studies, weighting them by precision under an explicit model. It can show that a literature contains a small average effect, substantial heterogeneity, or uncertainty too large for a confident summary. It can also inherit publication bias, incompatible measures, and weak designs. Combining ten biased studies does not manufacture an unbiased answer. A responsible synthesis examines study quality, selection, and sensitivity, then reports the range of conclusions supported under plausible assumptions rather than hiding judgment beneath numerical authority.

When a result does not repeat, the follow-up should be proportional to the claim's importance and the evidence's ambiguity. Investigators can compare protocols, verify code, inspect manipulation checks, estimate precision, and design replications that separate competing explanations. In an adversarial collaboration, researchers with opposing expectations choose a discriminating test together before observing its result. They must decide which outcome would count against each position. Agreement about the protocol cannot force agreement about theory, but it makes it harder for either side to move every criterion afterward.

Reforms also alter who bears costs and who can participate. Open data enables checking, but privacy and consent can restrict what should be released. Large collaborations distribute expertise; when their labor is largely voluntary, however, researchers without funded time are easier to exclude. A careful individual practice may also lose force when institutions continue rewarding novelty instead of rigor. Registered Reports, already represented in published work by twenty fourteen, show that reform developed through a broader movement rather than appearing after one replication project. Evaluating change therefore requires evidence about resources, incentives, and behavior.

The label replication crisis does not identify a single defect. The evidence revealed weak incentives, small samples, selective reporting, flexible analysis, measurement problems, and underspecified theories in different combinations. It also revealed a field capable of organizing expensive self-criticism in public. Calling the episode either total collapse or complete vindication discards the most useful evidence. The relevant unit of confidence is not psychology as a whole but a particular claim supported by a particular chain of studies.

Non-replication changes that chain without cutting it at one stroke. A precise contradiction should reduce confidence more than an underpowered ambiguity. Repeated, preregistered results across relevant settings should count more than one selected success. A theory that predicts its boundary conditions earns more than one that explains every outcome afterward. These are not mechanical scores. They are disciplined comparisons that keep uncertainty visible while allowing decisions. Science advances because claims can be revised, narrowed, connected to context, or abandoned.

The one hundred replications from twenty fifteen did not deliver a census of truth. They supplied a common record on which different theories of scientific error could collide. Along with earlier debates and other large replication projects, that argument became part of a broader credibility movement associated with new journal formats, reporting practices, and expectations of transparency; Registered Reports, for example, already had documented publications by twenty fourteen. The causal history is collective, not the achievement of one project. Which effects travel, which depend on context, and which were enlarged by selection remain empirical questions. A mature response begins with a better specified next test.
