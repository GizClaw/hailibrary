For her Saturday kitchen group’s booklet, Derya chose the flatbreads her grandfather made when the family came round. She thought she could copy out his recipe before lunch. On the back of a grocery list he had written, “Turn the bread when it’s ready.” She read the line twice. The words had always seemed clear while she was standing next to him.

“Can we print it?” she asked. Her grandfather said yes and went to open a window. Derya sat back down, but her pencil stayed above the page. She had watched him lift the edge of a flatbread almost without looking. He was usually talking at the time. If she used his sentence in the booklet, a reader halfway through making dinner would need to know what he had noticed.

She thought of writing “turn after two minutes”. That was how long the bread had taken the previous time she helped. Today the first one took longer. He had moved the pan to a smaller ring while he warmed another pot of soup. Neither of them had thought to write that down.

Derya began again on a clean page. She clipped the grocery list along one edge and wrote in the margin, “What changes first?” When her grandfather finished serving, she asked him to show her one more flatbread. This time she stood where she could see both the pan and his hands.

He invited her to do the turning. The first time she tried, the dough dragged against the pan. She waited, lifted the edge again, and saw small brown marks on the underside. It came away easily. She wrote down both things. Then she drew a line from her note to the old sentence, which was still there in her grandfather’s handwriting.

## Questions

1. Why does Derya hesitate to publish the recipe even after getting permission to copy it?
2. What is the effect of leaving a question in the margin instead of immediately filling the gap?

## Answers

1. She realises the instruction leaves out a judgement her grandfather makes from experience. Permission to copy his words does not make them enough for a new cook.
2. It makes the missing knowledge visible and gives Derya a reason to watch and test the cooking before she supplies an answer of her own.

**Leni’s page · Starting with a number**

I chose to weigh the flour for our apple pancakes. My neighbour has a set of metal cups that fit inside each other. I have a large blue mug that makes her cups look tiny. When I followed her instructions with my mug, I made more batter than my bowl could hold. I weighed what went into the next bowl and wrote down 150 grams of flour as a starting point.

The number gives readers a chance to make something close to what I made. It doesn’t tell them how much juice will come out of their particular apple. I also wrote, “Draw a line through the mixture with a spoon. It should stay open for a moment.” Our first reader asked me to demonstrate what “a moment” looked like. We took two photographs a second apart. In the first you could see the bottom of the bowl. In the next, the line was beginning to close.

**Mateo’s page · Starting with what you have**

The first person I made pancakes with was my older sister, in a kitchen with no scale. We filled a cup with flour, then used it again to measure half a cup of milk. We added a little more milk if the thick mixture came off the spoon in one heavy piece. When it began to fall slowly in a wide ribbon, we stopped pouring. My sister used to hold the spoon over my bowl and let me decide when it was ready.

I wanted my page to work for people with an ordinary cup and a spoon. If four people have asked for breakfast, though, I need some idea of how much I’m making. So I added a note under the drawing of my cup. It holds 200 millilitres and that batch made eight small pancakes. People can judge the mixture with their eyes and still know roughly how far it will go.

## Questions

1. What assumption do both recipe writers make about their readers?
2. For a first-time reader with a scale, which instructions would be easier to repeat and why?

## Answers

1. Both assume readers can interpret at least one sign while cooking. Leni expects them to recognise a line staying open, and Mateo expects them to judge how the batter falls from the spoon.
2. Leni gives the flour weight in grams, so the reader can reproduce that starting point with a scale. The photographs help with the next decision about the batter.

**Rima’s notebook**

I drew a pea in the corner of the label and wrote **Seasonal Green Rice** in my best handwriting. We had offered to serve one dish at a youth kitchen lunch, and this was going to be the first thing visitors saw. The label looked cheerful. If anyone had asked what made the dish seasonal, I would have pointed to the bright green bowl.

Someone did ask. A girl setting out the forks wanted to know where we had found fresh peas so early in the year. I showed her the bag from the freezer. “From the freezer,” I heard myself say. That told her where I'd fetched them. I had no idea when they'd been picked. I knew where the leafy greens in the bowl had come from. A neighbour had brought them that morning. As for the peas, the bag gave their weight and cooking time and very little else. By lunch I had taken the word “seasonal” off the card.

**Tomas’s note for the serving table**

The neighbour, Mrs Venn, had delivered the greens in a washing basket lined with a clean towel. She'd told me to wash the leaves well, then asked if anyone would be able to see them once they were mixed into the rice. I said there would be a label mentioning her greens. I had made that promise while Rima was still drawing her pea.

The quickest solution would have been to write **Green Rice**. That would have left Mrs Venn with nothing on the label to show which part of the meal she'd grown. I made a larger card with space for two lines. Under the name of the dish I wrote **Peas and spring greens. The greens were grown by Mrs Venn.** There was room for Rima’s pea in the corner. When Mrs Venn came for lunch, she read the second line aloud to the friend who had brought her.

## Questions

1. Why does Rima remove the word “seasonal” from the label?
2. What does Tomas want to preserve when he rewrites the label?

## Answers

1. The question about fresh peas showed that the label implied more than Rima knew. She could name where the greens came from, and the peas had come from the freezer.
2. He wants to keep the supplier’s contribution visible. He names the neighbour and the greens she supplied without making her responsible for every ingredient.

**Suri**

The trial dish was a bowl of soft beans with crisp onions on top. In the sample photograph, I had put on enough onions to cover most of the beans. On paper, the cost of making the dish for thirty had nearly reached our limit by the time I came to the onions. I crossed out the quantity, wrote half the amount and called that a fair compromise. Everyone would still get some of the crisp topping I liked.

Kel asked if I could remember how many people had actually tried it. I knew he had taken a bowl, but I hadn’t seen what he’d eaten. He told me he had spent most of the lunch picking the onions off his spoon. “You could have told me,” I said. He pointed to my photograph, which I had proudly shown around before we began eating.

**Kel**

I suggested buying a small amount at first and putting it in a bowl on the side. If it went quickly, we could use the rest of the money for another bag next time. I was nervous about asking Suri to change a photograph she'd worked so hard on. Suri put the onions out anyway. She kept half the onions back in a second bowl in case the first one ran low. At the end of lunch the second bowl was still full, though I saw a few people go back for more.

**Suri’s note on the final page**

We photographed the beans in the bowl they were served in. You can see a small dish of onions beside them. The method still tells you how to make the topping. It now says “if you like”, and there’s space to show another version when somebody comes up with one. I used the money we saved on the next pot of beans.

## Questions

1. Why does Kel argue against putting a little of the topping on every serving?
2. What does the full second bowl reveal, and why does Suri retain the original suggestion in the booklet?

## Answers

1. A small amount on every serving sounds fair to Suri, but someone who dislikes it would still have to take it. Kel wants people to decide for themselves and suggests testing demand before buying it.
2. The first bowl was enough even though a few people took seconds. That tells Suri about the amount used. She keeps the method for readers who enjoy the topping.

**Derya’s kitchen notebook**

When I first tested the bean and lemon soup, it made six bowls. For the next lunch I doubled every amount on the page and left my plan on the kitchen table. Someone had written “How many pots?” across the top by the time I came back. I drew a single large pot beside the question and went back to adding up the ingredients. Our timings were printed along the bottom of the page in a straight line.

On Saturday, the large pot barely fitted under the tap. It took much longer than I expected to get the soup hot. I could see bubbles near the sides while the liquid in the middle was barely moving. When the first six visitors sat down, I was still stirring. Across the room, the cook who'd pencilled in the question pointed at her watch. I held up one finger. Another minute, I hoped.

It took me another half hour to see what a second pot might solve. When we brought out all twelve bowls, the visitors came to the same narrow table at once. The two people serving had to reach past each other. One woman stepped out of the queue to let someone through and ended up waiting at the back.

Next time we used two ordinary pots, with the same amount in each as I’d tested. We used a separate ring for each and started the second pot a little later. That gave the servers time to serve the first six visitors, and word reached the back of the room that more soup was coming. The visitors who wanted to talk a little longer did so before lining up. The soup page in the booklet still serves six. In the margin there’s a drawing of two pots, with a short space between them.

## Questions

1. Which problem would still exist even if Derya used a bigger pot?
2. Why does making two separate batches help the team in two ways?

## Answers

1. A bigger pot wouldn’t add space at the narrow serving table. People would still gather there when all the servings appeared at once.
2. It lets the cooks keep using the tested amount per pot, and serving one batch at a time spreads out the queue and gives the servers room to work.

**Leni’s email to the youth lunch group**

The potato cakes on our page were mixed in an electric mixer. I’d like to demonstrate them in the three halls that have asked us for a workshop. We could bring the mixer from the youth kitchen and set it on the long table. That would save time for the group and everyone would get a chance to help shape a cake. I can send the hall managers a list of what people should bring if we book soon.

**Niko’s reply from Hare Hall**

Our long table is usually kept near the wash sink. The room’s only socket where we can use an appliance is on the far side, at the caretaker’s desk. I’d have to arrange a place for your mixer and time to move the furniture. Before we choose a date, could you tell me whether the mixer is essential? The visitors we’re hoping to reach have asked for something they can repeat at home. A family with a sink and a table but little work space might have the same difficulty we do.

**Eva’s test card**

I brought cooked potatoes from the youth kitchen and tried mashing them with a spoon. The pieces left by the spoon were bigger than the ones the mixer made. They still held together once I’d shaped them. We’ll put the spoon method on the main page and show the mixer as another option. There’s one job to agree on before the visit. Someone at the youth kitchen has to have the cooked potatoes ready before the workshop begins. The visitors will mash and shape them in the hall. Leni offered to cook the potatoes that morning. Niko could leave the long table exactly where it was.

## Questions

1. What does Niko’s reply reveal about the cost of Leni’s original plan?
2. What condition still matters for the version in Eva’s test card?

## Answers

1. The mixer saves the cooks some work, but Hare Hall would have to find space by its only suitable socket, move furniture and allow time for the extra setup.
2. Someone at the youth kitchen needs to cook the potatoes in advance. The group uses the space in the hall for shaping them.

**Yaz’s draft for the soup page**

I called it Friday Soup because my aunt makes it on that day. The words have the same feeling for me as the sound of her front door when I get back from school. I wanted the title to keep that feeling. When I read it aloud to her, she said, “That’s ours,” and asked me how many pages we were printing. I took this as approval of the name. I didn’t ask what the name might suggest to anyone else.

**Imra’s note at the tasting**

The dish called Friday Soup was the one I kept walking past until I asked about it. We were serving lunch on Saturday, and for a while I thought the label had been kept from an earlier event. I had come hoping to try something with lentils, which I’d only ever had at home. The name gave me no reason to look for them there. The label never mentioned them. When Yaz gave me a spoonful, I asked to see the recipe. I'd just called my dish Carrot Salad. It hadn't cost me a thing to choose that name. When Yaz hesitated over my suggestion of “Lentil and Lemon Soup”, I began to see her problem. That Friday name belonged to her aunt as much as to her.

**Yaz’s revised page**

The soup is on the menu as **Lentil and Lemon Soup**. The line immediately below says **We call it Friday Soup at Aunt Farah’s**. My aunt was there when we put the new label out. A visitor saw the second line and asked her about Fridays. She told him about carrying the pot to her sister’s flat after work. Then he asked if she always used lemon. She said she liked the soup best the way her sister made it, with a little extra. I added that to the margin after lunch.

## Questions

1. What does Imra first think “Friday Soup” means, and why does Imra walk past it?
2. What job does each line on the new label do?

## Answers

1. Imra thinks it may be an old label left over from Friday. Although Imra wants lentils, the label gives no clue that they are in the soup.
2. The first line says what the dish is. The second keeps the family name and tells readers who uses it.

**Derya**

We gave out the first booklet at the youth lunch. The copy I kept looked exactly the way I had wanted. Every title started at the same height. There was room to draw a little line around the ingredients. I put it in a folder so the cover wouldn’t get bent. A month later, one of the families we cooked with brought their copy back. The corner of the flatbread page was folded, and there was an arrow from the words about the underside of the bread to a note in the margin. “Hard to see in our pan,” the note said.

My first thought was to fix the sentence and print the booklet again. I set the clean copy beside the used one. A smaller title would leave space for an extra line. I had my ruler out before it occurred to me to ask what that line should say.

**Mateo**

I read the note about the pan, then turned the page and found a breakfast count next to my pancake recipe. “Good for three hungry people,” somebody had written. Right below it there was another note in a different pen. “Four with fruit.” I’d spent half a morning working out what a serving was supposed to mean. The two notes made me wonder what the readers had put on the table with their pancakes. I asked Derya if we could invite them to cook with us before we went back to the printer.

**Derya**

We put the used copy out at the next lunch. A family agreed to bring the kind of pan they used at home. I said I would do the turning, then asked their daughter if she would show me how she did it. She tested whether the bread would lift without sticking. That clue was already on our page. We underlined it so the next reader could find it sooner. When I put the booklet away, my thumb caught the folded corner. This time I left it as it was.

## Questions

1. What does the folded corner suggest about how readers used the booklet?
2. Why does Derya change her mind about printing the next edition immediately?

## Answers

1. It suggests someone returned to that page while using the booklet. Along with the notes, it shows that readers tried the recipes in real kitchens and had questions of their own.
2. Mateo helps her see the notes as evidence to test with readers. She decides to work through them in a cooking session before fixing the wording for a new edition.
