On Wednesdays I did the washing while Mum got my little brother ready for bed. The machines at the end of Ferry Street took thirty-eight minutes, which was almost exactly the length of one episode of the show I usually watched on my phone. If my headphones were dead, I read whatever was in my schoolbag. On the first Wednesday in March, my headphones were dead and the only thing in my bag was a maths test I had already failed.

An older man by the front window was setting up a chessboard on a low table. The spare chair had a folded newspaper on it. He moved the paper when he saw me looking.

“I used to play with my cousin,” I said. “I never got much good.”

“We can stop when the big machine starts its spin cycle,” he said. “Then you can tell everyone you were winning.” His name was Arun. He'd come to wash the thick blankets from his dog’s bed.

I took ages to make a move, then lost a piece on my third turn. On my sixth, I saw his hand go towards a piece and then stop. I thought he was going to let me keep mine. Instead, he took his glasses off and asked what I would do if I were playing his side. I looked again. There was a much better move for him, and it would have ended the game. He waited until I saw it. By then our machines were shaking. He dropped the pieces back in the box. I was still thinking about that sixth move as I pulled the washing out.

The next Wednesday I went back with half a basket of washing, a full phone battery and, for once, no headphones.

## Questions

1. Why does Ines agree to try a game with Arun?
2. What does the last line tell us about the way Ines sees her next visit?

## Answers

1. Her headphones are dead, and Arun offers a game they can stop when the machine starts to spin. His invitation makes it easy to try without committing to a full game.
2. She is already planning to come back for chess. The half-empty basket shows that washing alone is no longer the main reason to go.

The week after my second game, a square of paper appeared in the launderette window. The owner, Bea, had written **CHESS ON WEDNESDAYS** in letters big enough to read from the bus stop. By seven o’clock, a girl in a green school jacket was sitting in my chair. She moved over as soon as she saw my washing bag.

“I’m Etta,” she said. “Do you mind if I stay and watch? I’ve only played online.”

I told her I had only played here twice and put my bag on the floor. When Arun arrived, he had a leaflet about a Saturday team event at the Ferry Street Community Centre. Teams of four would play one another. At the end, the winners would get a medal each and everyone could stay for soup.

“Last year I sat outside and watched people coming out with bowls,” he said. “They looked as if they’d had a better afternoon than I had.”

Etta read the leaflet twice. “Could we enter from here?” She once played six games on her phone in one evening, she told us, and didn’t speak to a single person. She liked the idea of playing across an actual table. There were only three of us, though, and the entry form was due in two weeks.

Bea offered another square of paper for the window. I started to write **PLAYERS NEEDED**, then looked at the words from the other side of the glass. I wouldn’t have opened the door for that sign. I turned the paper over. **COME AND TRY ONE GAME**, I wrote. Underneath, in smaller letters, I added, **You can leave when your washing’s done**.

## Questions

1. How does Etta’s reason for joining differ from Arun’s?
2. Who might come in because of the last line on Ines's sign?

## Answers

1. Etta wants to play with other people after playing online on her own. Arun has watched people enjoy the community event and wants to take part this time.
2. Someone who is curious about chess but unsure about joining a team can come in for a single game and leave when the washing is done.

For a week the sign brought us two people who wanted to know how much it cost to wash a blanket. Then, one evening, a girl called Safa came in with a school jumper to dry. She put it in the dryer, counted out her coins and pressed the green button. Etta shifted her chair to make room.

“I’m in a hurry,” Safa said. She kept looking at the board while the jumper went round. When Arun moved his queen, Safa gave the smallest shake of her head. Etta asked what she’d seen. “If your knight goes there, what happens to his queen?” she asked me. Arun took one look and laughed. “I wondered when somebody would notice.”

Etta asked Safa to choose where to put her own pieces for the next game. She put half of them back in the box and showed us a way her uncle used to practise with just a few pieces. Soon Safa had taught me how to finish a game with a king and a rook, the piece that looks like a tower. I’d known how the pieces moved for years. That ending was new to me.

When the dryer stopped, I showed her the leaflet. “We’re still looking for a fourth person,” I said.

Safa pulled on her jumper. “I help at my family’s fruit stall on Saturdays.” She folded the leaflet into her pocket and dropped a spare coin in Bea's charity tin. She had reached the door when she turned back. “What time does the first game actually start?”

## Questions

1. Why does Etta ask Safa to choose where to put her own pieces?
2. What does Safa’s question about the start time suggest?

## Answers

1. She notices that Safa can already play. She offers Safa a way to join the practice as a capable player instead of assuming she needs a beginner’s lesson.
2. She is interested in playing if she can arrive after work. Her earlier answer about being busy was a real timing concern.

The games would start at two. Safa finished on the stall at half past one, but only if her brother got there in time to take over. “He usually does,” she said on Wednesday. Our entry form was due at eight that evening, and there was only one team place left. I opened the form on my phone and started filling it in while she carried a bag of clean clothes to the door.

“Ferry Street Wash,” I read out when she returned. “Arun, Etta, Ines and Safa.”

Safa stopped with one hand in her coat pocket. “Have you asked my brother?”

“No. You said he usually gets there.”

“He works a shift before he comes to us. I still have to ask him.” She waited beside the table while I cleared her name off the form. The clock above the change machine showed a quarter to eight. I wanted to point at it. Instead, I asked if I could send her the full times so she and her brother could work out which part of her shift he would need to cover. She gave me her number. I sent the times and waited while she stepped out to make a call.

At five to eight my phone buzzed. From outside the shop she'd sent a photo of the stall rota. Her brother had marked his own name beside hers at one o’clock. Underneath she wrote, “Yes. Can you add me now?” I typed her name for the second time. Same four names on the screen. This time, when I pressed send, Safa was at the door grinning at me.

## Questions

1. Why does Safa object even though Ines is pleased about filling the form?
2. What does Ines change about the way she asks Safa to join?

## Answers

1. Ines has written her name down before she agrees. Safa still needs to ask her brother when he can get to the stall.
2. She takes Safa’s name off the online form and sends her the full times so Safa can decide after checking with her brother.

The centre lent us a chess clock for our last Wednesday before the event. It had two buttons on top and two little screens. Each player started with fifteen minutes. When you finished a move, you pressed your button to stop your time and start the other player’s. The screen counted down so silently that I could almost forget it, until I looked up in the middle of a move and found three minutes gone.

My first game with the clock lasted six minutes. I moved the wrong piece twice because I was thinking more about the button than the board. “I wish we could leave this thing at home,” I said. I watched Etta turn it round in her hands. I expected her to tell me I would get used to it.

Instead she pushed the full board away and took two pawns, one white and one black. She put the white pawn near one edge and the black pawn near the other, each pointing towards the far end. “Let’s just move these until they reach the end,” she said. We moved one square at a time and pressed a button after each move. On our second try my hand went straight to the button without my having to look. Then Etta gave me back the rest of the pieces and started a real game.

When it was Arun's turn to practise, I remembered which hand he'd moved his rook with. I set the clock on that side, to his left. Etta looked at me.

“Rook, glasses, clock,” I said, trying out the move with my left hand. “He's quicker with this one.” Arun tried the button once. Then he moved it a little closer. We all played again until Bea turned off the lights above the back machines.

## Questions

1. What does Etta’s small exercise show Ines about the clock?
2. Why does Ines suggest putting the clock by Arun’s left hand?

## Answers

1. After practising the same simple moves twice, Ines can press it without thinking about it. It can become part of her routine.
2. Ines has noticed Arun moves his rook with his left hand. With the clock on that side he can move and press the button with the same hand.

The community centre smelled of soup long before anyone was allowed to touch it. In the main room there were six long tables. The names of the teams were on folded cards. I found **FERRY STREET WASH** and sat opposite a chair with no one in it.

The girl next to the empty chair was still wearing her bike helmet. Her team’s fourth player, her younger brother, was on his way up from a music lesson. She had called him from the door. He said he was at the crossing with his cello, waiting for the light to change.

At two o'clock the woman running the event lifted her clipboard. “A couple of boards aren't ready. We'll start the first round at two minutes past,” she said. Any team with a missing player would lose a point on that board. Arun didn’t say anything, but he looked at the wall clock and then at me. At first that point seemed worth having. I could lose to anyone by making one careless move, and I wanted a result that would make the others glad I had come.

Then I thought about Safa’s message on Wednesday. I put up my hand and asked whether all the boards could wait two minutes longer. The woman checked with the teams beside us. A few players were still getting into their seats, so she agreed to start at four minutes past two.

The boy came in carrying a cello case almost as big as the door. “Am I too late?” he asked. I moved my coat off the chair next to him so he had somewhere to put his case. “I've waited this long. You'd better give me a game.” He set down the cello and took off his coat. At our board, nobody had touched the clock yet.

## Questions

1. Why is the empty chair a problem for the other team?
2. Why does Ines's joke sound different from what she first thought about the empty chair?

## Answers

1. Their fourth player has not arrived. If the first games begin without him, they will lose a point on that board.
2. At first she wanted an easy point for her team. Now she's glad her opponent has arrived and is looking forward to playing him.

We played three rounds that afternoon. In the last round I had more pieces left than my opponent, a quiet boy whose red sleeves kept sliding over his hands. He asked if I would agree to a draw. That would give each of us half a point. I said I wanted to play on. Arun was still in the middle of a long game, and a full point for us seemed possible if I kept going.

Two minutes later I still had more pieces. I also had just under a minute on my clock. The boy traded queens and pulled his sleeves back. There were only a few pieces left on each side now. I saw a way to take one of his pawns, but I would have had to think through what happened next. I could already feel myself reaching for the piece too fast.

I took my hand away, checked that my king was safe, and moved him one square. Before I pressed my clock, I asked, “Would you still take a draw?” He looked carefully at my clock first. He could have made me play on. Then he held out his hand and we paused the clock.

I was still thinking about the extra half point I might have won when the woman put up the results. Our team was in the middle of the list. The team below us had exactly half a point less. Etta was waiting with a bowl of soup. I put mine on the bench beside hers and went back to find the boy in the red sleeves. I wanted him to show me what he had seen when he first offered the draw.

## Questions

1. Why does Ines first turn down a draw?
2. How does the end of the game affect Ines’s view of the point she earned?

## Answers

1. She still thinks she can win her game and earn a full point for the team.
2. She's still wondering about a win, but the result puts their team half a point above the next one. She goes back to ask what her opponent saw on the board.

The following Wednesday the four of us met at the launderette again. The leaflet had fallen off the window and was curled up on the floor. Our names were still on the back in Etta’s handwriting. I put it in my bag. Bea asked if we wanted her to take the other sign down as well.

Before I answered, two children came in behind their dad. They were twins. One had already put a hand on the empty chair by our board. “Can we both play?” she asked. “We’ve been waiting since last week.” There had been no room for them while we were practising for the event. I remembered seeing their dad fold shirts slowly near the window.

I asked Bea about the folding table behind the door. She brought it out while Etta fetched an extra board from her bag. At the first table Safa set up a game against Arun. At the second, the twins put out their pieces in the wrong order, took them all off the board and started again. I sat beside them with the clock turned off. When one of them almost lost her queen, I asked what move she would make from the other side. Her sister stood up. They both walked round the table to look.

My washing had finished ten minutes earlier. I emptied the machine for the next customer, put my bag by the door and helped Bea find a third chair. Then I picked up the sign from the window and moved it a little to the left. Now, from the bus stop, you could see both tables.

## Questions

1. Why does Ines ask for a second table after the event?
2. How does Ines pass on something Arun did for her in the first chapter?

## Answers

1. There wasn't room for the twins at the first board. A second one lets someone join a game without having to replace a person at the first table.
2. She asks the twins to look at the board from the other player’s side and lets them discover the better move, as Arun did with her.
