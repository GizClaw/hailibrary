When her class was preparing for the science fair, Saira thought the best bridge would be the prettiest one. She and her partner, Luka, made a long paper bridge with blue stars along the sides. At the next table, their classmates had made a plain bridge from folded paper. During a practice run, the plain bridge held twelve coins, but Saira’s bridge fell after only six. Saira wanted to change its design straight away.

Luka looked at the equipment. The gaps between the books were different, and so were the two kinds of paper. “We can’t compare those results,” he said. He suggested a new method for the experiment. They would use the same equipment, cut equal sheets from the same paper, and stack identical coins in the middle. They would measure the gap before every test. To keep the test fair, they would change one thing at a time. This time, the only difference would be whether the paper was flat or folded.

Saira kept a record of the number of coins each bridge could hold. They tested both designs three times with fresh paper. Each time, the folded bridge held more coins. Their conclusion fitted on one line. More coins stayed up on the folded bridges in these tests. It also helped Saira improve her own bridge. She folded the paper before she drew new stars on it.

On the day of the fair, a younger pupil asked whether longer bridges always broke first. Saira took out the record and showed him the notes from the first test and the second one. “We haven’t tested that yet,” she said. She gave him two books and asked what he would do first. He moved the books a little closer and reached for the ruler.

**Tess.** Good. We can put the books back on the marks every time. Let’s use the same equipment for both tests.

**Tess.** Later. We should change one thing at a time. For this experiment, let’s use the same paper and only change the shape.

**Amir.** All right. I’ll measure the gap and build the two bridges. Can you count the coins and keep a record of each result?

**Tess.** I can. Let’s try each design three times. If the same bridge does better every time, we’ll have something to show on the poster.

Eli was a reporter for the school magazine. One Monday, he heard that the library was going to take away a large table by the window. He often sat there with his friends, and he knew other students liked it too. He wrote a headline for his next article before speaking to anyone. “Our Favourite Table Is Going,” it said. He even took a photograph of the table while it was still empty.

Before the magazine could publish the article on its website, a friend asked Eli how he knew the table was leaving. His source was a message from a pupil who'd seen someone measuring the table. The message said nothing about taking it away. Eli decided to check the facts. He went to interview the librarian after lunch. She explained that the table was staying. The library wanted to move it closer to the door so more students could reach it easily. A smaller table would go by the window instead.

Eli’s own opinion was that the window was the best place to work. But the article needed to give both sides a chance to speak. He talked to a student who often had trouble finding a place to sit with her friends. She liked the idea of a larger table near the door. Another said she'd miss spreading her drawings out by the window. The smaller table might be fine for reading, she said, but her sheets of paper were huge.

Eli changed his headline to “A New Place for the Big Table.” He put the photograph next to a simple drawing of the new layout. His friend offered to print a copy for the library. On Friday, Eli found it beside the window. A student was looking at the drawing and pointing out where she wanted to sit. This time he could tell her which table was wide enough for her drawings.

On the morning after a school play, Becca found a blue sports bag under a seat in the hall. Her friend Mina had been looking for a missing blue bag the day before. Becca was about to contact her when she remembered that half the class carried the same kind of bag. She took it to the lost property desk so the school office could help find the owner.

The assistant asked Becca for a description. The bag was blue, with a white line along the side. A short piece of red string was tied to the zip. “Let’s check the small pocket for a name,” she said. There was no name, but there was a paper label with a drawing of a rabbit. They decided to leave one detail out of the notice. It mentioned the string and said where the bag was found. Anyone who thought it might belong to them could contact the office and describe what was in the pocket. The assistant put the bag in a cupboard with the other valuable things people had handed in.

At lunch, Mina came to the office. She recognised the red string but said there was a library card in her bag, with her name on it. After school, a younger pupil called Theo came in. He described the rabbit and explained that his sister had drawn it before the play. The bag did belong to him. He had left it under his seat after changing into his costume.

Later, Becca returned to the office to ask if there had been any news. Theo was just leaving with his bag. On the way out they passed the music room. Mina waved her own blue bag at them. “Left it on the piano stool!” she called. There was red string on that zip too.

On Friday, a group of students was going to a museum for a history project. Their destination was Northgate, two stations beyond Hill Road. It was the first time the students had planned the route themselves. Ava checked the timetable the night before and saw that they had four minutes to change trains at Hill Road. That did not seem long, but the teacher had told them to allow extra time for the trip. Even if they missed their connection, they could catch the next train and still arrive before their guide was due to meet them.

At the station, the students heard an announcement. Their train had a twenty-minute delay because there was a problem on the line. Passengers could go to platform two and take a train to East Park that would leave sooner. Ava found East Park on the map. There was a bus from there to the museum, but it only ran twice an hour. The group had to decide whether to wait for the delayed train or take the bus route.

A second announcement came over the loudspeaker. The train to Hill Road was cancelled. The students went to platform two. Before they got on, Ava called the museum and arranged to meet the guide fifteen minutes later. The guide told her which bus stop to use. It was nearer to the museum entrance than the train station, so the group did not have to walk as far.

They reached the museum just as the guide came outside. On the way home, Ava wrote a note for next year’s class. Under her original timetable, she drew the bus route and added the number to call. Under East Park she wrote, “The bus only comes twice an hour. Check it before you leave.” That was the detail she wished she'd written down on Thursday night.

**Zac.** Did you hear the announcement? A thirty-minute delay on the train from platform three. If we wait, will we make our connection at West End?

**Zac.** Isn’t that a longer journey? The teacher asked us to allow extra time, but we only have forty minutes before our museum tour starts.

**Ivy.** It’s a short train journey. The problem is the bus from East Park. It could take another thirty minutes if we just miss one.

**Zac.** Listen. They’ve cancelled our train. The announcement is asking passengers for East Park to go to platform two.

**Ivy.** Then let’s catch the next train to East Park. Can you tell the teacher about the change while I call the museum?

**Zac.** Yes. Can you arrange for us to join the next tour if we arrive late?

**Ivy.** I’ll ask. If they can’t move our tour, perhaps we can look round on our own and meet the guide after lunch.

**Zac.** Good idea. Shall we meet everyone by the big clock on platform two?

**Ivy.** Yes. Give them the new platform number first. We can explain the rest on the train.
