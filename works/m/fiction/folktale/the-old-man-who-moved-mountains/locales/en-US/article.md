# Old Man Yu and the Two Roads

An old Chinese legend begins in a village north of two enormous mountains, Taihang and Wangwu. The villagers' fields and market town lay south of the peaks. Reaching them meant climbing a road that curved around both mountains.

Toward evening, a neighbor dragged an empty cart home, too tired to stand straight. An elder everyone called Old Man Yu studied the dark mountains. They made every trip south harder, an obstacle the villagers could not avoid. “Could we open a passage between the peaks?” he wondered aloud.

At home, Yu asked his family to consider the idea. His wife, Lian, set an empty stone basket between them. “Your strength is not endless,” she said. “Where would we put everything we cut loose?”

The next morning, Yu chose the lowest place on the mountain wall. Lian filled a basket and lifted it once. They could carry the stone to a distant shore. Every trip from the mountain would take time.

A second elder, Old Man Zhi, arrived with his own handcart. Some of his household goods were tied to it. “There is another way,” he told Yu. “Move your family to the southern side. After a few hard trips, the move would be done. We may not live long enough to cut through a mountain.”

Yu did not push the suggestion aside. “I want to see how hard one trip is before I choose,” he said. In the early light, Zhi steered his partly loaded cart while Yu and Lian pushed. The road climbed and dropped while the peaks hid the south.

Near sunset, the three reached open ground beyond the mountains. Their aching shoulders showed how hard the safe trip had been. Zhi touched the cart. “If each trip goes like this, one household can finish moving.”

From that side, Yu could no longer see the northern village. He imagined his family living nearby. Their own trips would become shorter. His neighbors would still use the winding road. Lian asked, “Which problem did you mean to solve?”

They returned north and tried Yu's choice for one day. At dusk, their cut was only deep enough to hold the toe of one shoe. Zhi measured it with his staff. “At this rate, I still can't see when you'll finish,” he said. Yu put away his tool. “Tomorrow, we'll start from this cut.”

The cut slowly widened until someone could stand there with both feet. A child named Jun brought a small basket for loose pieces. Everyone could see the small new foothold. Several neighbors arrived with their own tools.

Winter snow covered the mountain path. Yu replaced a broken handle while Lian fixed baskets. In spring, neighbors returned to a cut wider than before. They knew where to begin without new directions.

Zhi found Yu resting while Jun and the neighbors carried stone behind him. “You are losing strength,” Zhi said. “Who will face this obstacle when you cannot?” Yu pointed to the busy path. “Ask the people already here.” That night, Zhi finished loading that same cart.

At this point, the old legend leaves ordinary village life. A guardian spirit listened above the mountain as the sound of tools returned each season. The spirit went to the Ruler of Heaven. “They rest,” he reported, “but the work does not end.”

The legend says the Ruler of Heaven sent the two sons of Kua'e, beings famous for their great strength. Each son lifted one mountain onto his back. Before morning, they carried both peaks far from the village road.

At dawn, Zhi rolled that loaded cart onto the level road where a mountain had stood. He reached the southern fields quickly, then turned home. When Yu saw him again, Zhi was bringing the same load back instead of leaving.

The two old men walked among travelers heading both ways. Zhi looked at his cart's straight tracks. “On this road, even this loaded trip was easy,” he said. Yu moved aside as Jun hurried past with an empty basket.
