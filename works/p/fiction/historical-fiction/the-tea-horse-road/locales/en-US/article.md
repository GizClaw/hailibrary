# The Tea Horse Road

In the late Qing dynasty, fourteen-year-old Yunsheng joined a caravan from Pu'er carrying compressed tea north from Lijiang. At Pine Wind Post, a trader heading north would buy the load and carry it toward Tibetan regions. Eight mules wore wooden pack frames, with wrapped tea bundles balanced on both sides. Yunsheng had walked shorter roads before, but never this far into the mountains.

At the yard gate, Uncle Ma checked each rope and looked at the low sun. “We promised Pine Wind Post that we would arrive tomorrow evening,” he said. “The tea buyer leaves the following morning. If we miss him, payment must wait.” Several workers had families counting on that money.

They left before dawn. Yunsheng wanted Uncle Ma to see that he could manage a full day's pace. When the trail climbed into cloud, he kept the last two mules close and called out before each bend. “Good,” Uncle Ma said. “Do the same when you are tired.”

By midday, rain darkened the stones. The lead mule's copper bells kept a steady beat. Then Willow snorted near Yunsheng, and a tea bundle knocked against its wooden frame. He turned. The bundle leaned outward on the patient gray mule near the end of the line.

Lan lifted the bundle while Yunsheng pulled its rope tight. The load looked level again. “We should check the whole frame,” Yunsheng said. Uncle Ma studied the clouds above the pass. “At the flat spot beside the trail. A long stop now may cost us the buyer.”

The trail narrowed at the pass entrance. Cold rain ran over a sloping stone, and loose pebbles rolled away under Willow's rear hoof. The mule spread its legs and would not step forward. Uncle Ma reached for the lead rope, but Yunsheng asked, “Can we unload here first?”

“That flat spot just ahead, off the trail, is safer for stopping,” Uncle Ma replied. Willow tried one step, and its rear hoof slid again. Yunsheng pointed beneath the frame, where wet hair lay flat around a rubbed patch of skin. “The frame is moving,” he said. “If Willow falls, nobody reaches the post.”

Uncle Ma finally raised his hand, and the entire line stopped. The crew led the mules onto the nearby flat spot and removed Willow's cargo. Once the tea bundles were down, Lan found a cracked wooden bar hidden under the rope. The break had let the frame twist with every step.

Lan knew a lower path through the forest, but it would add most of a day. “Most of a day?” Uncle Ma asked. “The crew is waiting for its pay.” “I know,” Lan said. “But can we promise she'll cross that wet ledge?” She tightened Willow's lead rope. Uncle Ma looked at the pass and said nothing.

Yunsheng expected Uncle Ma to choose the pass. Instead, the leader turned the first mule downhill. “We take the forest path,” he said. “Keep the tea dry, and keep the loads even.” Lan led Willow near the middle. Yunsheng waited for the other mules to turn, then followed at the rear with the empty broken frame across his shoulders.

Beneath the trees, they shared Willow's tea among several lightly loaded mules. Each received one small bundle on each side, so no load leaned. Rain tapped the wrapped tea, but water did not reach the leaves. Yunsheng checked the ropes again before the line moved.

The forest path stayed broad, yet roots and mud kept the pace slow. Uncle Ma no longer called for speed. From the rear, Yunsheng listened past the lead mule's bells for a slipping hoof or a knocking bundle. For the rest of that wet afternoon, the loads stayed quiet.

The slower forest path had cost them the day, so they stopped for the night at a timber shelter. The evening they had promised was already past. Lan let Willow rest without a load and checked its back and legs. The rubbed skin had not opened. Uncle Ma placed a short piece of wood beside the cracked bar. Yunsheng wrapped rope around both pieces until the frame held straight.

Clear light reached the lower trail the next morning. Across the valley, fresh stones covered part of the upper path. Yunsheng could not tell when they had fallen. Uncle Ma watched for a moment, then sent the line onward.

Pine Wind Post came into view near sunset, a day late. The buyer was nowhere in the yard. Keeper Luo shook his head. “The buyer left this morning. He will return in three days.” Uncle Ma's mouth tightened, but he did not blame Yunsheng.

Keeper Luo inspected the dry bundles and locked them in his storehouse. “The buyer left his terms,” he said. “He'll pay one-tenth less for a late load. Lodging is two copper coins per person each night.” “Can you lower the lodging rate?” Uncle Ma asked. Keeper Luo shook his head. After a pause, Uncle Ma counted out the first night's coins.

Three days later, the buyer returned and opened a bundle. The tea was dry, and every mule could still travel. He paid the lower price Keeper Luo had named, but he bought the full load. The crew bought goods to carry home. Lan removed the temporary wood, replaced the cracked bar, and tightened Willow's frame.

At the next departure, Uncle Ma handed Yunsheng Willow's lead rope. “You heard Willow before anyone else,” he said. “If you hear trouble today, stop the whole line. Don't wait for my order.” Yunsheng closed his hand around the rope and walked ahead of Willow, keeping her at the rear of the line. The full team moved south toward Lijiang, every return bundle level, while the two copper bells rang beyond the bend.
