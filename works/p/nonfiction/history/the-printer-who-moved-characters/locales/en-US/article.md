# The Printer Who Moved Characters

The museum would open in two days, and one frame in the printing room was still empty. Mei had drawn an imagined portrait of Bi Sheng for it. Dr. Chen studied the bold face, then asked, “How do we know he looked like that?” Mei had no answer. She folded the drawing and looked at the empty frame.

They began with the older method beside the empty frame. A carver cut a whole page in reverse on one woodblock. After ink met the raised surface, paper could take the page again and again. The block kept that page ready, but a different page needed a different carving.

Dr. Chen opened a copy of Shen Kuo's Dream Pool Essays. Its short passage about Bi Sheng is the main historical source for his invention. It tells us much about a method and little about a life. “Can one passage carry an exhibit?” Mei asked. “Only if we show where its evidence ends,” Dr. Chen replied.

The passage calls Bi Sheng a commoner and places his work in the Qingli years, from 1041 to 1048. It does not name a book he printed or describe his face, voice, home, or workshop. Mei put her portrait away. The museum clay figure had a deliberately ordinary face. The figure stood only for actions recorded in the passage, not for Bi Sheng's appearance. A label would identify the scene as a reconstruction.

Next, they followed the recorded actions. Bi Sheng formed small pieces from sticky clay and carved one raised character on each. He hardened the pieces in fire. A page no longer had to stay inside one block. Its characters had become separate parts that a printer could select and move.

The reconstruction showed an unfired piece slumping beside the hardened type. Later scholars still debated whether ordinary clay worked well. Some thought fired type would break too easily. A later scholar pointed to a nineteenth-century book successfully printed with clay type. Shen Kuo himself preferred fired clay to wood in this system and said Bi Sheng's types were still carefully preserved. The display showed both pieces and left the question open.

Making pieces was only the start. The record says the printer covered an iron board with a mix of pine resin, wax, and paper ash. An iron frame sat on top. Heat would soften the bed under the type, so the pieces could be fixed together for one page.

The printer packed the clay pieces close inside the frame. Before printing, he pressed a flat board across them until their tops made one even surface. Mei tried the replica. One high piece rocked the board, and one low piece did not touch it. She reset both pieces until the board rested evenly.

Bi Sheng's movable type did more than break a page into pieces. Shen Kuo describes two iron boards working in turn. While one board printed, the next page could be arranged on the other. In the reconstruction, the artisan inked one board and set a clay piece into the second. Once the first page was printed, the boards were ready to switch jobs.

Shen Kuo was clear about the size of the job. For only two or three copies, he said, the method was not convenient. For tens, hundreds, or thousands, he called it fast. The setup took time, and many copies could repay that effort. Mei wrote one question beside the copy counter: “How many copies?”

A page might repeat the same character, so one clay piece for each character was not enough. Common characters had many copies; Shen Kuo says some had more than twenty. Unused pieces went into sections of a wooden case, arranged by rhyme. Mei sorted the replicas, then found the next common character without digging through the whole tray.

What if the next page needed an uncommon character that was missing? The passage says a printer could carve it then and harden it over a grass fire. The reconstruction showed that step. The hands-on table had no working flame; its brazier was painted clay. Mei held up a blank piece. “Good,” she said. “This fire can't burn my fingers or wake the alarm.”

After printing, heat softened the binding mix again. Shen Kuo wrote that one brush of the hand made the clay types fall free and stay perfectly clean. The printer returned them to their case, ready to join different pages. One by one, the clean pieces filled the empty compartments.

Mei set the woodblock beside the loose type. “So movable pieces always win,” she said. Dr. Chen pointed to the time needed for sorting and setting. Mei saw the trade-off: avoiding a new full-page carving meant spending time sorting and setting loose pieces. A fixed block could print the same page again without more carving. Separate pieces helped when pages changed or many copies justified the setup. Mei took back her claim and began timing both demonstrations.

Shen Kuo also explains why Bi Sheng avoided wood in this arrangement. He says wood grain varied, moisture made the surface uneven, and the pieces stuck to the binding bed. His criticism applies to Bi Sheng's setting method. Later printers could prepare wood and hold it in other ways. Dr. Chen lifted one damp wooden replica, and it clung to the model bed.

Later printers did use wooden movable type. Around 1298, Wang Zhen used wooden type to print a county gazetteer. His Book of Agriculture, published in 1313, later described the method. A living tradition in China still arranges wooden characters, prints a page, takes it apart, and reuses the pieces. Mei asked Dr. Chen to place this display after the clay reconstruction in the visitors' path.

Metal offered another path. Korean printers cast metal type by the thirteenth century. Jikji, printed in 1377, is the oldest surviving book made with movable metal type. Its pages show what cast type could produce, although casting and setting still demanded equipment and skill. Dr. Chen placed its case beside the later wood display, leaving the clay reconstruction across the gallery.

On opening morning, the portrait frame remained empty. Below it, a full-page woodblock lay face-down beside the movable clay replicas. Three blank color-coded trays kept Shen Kuo's record, later evidence, and unknowns separate. Mei watched a visiting child move one replica and ask how the next page would change. The child reached for one more piece.
