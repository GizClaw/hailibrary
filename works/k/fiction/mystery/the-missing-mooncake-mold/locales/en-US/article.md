# The Missing Mooncake Mold

Mid-Autumn was only a few days away. Red lanterns moved above Lantern Street while Grandpa Lu set his flour bag on the workbench.

Grandpa Lu planned to make mooncakes in the shared courtyard. “Please bring my old wooden mooncake mold. It has a flower carved in it,” he told Mei. The mold usually hung next to his green toolbox, but the peg was empty.

Mei checked under the bench and in every section of the toolbox. “Someone took it!” she said. “I’ll ask every shop on the street.” Amara arrived and set down her backpack.

In the months since they met, Amara had learned enough everyday Chinese to chat with Mei. She studied the peg. “It’s still straight, and the wood has no fresh scratches.” Mei saw that someone had lifted the mold with care. That was their first clue.

Mei heard her dog Bean snuffling. He sniffed a curved flour mark on the bench. White spots crossed the stones toward Auntie Chen’s kitchen. Grandpa Lu’s flour bag sat apart, with no spill nearby.

The spots seemed to stop under the camphor tree. Water from Bean’s bowl had washed them off the stones. Wind tugged the lanterns. Bean nudged his bowl and revealed a damp note that the wind had blown beneath it.

Mei read: “Borrowed the flower mold. Back by sunset. Thanks, Grandpa Lu.” “Borrowed” changed her plan, but she still could not read the blurred name. “The flour leads there, so maybe she borrowed it,” Amara said, pointing toward the kitchen. They hurried back to Grandpa Lu.

Grandpa Lu cleaned his glasses and read the note. Then he smiled. “Auntie Chen asked me yesterday, and I said yes. Looks like my memory needs fixing.”

Beyond the wet stones, Amara found the flour spots again. They led into Auntie Chen’s kitchen. The missing mold rested beside round pieces of dough.

Auntie Chen pressed soft dough into the carved mold. She turned it over and tapped it on a folded cloth. A round cake dropped out with a raised flower. The mold gave the dough its round shape and flower pattern.

Mei showed Auntie Chen the damp note and said they found it by Bean’s bowl. Auntie Chen nodded, then showed Amara the first tray. “The wind carried it there. My floury hands left the spots. I made these mooncakes for your family’s first Mid-Autumn here.”

Mei held the damp note near Bean’s nose and laughed. “Next time your bowl hides a clue, give us one bark.” Amara grinned. Bean wagged his tail under the table.

Auntie Chen helped Grandpa Lu complete his batch. Afterward, the girls wrapped the clean mold in cloth. Mei hung it on the peg beside his toolbox.

At sunset, Auntie Chen brought warm mooncakes to the courtyard. Their raised flowers matched the mold’s carving. Grandpa Lu picked one up. “Good. The mold is home, and the mooncakes are ready before the festival moon is full.”
