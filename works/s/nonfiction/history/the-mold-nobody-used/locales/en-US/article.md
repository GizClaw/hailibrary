# Before the Mold Became Medicine

The familiar version of penicillin's history can fit into one bright scene. A lone scientist notices mold on a forgotten dish and suddenly discovers a cure. The real history begins with that observation, but it does not end there. A clear patch on a dish was evidence of an effect. It was not yet a safe drug, a tested treatment, or a way to make enough medicine for a hospital.

In September 1928, Alexander Fleming was sorting culture dishes at St Mary's Hospital in London. One dish held colonies of Staphylococcus bacteria and an unwanted mold. Near the mold, the bacterial colonies had become clear or failed to grow normally. Fleming kept the unusual culture instead of throwing it away. He set the dish back on his bench and began asking what had cleared the space around the mold.

The plate showed where mold and bacteria had grown together, but it did not explain why. Perhaps the mold released a substance that moved through the jellylike agar and harmed the bacteria. Perhaps some other condition had cleared the area. Fleming tested the first interpretation by growing the mold in liquid and placing filtered liquid near fresh bacterial cultures. Clear zones formed again.

Fleming named the active material penicillin, after the Penicillium mold. He compared its effects on several kinds of bacteria. Some stopped growing almost completely, while others were hardly affected. This pattern mattered because it could be repeated and measured. It also set a boundary. Penicillin was not a substance that killed every microbe, and one unusual plate could not establish its medical value.

In 1929, Fleming published his results. He described the clear zones, the filtered mold liquid, its effects on different bacteria, and possible uses. He thought it might help separate certain bacteria in the laboratory or treat some surface infections. The paper also reported toxicity checks. Fleming injected penicillin into a rabbit's vein and a mouse's abdomen without observing a toxic effect. He did not infect the animals first, so the experiment did not test protection against infection. Nor did he treat a deep infection.

Fleming himself later said that a spore came through an open window, and that story became popular. Historians have found reasons to doubt it. C. J. La Touche kept mold cultures in a mycology laboratory one floor below Fleming's. A spore may have traveled upstairs from that lab, which is the more likely route, but it has never been proved. The plate recorded no route. The downstairs explanation fits the evidence better, yet it remains an assumption.

Nor did everyone simply forget the mold. Fleming and assistants Stuart Craddock and Frederick Ridley tried to concentrate the active material. Fleming continued using penicillin as a tool for studying bacteria and shared the mold with other researchers. The larger problem was practical. The crude liquid was weak, difficult to purify, and easily lost its activity. A useful laboratory effect had not become a dependable medicine.

During the 1930s, other researchers also examined penicillin or received cultures. None built a complete path from mold broth to a tested drug. Calling this period “nothing” hides their work. Calling it “almost a cure” goes too far in the other direction. The surviving record supports a narrower interpretation: the effect was known, but the methods and evidence needed for treatment were still missing.

At Oxford University, Ernst Chain later found Fleming's paper while reviewing antibacterial substances. Howard Florey led a team that asked a different question. Could the active material be separated, measured, and tested inside a living body, rather than only on bacteria in a dish? That larger program began in earnest in 1939 and required several kinds of skill.

Norman Heatley helped design methods to grow the mold and recover penicillin from huge amounts of liquid. Chain and Heatley used changes in acidity and solvents to move the active material away from unwanted matter. Edward Abraham helped remove further impurities. To purify penicillin meant separating it from the many other substances in the broth while keeping its fragile activity. Each step could destroy part of the small yield.

The Oxford team also needed a fair way to compare batches. Heatley's plate test measured the clear ring around a sample placed among bacteria. A wider ring meant stronger activity under the test conditions. This assay could not tell the team everything about a future medicine. It replaced guesses such as “this batch looks good” with a shared unit. Records could now connect a process change with a measured result.

In May 1940, the team tested penicillin in mice infected with dangerous bacteria. Four infected mice received penicillin, and four controls did not. By the next morning, the untreated controls were dead while the treated mice were alive. The comparison did not prove that penicillin would cure people. It did show something the original dish could not: the material could act inside a living animal under those conditions.

The Oxford scientists published the mouse result in 1940. The result was striking, but it answered only one question. Treating a person would require much more penicillin than a dish test or small-animal experiment. The team needed cleaner material, careful observation, and far more of it. The interpretation shifted from a laboratory effect with limited, unproved treatment possibilities. The mouse evidence now supported testing penicillin as a systemic drug.

In February 1941, doctors at Oxford treated Albert Alexander, a police officer with a severe bacterial infection. Accounts disagree about how his first injury happened, so that detail cannot be stated with confidence. After penicillin treatment began, his condition improved. Then the supply ran out. The infection returned, and Alexander died. The case showed both a powerful response and the terrible cost of having too little medicine.

The team treated more patients and gathered stronger clinical evidence, but every dose demanded extraordinary labor. Workers grew mold across hundreds of shallow vessels. They collected the liquid, extracted the active material, and even recovered penicillin from patients' urine. Heatley designed stackable ceramic containers and a continuous extraction device. The makeshift Oxford factory showed that treatment was promising; it also showed that hospital-scale hand production was not enough.

Britain's wartime factories had little spare capacity for a difficult new process. In 1941, Florey and Heatley traveled to the United States seeking help. They carried methods, samples, and test standards rather than a finished recipe. At a federal laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, agricultural scientists joined the effort. Drug companies, government offices, chemists, engineers, mold specialists, and production workers joined them.

The Peoria researchers tested nutrients and many mold strains. Corn steep liquor, a by-product of corn processing, helped the mold produce more penicillin. A strain found on a moldy cantaloupe gave a higher yield than Fleming's original strain under production conditions. This detail corrects another simple tale: factories did not merely enlarge Fleming's plate. They changed the strain, nutrients, equipment, and controls.

Large tanks introduced new failures. The mold needed air, yet bubbling air made foam. Excessive agitation could damage the mold and reduce its penicillin yield, while heat could damage the penicillin itself. Engineers developed deep-tank fermentation, sterile air systems, cooling, mixing, foam control, extraction, and freeze-drying. No single clever device solved production. Many linked changes had to work reliably, batch after batch.

By 1944, United States production had risen enough to supply penicillin for severe Allied casualties during the Normandy invasion. Wartime need accelerated the program, while scarce supplies still had to be assigned carefully. The achievement belonged to a network across nations and institutions. It included scientists whose names became famous, workers who tended cultures, clinicians and patients, government coordinators, and industrial teams that made uncertain processes repeatable.

In 1945, the Nobel Prize recognized Fleming, Chain, and Florey. Heatley and many other contributors were not named in that prize. Surviving equipment and records make their work visible. In a museum display, the clear-ring dish can stand beside Heatley's extraction apparatus, a culture vessel, and a penicillin vial. Together, those objects show the distance between the mold on Fleming's bench and the medicine in the vial.
