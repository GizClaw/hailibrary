# The Stones That Sailed

Across Racetrack Playa, stones rest at the ends of long tracks pressed into pale mud. One groove runs straight; another sweeps through a broad turn. For decades, researchers arrived after the stones had stopped. The tracks established two facts: the rocks had changed position, and the mud had been soft enough to record the trip. The marks held no timing or weather data. Investigators had to reconstruct the force on each rock from what remained.

The Racetrack lies in a remote part of Death Valley National Park. It is a playa, or dry lakebed, ringed by mountains. Rock fragments fall from the slopes and reach its nearly level floor. Most days, the hard surface looks dry and still. Occasional rain or snow can spread a shallow pond across the mud. Later, the water disappears. A track may last long after the brief wet period that allowed it to form, so a visitor sees the result rather than the motion.

Reports of the strange tracks drew scientific attention by the 1940s. Researchers proposed more than one hypothesis, meaning an explanation that evidence could test. Wind seemed necessary because many trails followed directions expected from storm winds. Water also mattered because wet clay offered less resistance than dry ground. Yet those ideas left a difficult question. Could air push a heavy stone directly, or did water and ice change the forces acting on it? The same trail could support several stories unless another observation separated them.

One group aimed the wash of an aircraft propeller across wet playa mud. Natural stones moved only when the wind speed exceeded twenty meters per second. Pulling tests and calculations demanded even greater force for some rocks. The experiment established a useful threshold for direct wind on slippery clay. Investigators could now compare that extreme with other mechanisms and with any weather record collected during a future movement.

Other scientists pursued ice. Stanley and later observers noted nearly identical trails left by far-apart rocks. A wind-driven sheet could link their motion. In the thick-ice version, rocks froze into the sheet and floated partly clear of the mud. That would reduce friction, the force that resists sliding between surfaces. The matching tracks became evidence for shared motion and a reason to examine winter ice closely.

A later field test challenged a simple ice-sheet model. Sharp and Carey drove a ring of steel reinforcing rods into the playa around several stones. A solid sheet carrying the whole group should have snagged on that barrier. After winter, one stone had moved beyond the ring while another remained inside. Sharp and Carey treated the split result as evidence against ice. Because nobody witnessed the episode, the ice might have broken, melted, or passed over a low rock.

Earlier trail studies had found another clue: paths from stones as much as 830 meters apart sometimes turned together. Stanley reported the pattern in 1955; Reid's team measured it again in 1995. A 1996 differential-GPS map of 162 stones complicated the picture. Most routes shared broad tendencies, but small departures in direction suggested that individual stones often moved independently. An adequate model had to allow shared turns without forcing every nearby rock to travel as part of one rigid raft.

By then, the main difficulty was clear: tracks and maps preserved the aftermath, while experiments supplied controlled conditions. Natural movement was rare enough for stones to remain still for years. Even when rain, freezing temperatures, sunlight, and suitable wind met during one winter period, a short event could occur far from anyone watching. Researchers needed instruments that could wait on the playa through every empty day.

In winter 2011, a team began a study under National Park Service permits. Park rules protected native Racetrack stones from drilling, so the team brought similar limestone blocks from outside the playa. Fifteen blocks held custom Global Positioning System recorders. Beneath each one, researchers buried a magnetic trigger. When a rock moved away, its logger switched to one-second recording. A weather station tracked wind, temperature, sunlight, and rain. Time-lapse cameras watched the southeastern playa. The instruments could connect a rock's route and timing with water, ice, weather, and photographs from the same event.

The cameras used time-lapse photography, taking images at set intervals so slow changes could be compared later. From November through March, one image recorded playa conditions every hour. The weather station measured wind each second, along with temperature, sunlight, and rain. Team members returned five to eight times a year to replace batteries and collect data. They expected years without movement. Even a dry winter would reveal whether standing water, freezing, sunlight, or suitable wind had been missing.

Late in 2013, a winter storm brought rain and snow to the basin. A shallow pond spread across the southern playa and remained for more than two months. Temperatures dropped below freezing on many nights, so the pond repeatedly froze and thawed. The combination was unusual. Earlier snowy periods had not always produced enough standing water, while another flooded period had brought little freezing. This time, water, cold, and a long-lived pond overlapped while the instruments were ready.

After a cold night, thin ice covered the pond. Late-morning sunlight melted open water around the rocks and split the broad sheet into floating panels. A light southerly wind drove the water and ice northward, toward the pond's shallow, upslope edge. On the way, the panels pressed against stones resting on saturated mud. Ice along the shallow northern edge likely melted first, opening water into which the panels could move.

GPS rocks moved on December 4 and December 20, 2013. On December 4, two rocks that began about 153 meters apart started within six seconds of each other. Both traveled for sixteen minutes at several meters per minute. Another instrumented rock moved for 12.3 minutes on December 20. Across several events that winter, some GPS rocks covered as much as 224 meters. Once motion began, their loggers stored position and speed every second.

On December 20 and 21, researchers watched ice break with soft popping sounds as stones crept across mud beneath shallow water. They saw another movement on January 9, 2014. One photographed stone traveled for about eighteen seconds while broken ice gathered on its upstream side. Stationary rocks nearby made the slow shift visible. The team matched those firsthand observations with photographs, fresh trails, weather records, and GPS positions from the same pond season.

The observed mechanism used much thinner ice than earlier models proposed. The ice was only about three to six millimeters thick, far too thin to float large rocks. Each stone remained on the muddy bottom as wide panels pressed against its side. Wind and flowing water moved the panels, while saturated mud reduced resistance below. Several ice pieces could pile up against one rock and transfer force across a broader surface. The resulting push slid the stone along the lakebed.

The motion was easy to overlook. GPS rocks moved about two to five meters per minute, sometimes for sixteen minutes; one photographed trip lasted only eighteen seconds. Without a fixed reference, an observer from firm ground might miss the change. Muddy water and ice could also cover a new trail until wind shifted the pond aside. Park visitors should stay off wet mud and leave every rock in place, protecting the fragile surface that preserves the tracks.

Thin, breaking ice could produce both similar and different tracks. One large panel might push many rocks in related directions. A crack or steel rod could split the panel, and changing water depth could alter which rocks its edge touched. Ice might pass over a low stone yet catch a taller neighbor. Two rocks could start together and stop at different places. The old corral result now fit a model in which the ice fractured or rode above the stone that remained inside.

Earlier work remained part of the explanation: propeller tests set an approximate direct-wind threshold, and trail studies documented group motion. GPS mapping exposed departures from that group pattern. Ice proposals connected winter conditions with parallel routes. The 2013 and 2014 observations supplied a working combination—light wind moving shallow water and thin floating ice across soft mud, where the panels pushed grounded stones.

The study had important limits. Researchers directly observed this mechanism during one pond season and obtained complete movement records from only some GPS rocks. They did not watch every old trail form. The largest natural stones were not seen moving, and other conditions might matter in other events. The evidence strongly explains many Racetrack movements, especially the patterns observed that winter. It does not justify saying that every stone in every decade moved in precisely the same way.

By the second week of February, the pond had dried. Fresh tracks crossed the pale mud beside older ones. Researchers matched each instrumented route with wind measurements, GPS positions, time-lapse images, and firsthand views of moving ice. The old and new grooves looked much alike. For the new ones, however, the team now had timestamps, speeds, weather logs, and photographs from the hours when they formed.
