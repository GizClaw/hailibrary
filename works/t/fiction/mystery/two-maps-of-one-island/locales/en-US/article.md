# Two Maps of One Island

Mira had promised the island museum a new summer exhibit, and the empty display case was already waiting. She planned to fill it with Elias Ward's old survey map, the one school groups called “the first true shape of Bellweather Island.” Then Gran Tessa opened a flat drawer and found another map beneath it. Nola Reed had drawn this one twenty-eight years later. When Mira set the two sheets together, the island seemed to change shape under her hands.

“Ward put the lighthouse on the edge of the sea,” Mira said. “Reed put it far inland. Ward missed half the west cove, and he invented a ledge in the north.” She reached for a card and wrote a sharp heading: WARD'S ERRORS. Gran Tessa did not take the card away. She only asked, “What makes Reed's map more reliable?”

Mira pointed to the later date. Gran waited. A newer date, Mira realized, was not evidence by itself.

The disagreement was too large to dismiss. On Ward's map, the western coastline curved tightly around the hill. Reed showed a broad, pale flat beyond the same curve. Ward drew a long rock shelf at the northern point; Reed ended the island before it. Worst of all, the lighthouse marks stood almost a finger-width apart. Yet both surveyors placed the ridge spring, the old harbor wall, and the museum's stone steps in nearly the same positions. Ward had also drawn the spring and every tight turn on the uphill road with unusual care.

Mira traced both maps onto thin paper. She stacked the copies and pressed pins through the three fixed landmarks. The holes met. Even several small bends in the uphill road lined up.

“A careless surveyor would have scattered mistakes everywhere,” Mira said. Her voice had lost its certainty. Gran gathered the pins. “Keep the three differences separate until they give us three answers.”

They packed the copied outlines, a camera, a measuring cord, and the museum's tide clock. Beside the maps sat a photograph box marked with a lighthouse shape and two dates. Mira added it to the list for their return.

The west cove would come first because the morning water was low. They would compare the lighthouse sites on the walk north, then examine Ward's vanished shelf from the fenced overlook. Gran locked the originals away. “Our evidence can get wet,” she said. “The archive cannot.”

At the cove, the sea had pulled back from a wide floor of hard sand and dark pools. Reed's line followed the water's present edge. Ward's tighter curve ran along a band of stones much closer to the hill. Mira stayed on the marked public path and stretched the cord between two iron rings above the flat. The distance matched Reed's broad shape closely.

“One point for the newer map,” Mira said. Gran opened her notebook and wrote, “Low water, 8:10.”

They ate lunch on high steps while the water returned. It crossed the sand Mira had measured, filled the dark pools, and finally reached the old band of stones. The visible edge of the island now followed Ward's tighter curve.

Mira spread the two tracings across her knees. “Reed's edge was here this morning,” she said, touching the flooded flat. Then she tapped Ward's curve beside the stones. “Now the flat is flooded, and Ward's curve meets the water.”

Beyond the cove, Mira found a low circle of fitted stone beside the cliff path. It was too even to be natural. Rust-stained bolt holes circled its empty center, and chips of red roof tile lay among the grass roots. Ward's map placed the lighthouse exactly there. Farther inland on the hill, the present lighthouse stood at Reed's mark.

Mira opened the field list. Beside “photograph box,” she drew a question mark.

Back in the archive, Gran opened the photograph box. One image showed the lighthouse on the coastal base. Another showed its lantern room removed while the intact stone tower rested on heavy timber supports. In the last, the same tower stood on a new foundation farther inland on the hill, its lantern room reattached. The dates fell between the two surveys.

Mira slid Ward's map under the first photograph and Reed's under the last. The old base and the hill mark lined up with the two images. She left the pairs together on the table.

The northern overlook left the hardest difference. Ward's long rock shelf was nowhere below them. Reed's shorter point matched the cliff they could see, but Mira would not call that proof that Ward had invented land.

At the fence, a narrow seam ran through the exposed rock. Below it, angular blocks rested in the water. Their color matched the cliff above. “Something broke here,” Mira said. “But we still don't know when, or how much.”

The archive held four photographs taken from the same hill across many years. In the earliest image, a flat shelf reached beyond the seam. A later picture showed a deep crack. After a severe island storm, the next photograph showed open water where the outer shelf had been. The final image matched Reed's outline.

Mira placed Ward's survey date to the left of the row and Reed's to the right. All four photographs fit between them.

Mira added a label for erosion, the gradual wearing away and removal of soil or rock. Wind, waves, and storms might all have contributed on Bellweather Island, but the archive did not measure each cause. The photographs supported a narrower statement: the shelf shrank over time, and the largest visible change followed that storm. Mira copied that sentence beneath the four images.

Gran brought out the surveyors' job sheets before Mira could close her notebook. Ward's instruction read, “Show every route a rescue cart can use when the sea is high.” The sheet told him to emphasize springs, steep ground, and safe turns. Mira wrote perspective at the top of a new column. Under Ward's name, she copied “spring” and “road turns,” then reopened his map.

Reed's instruction read, “Show all ground exposed at low tide. Mark foot and cart access across the flat to the harbor wall, the boat approach on the harbor side, and the present beacon position.” Her map widened the west cove, marked the relocated lighthouse, and showed the exposed crossing in detail.

At low tide, workers could carry tools or push carts across the dry flat while their boats waited in deeper water beyond the harbor approach. Rising water covered that walking route after lunch and made the harbor-side approach deep enough for boats. Mira drew two arrows beside Reed's job sheet, each with a different time.

Mira spread both maps over a light table. The fixed landmarks formed one steady frame. Inside it, the coast shifted with tide and erosion, the lighthouse moved inland, and each job selected different details.

She connected each disagreement to its evidence: morning and afternoon tide notes, dated photographs of the shelf and tower, and the two job sheets. Then she pushed the dark “WARD'S ERRORS” card to the edge of the table and began a list of conditions for the exhibit.

She tested Ward's work again. His spring, wall, steps, high-water edge, and former lighthouse base agreed with surviving evidence. One small inland path ended too early, and no record explained why.

Mira wrote “uncertain” beside the path and placed a blank token beside the unexplained path model.

Reed's map guided workers across the west flat on foot or with handcarts at low tide. Mira laid a blue strip over the crossing. Gran tapped the harbor side. “The water will cover this route after lunch. Once the harbor-side approach is deep enough, boats can reach the wall from this side,” Mira said.

Reed's map showed the present lighthouse but left the old base blank. Mira added a warning strip and an old-site marker.

For the exhibit, Mira cut a clear overlay. The bottom layer showed the fixed ridge, spring, wall, and steps. A blue layer showed the low-water and high-water coastlines.

A gray piece restored the northern shelf's lost outer section with a broken edge. Two small lighthouse shapes occupied the old and new sites. Beside each layer, Mira named its date, condition, purpose, and remaining uncertainty. Visitors could separate the changes or place them together.

Gran read every label aloud while Mira checked it against the evidence. The heading WARD'S ERRORS went into the recycling box. In its place, Mira chose TWO MAPS, TWO MOMENTS.

She kept one sentence about what remained unknown: “Some small line differences may be measurement errors, but the surviving evidence cannot identify which ones.” Gran tapped the sentence. “Keep it,” she said.

On opening day, a young visitor asked which map his family should follow from the museum to the lighthouse. Mira asked when they planned to walk. “After lunch,” he said.

Mira checked the tide clock beside the door. Today's high water would arrive before their walk ended. She slid Ward's high-water route layer over Reed's present lighthouse mark and traced a path above the cove. The visitor carried the combined guide to his family while the two old maps remained side by side in the case.
