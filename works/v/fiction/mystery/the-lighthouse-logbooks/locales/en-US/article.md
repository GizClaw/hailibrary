# The Lighthouse Logbooks

Nia expected dust, not an argument. The Beacon Island museum was reopening its lighthouse room, and she had volunteered to record the audio guide. On the wall, an old brass plaque gave the wreck of the Dorian Star a simple ending: “Assistant Keeper Maia Venn abandoned the fog bell. Head Keeper Orrin Hale kept the lamp burning, but the ship ran aground.” Hundreds of visitors had heard that accusation as fact. Mr. Bell, the curator, handed Nia a cloth and said, “Read everything before you record that sentence.” The warning sounded ordinary until she opened the cabinet beneath the plaque.

Two leather books lay inside. Orrin's official log had a stiff red cover and numbered pages. Maia's smaller duty book was blue and swollen from damp. The museum had displayed the red book before, but the blue one had remained in storage. Both described the same storm, yet their accounts pulled in opposite directions. Orrin blamed a silent bell. Maia blamed a missing light. Each book had been kept at a different post in the tower, and neither writer could have watched the whole station. Nia set her microphone aside. The audio guide would have to wait until she understood why two careful records disagreed.

Nia read Orrin's entry aloud. “The flame remained steady and the lens continued its turn. Assistant Venn struck the bell twice, then deserted her post. The ship altered course and grounded on North Bar.” Below it, Orrin had added that inspectors had approved his lamp room that same week. His sentences were neat, certain, and centered on the equipment under his command. Because he signed the official station report, later writers repeated his version. He reported what he saw from inside the lantern room. He did not say whether the light had remained visible from the water.

Maia's book answered in hurried lines. Nia read, “The white signal vanished for three turns. I pulled the bell rope twice before it went slack. A loose sheet beat against the gallery rail. I ran upstairs, but the ship was already turning.” The blue book had never become an official report. Maia also noted that Orrin had warned her after an earlier late watch. She had reason to defend her job, just as Orrin had reason to defend his inspection. Motive could shape either account, but it did not prove that either keeper had lied.

Nia made two columns, then crossed out the headings TRUTH and LIE. She replaced them with POSITION, MOTIVE, and LIMIT. She left a fourth space for evidence from outside both books. Orrin stood beside the inner lamp, above the gallery. Maia worked at the bell platform below and outside the lantern room. Orrin could inspect the flame and turning lens, but the wall hid the seaward glass from him. Maia could see the outward flashes and feel the bell rope, but she could not see the lamp itself. Their locations gave each writer useful knowledge and a serious blind spot.

The written times seemed to destroy any hope of comparison. Orrin placed the violent hail at eleven forty-seven. Maia wrote eleven forty-one. If both clocks were right, their warning failures might have happened separately. However, both described one unusual sequence: hail hit the north windows, the tower shuddered, and a storage door slammed below. The order was too specific to be two separate events. Nia drew a line between the entries. If the clocks disagreed, the shared sequence could still place the keepers in the same six-minute window.

Earlier pages explained the gap. For four evenings, Orrin had compared the lamp-room clock with the harbor clock. Each entry said it was six minutes fast, followed by the same promise to adjust it after the storm watch. The repeated delay was embarrassing, but it also made the offset traceable. Maia's duty book used the harbor clock. Once Nia subtracted six minutes from Orrin's times, the hail, two bell strokes, missing flashes, and ship's turn fell into one order. The earlier clock checks independently corroborated her timeline; they strengthened it without proving what caused the wreck.

Mr. Bell brought out the rescue station's narrow incident slip. Its writer had watched from the opposite side of the harbor and had no role in either keeper's dispute. The slip recorded two bell strokes, then silence. It also said the lighthouse signal failed to appear during three expected sweeps before the ship changed direction. That independent view matched Maia's observations about the warnings, but not her explanation for them. The witness was too far away to see a rope break or a lamp fail. Nia now trusted the pattern of events more than any single account of its cause.

A cutaway model of the old lighthouse stood in the next case. Nia removed its back panel. At the center was a tiny lamp, surrounded by a turning glass lens. Beyond the lens, the lantern room had a ring of outer windows. A removable paper beam showed the path from center to sea. The model made one limitation—Orrin's blocked view—visible: a steady flame inside did not guarantee an open path through every outer pane. “He answered whether the lamp was burning,” Nia said. “Maia recorded whether the beam swept across the seaward windows and appeared over the harbor.” Mr. Bell nodded, but he did not tell her which answer mattered most.

Nia returned to Orrin's log and read the page before the wreck. A stone chip had cracked one outer pane during the afternoon storm. Until new glass arrived, Orrin had tied a heavy canvas rain guard across the damaged landward section of the gallery. He listed two brass hooks and three rope loops. He expected the guard to remain on the sheltered side. The repair stood outside the lantern room, beyond his usual view. His later claim about the steady lamp could be accurate even if the temporary cover moved.

Maia's phrase “a loose sheet” no longer seemed vague. From the bell platform, she would have looked up at the gallery just as the hail struck. Her book said the material snapped, crossed the white beam, and hit the seaward rail. She never called it Orrin's rain guard, perhaps because darkness and rain hid its color. Orrin never mentioned hearing canvas. Thick glass, wind, and the turning mechanism surrounded him. The two logs described different parts of one structure, not two separate storms.

The museum still had a folded piece of salt-stiff canvas from the old gallery. One corner held three rope loops. Across the opposite edge ran a diagonal tear. In the hardware tray, one brass hook was already bent toward the sea. A faded inventory card linked both objects to the next morning's cleanup. The objects could not replay the storm, yet their shapes fit a limited inference: a gust may have torn the outer edge free while the landward loops held. The loose canvas could then swing across the seaward windows and hide several passes of the beam.

Nia tested the idea on the unlit model. She fastened a paper shape where the rain guard had hung and moved only its torn corner. The shape crossed the seaward windows but never touched the central lamp. It covered the model's outward beam for part of each turn. The test did not reproduce the force of the storm, so Nia did not call it proof. It showed that Orrin's steady flame and Maia's missing signal could occur at the same time.

The bell still troubled her. The preserved rope rested on a padded support beside the model. Its smooth lower end looked sound, but an old splice had been hidden above a wooden guide. A cleanup record from the morning after the wreck said the two ends were recovered separately beneath that guide. A conservator's magnified examination, performed without pulling the rope, documented matching worn fibers at the separated splice. Nia tested a replica made to the recorded dimensions; its splice parted after two pulls. The historical break provided a counterexample. Orrin's claim assumed that two strokes meant Maia chose to stop. A failed rope could create the same two strokes and sudden silence.

Nia arranged copies of the records across the floor. Corrected for the fast clock, the sequence began with hail and a tower shudder. The canvas edge probably tore loose and crossed the seaward panes. Maia saw three missing flashes and pulled the bell rope. Her account, the rescue slip, and the cleanup record indicated that the old splice separated after two strokes. Then the Dorian Star changed course and struck North Bar. No single record established this sequence; every link required corroboration from another clue.

She tried to defeat her own explanation. Perhaps the lamp went out briefly, as Maia believed. Orrin's close view argued against that, and neither log described relighting, smoke, or a stopped lens. Those absences could not rule out every brief failure. Perhaps Maia simply left the bell, as Orrin believed. The separated splice and her immediate run upstairs made that less likely. The moving rain guard and broken rope explained more evidence with fewer unsupported assumptions, but “most likely” was still not “certain.”

The keepers' motives now looked different from their limitations. Orrin had protected a recent inspection and his authority as head keeper. That pressure may explain why he treated Maia's silence as disobedience. Maia had protected a job already at risk. That pressure may explain why she named the missing signal but did not admit how little she knew about the lamp. Neither motive erased the accurate parts of a log. Those differing pressures warned Nia where each writer's inference moved beyond direct observation.

Nia wrote a qualified conclusion for the new exhibit. The evidence supported two warning failures during the critical minutes. The outward light was probably blocked by the torn rain guard. The recovered rope and three records indicated that its old splice separated after two strokes. Together, those failures likely contributed to the grounding. The records could not show what the ship's crew saw from the deck, why the ship changed course, or how much the storm itself mattered. A shipboard log, if one survived, could test those remaining questions.

Mr. Bell read Nia's draft beside the old brass plaque. He paused at “Maia Venn abandoned the fog bell,” then unscrewed the plaque from the wall. “The town repeated Orrin's conclusion because his book was official,” he said. “Your version makes room for both records without pretending they are equally complete.” Nia shortened one sentence that sounded too certain. The replacement label named Orrin's observation, Maia's observation, the physical clues, and the limits of the museum's conclusion.

At the reopening, a visitor listened to Nia's audio guide and studied the two logbooks in separate cases. He asked, “So which keeper was right?” Nia turned the lighthouse model until the paper rain guard crossed the seaward windows. “Orrin was probably right that the flame stayed lit,” she said. “Maia was probably right that the warning disappeared. Neither could see enough to explain the wreck alone.” The visitor pulled the model's bell rope. The bell rang twice; then the hidden splice separated and the rope went slack, while both books remained open beside it.
