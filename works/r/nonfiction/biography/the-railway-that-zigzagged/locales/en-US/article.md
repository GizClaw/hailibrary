# The Railway That Zigzagged

In 1905, China approved a railway from Beijing to Zhangjiakou and placed Zhan Tianyou in charge of its engineering. The line would become the first major Chinese railway designed, built, and financed without foreign control. That public goal made every choice visible. If the project failed, critics would blame more than one engineer. They would question whether Chinese teams could direct work of this scale.

Zhan brought more than confidence to the task. He had entered Yale's Sheffield Scientific School in 1878 and graduated in civil engineering in 1881. After returning to China, he gained years of railway experience. He had learned to compare measurements, materials, and operating needs before choosing a design. Yet no classroom could flatten the mountains north of Beijing. Training gave him methods for measuring a problem; it did not give him an easy route.

The planned railway covered about two hundred kilometers. Much of it crossed manageable country, but the Guanggou section climbed through the Jundu Mountains toward Badaling. Steam locomotives had limited pulling power on steep track. At the same time, the project had a restricted budget and schedule. A safer, gentler line might require more distance, more digging, or more money.

Survey teams walked the ground and compared possible paths. A direct line looked short on a map, yet it could meet slopes too steep for a train. A longer route could rise more gradually, but added track also cost money. A very long tunnel could avoid some turns, but rock had to be removed before any train could pass. Zhan's task was not to find a perfect choice. It was to fit several imperfect choices together.

Four tunnels were needed on the mountain section. Badaling was the longest, extending more than one kilometer through the ridge. Digging only from the two ends would limit the number of crews that could reach the rock face. More crews could not simply crowd into the same narrow opening. To save time, the engineers needed additional places where work could begin.

They sank two vertical shafts from the ground above the planned Badaling Tunnel. Each shaft reached the tunnel level below. Crews could then dig in both directions from each shaft, while other crews worked inward from the two entrances. Instead of two working faces, the tunnel had six. The method traded extra digging from above for more work happening at once.

Six faces created another demand: they had to meet along the same planned line. Surveying and repeated checks therefore mattered as much as speed. A small error underground could send two headings past each other. Zhan's engineering responsibility included organizing measurements, tools, crews, and timing so that separate efforts became one tunnel.

Workers drilled and blasted the rock, then carried broken stone away. The shafts moved people, tools, and rock between the surface and the tunnel level. This labor was difficult and dangerous, even when carefully directed. Calling the tunnel Zhan's achievement must not erase the many workers and engineers whose hands opened each face.

After about eighteen months of work, the Badaling headings joined. A construction album published in 1909 preserves photographs taken in 1908 of the tunnel entrances, bridges, locomotives, and railway employees. Those images do not show every decision or every worker. They do provide direct evidence that the tunnel and the larger mountain line had taken physical form before the railway opened.

Beyond the tunnel problem waited the steep approach near Qinglongqiao. Parts of the Guanggou line rose about thirty-three meters for every thousand meters forward. Railway engineers call this rise the grade. For the steam locomotives available then, the climb required special care and additional pulling power. Making the track steeper still was not a safe answer.

Zhan considered how distance and excavation could be exchanged. His plan bent toward a lower ridge near Qinglongqiao and added several kilometers of track. The detour reduced the amount of mountain that needed to be cut or tunneled. It did not remove the climb, but it gave the railway more room to gain height while staying within the chosen grade.

At Qinglongqiao, the track entered a switchback and then reversed direction. A train used two locomotives, one at each end. On the first arm, one engine pulled while the other pushed. After the train stopped and the track was set, their jobs changed. The former rear engine led the train up the next arm toward Badaling.

This arrangement solved a local problem, but it carried a cost. Trains had to stop, reverse, and use two locomotives on the hard climb. The sharp curves and steep grade later limited how much traffic the old route could carry. A longer tunnel might have produced a straighter line, yet it would have demanded more money and time during construction. The switchback balanced the needs of 1905, not every future railway.

The switchback was not a new invention created by Zhan. Railways in other countries had already used zigzag tracks to climb steep land. His contribution was the specific Qinglongqiao design, joined to the tunnel plan and the available engines. He selected a known method because it fit this mountain and the project's limits.

Construction advanced in sections. The route from Fengtai to Nankou was completed in 1906, the mountain section to Kangzhuang in 1908, and the final section in 1909. The whole railway was finished in less than four years, ahead of schedule and under budget. The construction album records the three completion years alongside photographs of the finished line.

The 1909 railway album shows stations, factories, bridges, tunnels, trains, and laborers across the line. It also contains a portrait of Zhan. The record supports calling him the chief engineer, but it does not turn a team project into one man's handiwork. His leadership depended on surveyors, construction crews, other engineers, railway workers, and Chinese financing.

One later story shows why evidence matters. Zhan promoted the use of the automatic coupler in China, but he did not invent it. The Janney knuckle coupler was patented in the United States before the Beijing–Zhangjiakou project began. Zhan even distinguished its foreign name from his own in an engineering glossary. Adoption was important work; invention was a different claim.

The strongest account of Zhan's achievement needs no borrowed invention. Under pressure of terrain, cost, time, and public trust, he connected known methods with exact local decisions. Two vertical shafts let crews excavate on six working faces at once in the Badaling Tunnel. The Qinglongqiao switchback traded a longer route and slower operations for less excavation and a manageable grade. In a 1908 photograph, one passenger train and one freight train occupy separate sections of the completed mountain line. The rails curve across the rock and approach the tunnel.
